What are we really hoping to learn when we gather after an incident to discuss what went wrong? The official answer is usually expressed in terms of causes and remediation — understanding the sequence of events, identifying contributing factors, agreeing on actions that will prevent a recurrence — and this answer is not wrong, though it is arguably incomplete. A postmortem is also a social occasion, perhaps one of the more emotionally charged occasions in the life of a technical team, since it involves revisiting a period of stress and confusion in front of colleagues, some of whom may have made decisions that contributed to the trouble. It may be that the quality of what is learned depends less on the rigour of the analysis than on the atmosphere in which it takes place; people who feel safe will describe what actually happened, while people who feel judged will describe what they wish had happened. I suspect most experienced teams understand this, even if their templates do not reflect it.

The movement towards so-called blameless postmortems represents a considerable advance in this respect, and I would not wish to diminish it. The recognition that incidents arise from systems rather than from individual failings, that people generally act reasonably given the information available to them, and that punishing mistakes merely teaches people to conceal them — these are hard-won insights, and teams that have absorbed them are markedly better at learning from failure. Yet the word "blameless" can itself become a kind of ritual, invoked at the start of the meeting and then quietly forgotten as the discussion proceeds. Is it possible to declare an absence of blame while the room is still full of it? You may have attended postmortems in which the formal language was impeccably neutral while the glances across the table told a rather different story.

There is also a tendency, perhaps inevitable, for postmortems to produce a narrative that is more coherent than the events it describes. Incidents, as experienced in the moment, are confusing and nonlinear; people pursue hypotheses that turn out to be wrong, overlook signals that seem obvious in hindsight, and make decisions under conditions of genuine uncertainty. The postmortem document, by contrast, tends to present a tidy sequence in which each thing led to another and the correct response was, in retrospect, fairly clear. This tidiness has its uses, since it makes the lessons easier to communicate to people who were not present. It also, I suspect, conceals precisely the aspects of the incident from which the most could be learned — the confusion itself, the reasons why the correct response was not obvious at the time, the gap between what the dashboards showed and what the responders understood.

What might a more honest postmortem look like? It seems to me that it would give as much attention to the experience of responding as to the technical chain of causation; it would ask not only what failed but what made the failure difficult to recognise and difficult to address; it would treat the responders' confusion as evidence about the system rather than as evidence about the responders. It might also be a little more modest about its action items, which have a way of accumulating in trackers long after the incident has been forgotten, each representing a good intention that was never quite resourced. Perhaps a postmortem that produced fewer actions, taken more seriously, would contribute more to the resilience of a system than one that produced a long list whose main function is to demonstrate diligence.

The postmortem, in the end, is one of the few occasions on which organisations formally set aside time to reflect on their own fallibility. That is a remarkable thing, when you consider how much of working life is organised around the projection of competence and control. The practice asks us to look directly at the moments when our systems and our judgement failed, and to do so in the company of others, without the protective distance that usually allows us to avoid such reflection. Whether it succeeds depends less on the format than on the willingness of those involved to be genuinely curious about what happened. Perhaps that is the deepest lesson the postmortem has to offer: that learning from failure is not primarily a technical skill but a moral one, requiring a kind of courage that no template can supply.
