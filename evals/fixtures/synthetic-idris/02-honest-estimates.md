Why is it that we continue to ask for estimates when so few of us believe in them? The question is not entirely rhetorical, since the practice of estimation survives in almost every software organisation despite a long and well-documented history of disappointment, and the persistence of a practice that consistently fails usually suggests that it is doing something other than what it claims to do. Arguably the estimate is not a prediction at all; it is a negotiation conducted in the vocabulary of prediction, a way of reconciling what the business wants with what the engineers fear, in which both parties agree to pretend that the resulting figure describes the future. I suspect most experienced engineers understand this intuitively, even if they would not articulate it in quite those terms. The difficulty arises when the pretence is forgotten — when the negotiated figure hardens into a commitment, and the commitment becomes a measure by which people are judged.

It may be helpful to consider what we are actually doing when we produce an estimate for a piece of software work. We are imagining a sequence of activities that has not yet happened, in a system we only partially understand, subject to interruptions we cannot foresee, and we are compressing all of that uncertainty into a single figure that must fit neatly into a planning document. The act is, in some sense, heroic; it asks the imagination to perform a task for which it is poorly equipped, and it rewards confidence over accuracy. Is it any wonder that the results are unreliable? What is more surprising, perhaps, is how rarely the unreliability is acknowledged in the planning process itself, as though each new estimate were an entirely fresh attempt rather than the latest entry in a long series of similar misjudgements.

There is a considerable literature on techniques that promise to improve the situation — relative sizing, probabilistic forecasting, the breaking down of work into ever smaller pieces — and I do not wish to dismiss any of them, since each captures something true about the problem. Yet I am struck by how often these techniques are adopted as a kind of procedural reassurance rather than as a genuine change in how uncertainty is discussed. A team may replace its hours with points, or its points with ranges, while the conversation surrounding the estimate remains exactly as it was: somebody asks when the work will be finished, somebody else provides an answer they suspect is optimistic, and everyone proceeds as though the answer were reliable. The technique changes; the institutional relationship to uncertainty does not. It may be that no method of estimation can compensate for an organisation that is unwilling to hear the word "unknown".

What would it look like to take uncertainty seriously? You might begin by treating every estimate as a statement about the present state of our knowledge rather than about the future, and by expecting it to change as that knowledge changes — which is to say, by treating revision as a sign of learning rather than as a failure of discipline. You might also distinguish, more carefully than we usually do, between the parts of a project that resemble work we have done before and the parts that are genuinely novel, since the former can often be estimated with reasonable confidence while the latter resist prediction almost entirely. None of this is especially original; it is, arguably, what thoughtful engineers have been saying for a very long time. The obstacle has never been a shortage of ideas but a reluctance, on all sides, to relinquish the comfort that a precise-sounding figure provides.

Perhaps the deepest difficulty is that estimates are asked to carry an emotional burden they were never designed to bear. They reassure the people who fund the work that it is under control; they reassure the people doing the work that they are trusted; they give everyone a shared fiction around which to organise their anxieties. To abandon them entirely would be to remove a source of comfort without offering anything in its place, and I am not sure that most organisations are ready for that. What we can perhaps do is hold our estimates a little more lightly, speak about them a little more honestly, and remember that the future has never been especially interested in our plans for it. In the end the question is not whether we can predict the work, but whether we can build institutions that remain humane when the prediction fails.
