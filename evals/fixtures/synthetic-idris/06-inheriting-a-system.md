There is a moment, familiar to anyone who has joined a team partway through its history, when you open a codebase for the first time and realise that it was written by people who are no longer there to explain it. The experience is not unlike entering a house whose previous occupants left in a hurry: the furniture is arranged according to a logic you cannot quite reconstruct, certain rooms appear to have been abandoned, and there are fixtures whose purpose remains obscure however long you study them. We tend to call such systems "legacy", a word that carries a faint note of disapproval, as though the inheritance were a burden rather than a gift. It may be that the word itself shapes our attitude more than we realise; it invites us to see the previous authors as obstacles rather than as colleagues separated from us only by time. I suspect that a different vocabulary might produce a rather different kind of engineering.

Why is it so tempting to assume that the people who came before us were careless? Part of the answer, perhaps, is that we see only the results of their decisions and not the circumstances in which those decisions were made — the deadlines that pressed upon them, the constraints they were working around, the knowledge they did not yet have and could not have had. A piece of code that appears absurd in isolation often makes considerable sense once its history is understood; the strange workaround was a response to a defect in a library that has since been fixed, the duplicated logic reflects a period when two teams were not speaking to one another, the unusual structure preserves compatibility with a client that nobody now remembers. None of this is visible in the code itself, which records the decision while silently discarding the reasoning. The consequence is that we judge our predecessors against a standard of knowledge they did not possess, and we find them, predictably, wanting.

It seems to me that the first obligation of anyone inheriting a system is a kind of archaeological patience. Before we rewrite, we should try to understand; before we understand, we should perhaps try simply to observe, noting what the system does and how it behaves under different conditions, without yet forming opinions about whether it ought to behave that way. This is slower than the alternative, and it can feel unproductive, since the organisation usually expects newcomers to demonstrate value quickly and the most visible way to do that is to change things. Yet the changes made without understanding have a way of producing consequences that nobody anticipated — the removed check that turned out to be protecting something important, the simplification that broke an integration whose existence was undocumented. Is it not better to be slow and right than quick and responsible for an outage?

There is also something to be said for the humility the experience can teach, if you allow it to. Every system we build will eventually be inherited by someone else, and that person will look at our careful decisions with the same bewilderment we now direct at the work of our predecessors. Arguably this is the most useful perspective an engineer can cultivate: the awareness that our own code is legacy in waiting, that the clarity we feel while writing it will not survive our departure, and that the kindest thing we can do for our successors is to leave behind some record of why things are the way they are. Would we write differently if we genuinely believed that our work would be read by a stranger who might be tempted to think us careless? I suspect we would — and I suspect the difference would be considerable.

The inheritance of systems is, in the end, a particular instance of something much older and more general. Institutions are always passed from one generation of participants to the next, carrying with them habits and structures whose original rationale has faded; each generation must decide what to preserve, what to modify, and what to discard, usually without full knowledge of what it is deciding about. Software merely makes this process unusually visible, since the inheritance is written down in a form that can be read, however imperfectly. Perhaps the best we can hope for is to approach what we inherit with curiosity rather than contempt, and to leave behind something that our successors will be able to understand a little better than we understood what was left for us.
