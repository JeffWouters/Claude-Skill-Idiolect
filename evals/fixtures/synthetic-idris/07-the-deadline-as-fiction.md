Where do deadlines actually come from? The question may seem odd, since most deadlines arrive with an air of inevitability, as though they had been imposed by the nature of things rather than decided by particular people for particular reasons. Some are genuinely external — a regulatory requirement, a contractual obligation, an event that will happen whether or not the software is ready — but a considerable proportion are, in some sense, invented; they emerge from planning conversations, from the desire to have something to announce, from a general feeling that work without a date attached is work that will never be finished. I do not say this to dismiss them, since invention is not the same thing as arbitrariness. It may be that invented deadlines serve a real purpose, concentrating attention and forcing decisions that would otherwise be postponed indefinitely; the difficulty is that we so rarely distinguish between the invented and the unavoidable, and we therefore tend to treat all of them with the same anxious reverence.

Consider what happens to a team as a deadline approaches. There is often a gradual narrowing of attention, in which everything not directly connected to the immediate goal begins to seem like a distraction; conversations become shorter, decisions become quicker, and the careful deliberation that characterised the early stages of the project gives way to a more pragmatic, sometimes more ruthless, style of working. Some of this is healthy — the pressure clarifies priorities and dissolves the kind of endless refinement that can paralyse a project. Some of it, arguably, is not; corners are cut that will have to be repaired later, people work hours that they would never accept in calmer periods, and the quality of judgement declines precisely when the stakes are highest. Who decides whether the trade-off is worth making, and on what basis? In my experience the decision is almost never made explicitly; it simply happens, as the logic of the deadline asserts itself.

There is a curious psychology to all this. A deadline that is widely understood to be arbitrary can nevertheless exert enormous force, because once it has been announced it acquires a reputational dimension; missing it would mean admitting that the original plan was wrong, and the people who made the plan may be more invested in its correctness than in its outcome. You have perhaps seen this dynamic play out, in which a date that everyone privately believes is unrealistic is publicly defended until the last possible moment, at which point it is quietly moved and the whole exercise begins again. The consequence is a kind of institutional theatre, in which the deadline functions less as a planning tool than as a performance of confidence. I suspect that most participants in this theatre understand its nature perfectly well; they simply feel unable to be the one who stops the performance.

Is there a better way to relate to deadlines? It seems to me that the most important step is simply to be honest about their origins — to ask, whenever a date is proposed, what would actually happen if it were missed, and to allow the answer to shape how seriously the date is treated. A deadline tied to an unmovable external event deserves one kind of response; a deadline chosen because it seemed like a reasonable target deserves another, and conflating the two does a disservice to everyone involved. Perhaps we might also learn to renegotiate dates earlier and more openly, treating a revised deadline not as a failure of planning but as the natural result of learning more about the work. None of this is technically difficult, and none of it requires new tools or elaborate methods. What makes it difficult is the reluctance to disappoint, which is, in some sense, a reluctance to be honest.

Deadlines, like so many features of working life, are social agreements that have forgotten they are social agreements. They present themselves as facts about time when they are really facts about people — about what we have promised one another, what we fear, and what we are prepared to sacrifice to avoid that fear. To see them clearly is not to abandon them but to hold them with a certain lightness, recognising that the calendar is a tool we have made rather than a force that governs us. Perhaps the healthiest teams are simply those which remember, even under pressure, that every date on the plan was once a decision, and that decisions can, with sufficient courage, be made again.
