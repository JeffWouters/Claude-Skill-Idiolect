There is a peculiar quality to the silence of a house in the early hours when you are waiting for something to go wrong. Perhaps anyone who has carried a pager, or its modern equivalent, will recognise the sensation — the half-sleep in which part of the mind remains alert for a sound that may never come, the phone placed within reach on the bedside table, the faint sense of guilt that accompanies any attempt to relax fully. We tend to describe on-call work in operational terms, as a matter of rotations and escalation policies and response expectations, and these descriptions are accurate as far as they go. What they tend to omit is the experiential dimension of the work, which is arguably where most of its cost is borne. It may be that the hardest part of being on call is not the incident itself but the long vigil that precedes it, and the knowledge that the vigil will resume as soon as the incident is resolved.

What happens when the alert finally arrives? In my experience there is a curious transformation, in which the diffuse anxiety of waiting condenses into something sharper and, oddly, more bearable; there is now a problem to be understood, a system to be examined, a sequence of hypotheses to be tested. The mind, which had been circling uselessly in the dark, suddenly finds a purpose to which it can attach itself. Yet this purpose must be pursued in conditions that are almost designed to impair judgement — alone, tired, working from dashboards that were built by someone else for some other purpose, uncertain whether the anomaly on the screen is the cause of the trouble or merely one of its symptoms. It is a considerable test of composure, and I suspect it is one for which very few of us have been explicitly prepared. The organisation assumes that competence during the day will translate into competence at night, and the assumption is, at best, only partly true.

Consider the loneliness of it. During working hours, a difficult problem is usually a shared problem; there are colleagues to consult, channels to post in, the reassuring presence of other people who might notice what we have missed. At night that social fabric thins almost to nothing, and the engineer on call must decide, often without much guidance, whether the situation warrants waking someone else. Who wants to be the person who raised the alarm for nothing? The hesitation is understandable, and it is also, arguably, one of the more dangerous features of on-call culture, since it encourages people to struggle alone with problems that would be resolved far more quickly with a second pair of eyes. An escalation policy may say, in formal terms, that help is always available; the lived reality is often that asking for help feels like an admission of inadequacy.

It seems to me that the teams which handle on-call work most humanely are those that treat these experiential questions as legitimate subjects of attention rather than as private matters for individuals to manage. They talk openly about the fear of escalating; they make it clear, repeatedly and by example, that waking a colleague is a reasonable decision rather than a failure; they review not only what went wrong with the system but what it was like to be the person responding. You might think of this as a kind of institutional acknowledgement that the pager is carried by a human being, with all the fragility that entails. None of it requires elaborate process — it requires, perhaps, only a willingness to ask how the night actually felt and to take the answer seriously. Is that really so much to ask of a team that depends on someone being awake when everyone else is asleep?

I have come to suspect that on-call work reveals something about the organisations that practise it which is otherwise difficult to see. It shows us how much responsibility we are willing to place on individuals without offering corresponding support; it shows us whether our systems were designed to be understood by tired people or only by rested ones; it shows us, in a sense, what we believe we owe to one another when nobody is watching. The quiet hours are where these beliefs are tested most honestly, because the usual performances of collaboration fall away and only the underlying commitments remain. Perhaps the measure of a team is not how well it works together in the daylight but how well it looks after whoever is left holding the phone in the dark.
