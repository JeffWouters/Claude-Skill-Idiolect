What is code review actually for? The question sounds naive, and most teams would answer it without hesitation — to catch defects, to maintain standards, to share knowledge across the team — yet the answers tend to conceal a considerable disagreement about which of these purposes matters most, and the disagreement shapes almost everything about how reviews are conducted. A reviewer who believes the primary purpose is catching defects will read differently from one who believes it is teaching, and both will read differently from one who treats the review as a gate that must be passed before work can be declared finished. It may be that many of the frustrations associated with code review arise not from bad behaviour but from these unspoken differences in purpose; the author expects one kind of attention and receives another. I suspect that few teams have ever articulated, explicitly and together, what they want their reviews to achieve.

Consider the experience of submitting work for review. You have spent considerable time on something, you have made a series of decisions that seemed reasonable at the time, and you now present the result to colleagues whose judgement you respect and whose approval you require. There is inevitably a degree of exposure in this, however experienced you may be; the review is, in some sense, an evaluation not only of the code but of the person who wrote it. Is it any wonder that comments which seem neutral to the reviewer can feel pointed to the author? The written form compounds the difficulty, since it strips away the tone of voice and facial expression that would soften the same remark in conversation, leaving only the bare words to be interpreted by someone who may already be anxious about how they will be received.

There is also the question of what reviewers are able to notice. Reading someone else's code is cognitively demanding work; it requires us to reconstruct the author's intentions from the artefact alone, often without the context of the conversations and false starts that shaped it. Perhaps unsurprisingly, reviewers tend to concentrate on what is easiest to see — naming, formatting, small matters of style — while the deeper questions of design and architecture receive comparatively little attention, since they would require a level of engagement that the reviewer may not have time to give. The result is a review culture that is arguably inverted, lavishing scrutiny on details that could be automated while passing over the decisions that will shape the system for a long time to come. Who among us has not approved a change whose overall direction we doubted, simply because the individual lines seemed acceptable?

It seems to me that the most valuable reviews are those which begin, rather than end, a conversation. They ask questions instead of issuing verdicts; they distinguish clearly between matters of preference and matters of correctness; they acknowledge what is good about the work as well as what might be improved, not as a rhetorical softening but as genuine information about what the author should continue doing. Such reviews take longer, and they require a certain generosity of spirit that is difficult to sustain under deadline pressure. They also, I suspect, produce better software over time, because they cultivate a team in which people are willing to submit unfinished thinking for discussion rather than waiting until their work is defensible. The alternative — a culture in which reviews are adversarial and authors learn to anticipate criticism — tends to produce code that is polished on the surface and fragile beneath it.

Perhaps the most useful thing a team can do is to talk openly about its reviews, not in the abstract language of guidelines and checklists but in the concrete terms of how particular reviews felt to give and to receive. Such conversations are rarely comfortable, since they touch on questions of status and competence that most of us would prefer to leave unexamined; they are nevertheless the only means by which a shared understanding of purpose can emerge. Code review, in the end, is one of the few occasions in working life when we are formally invited to look closely at one another's thinking. What we do with that invitation says a great deal about whether we regard our colleagues as collaborators to be understood or as sources of error to be contained.
