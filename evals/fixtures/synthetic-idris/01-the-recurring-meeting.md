What is a meeting, really, once it has been placed on the calendar and allowed to recur indefinitely? It may be that the recurring meeting is the closest thing most organisations have to a ritual, in the anthropological sense of the word — a gathering whose original purpose has quietly dissolved while its form persists, maintained by a vague institutional reluctance to be the person who suggests cancelling it. We arrive, we exchange the customary pleasantries, and we wait for someone to articulate why we are there; perhaps someone does, and perhaps the explanation is convincing, but more often the conversation drifts toward whatever happens to be most visible that week. I suspect that very few of us would defend the meeting if asked to justify it from first principles, and yet we continue to attend with considerable loyalty. There is something in this that deserves more attention than the usual complaints about wasted time, because the persistence of the ritual tells us something about what teams actually need from one another.

Consider the way a meeting begins its life. Usually it is created in response to a genuine difficulty — a project that has lost its sense of direction, a dependency between teams that nobody quite owns, a decision that keeps being deferred — and in those first weeks it is arguably the most valuable hour anyone spends. The people in the room have a shared problem; they bring information that cannot easily be written down; they leave with a clearer sense of who is doing what. The difficulty is that the problem eventually resolves, or changes shape, while the invitation remains exactly as it was, reproducing itself week after week with the indifference of any automated process. Does anyone notice the moment at which the meeting stops serving its purpose? In my experience the transition is almost always invisible, since the attendees are still talking, the notes are still being taken, and the calendar still insists that this is where we ought to be.

It would be easy to conclude from this that recurring meetings should simply be abolished, and there are teams that have tried precisely that experiment with a certain evangelical enthusiasm. I am not convinced that the conclusion follows. What the meeting provides, even in its degraded form, is a guaranteed occasion for contact — a moment when you can raise something half-formed without having to justify interrupting anyone, when the quieter members of a team can be seen and heard, when small frictions surface before they harden into grievances. Perhaps the ritual persists precisely because it performs this function, however inefficiently, and because nobody has designed a better substitute. In some sense the meeting is less a tool for making decisions than a piece of social infrastructure; its value is not visible in any particular session but in the accumulated familiarity it produces over months. Remove it without replacing that function, and we may discover that the team has become efficient in a way that is also, subtly, lonelier.

What, then, might we do differently? One possibility is to treat each recurring meeting as something that must periodically earn its renewal, rather than as a permanent fixture whose continuation requires no argument. A team might pause every so often and ask, with genuine openness, whether the original purpose still holds, whether a different purpose has replaced it, and whether that new purpose is worth the collective attention it consumes. The conversation is often uncomfortable, since it touches on questions of status and inclusion that are rarely discussed openly; the person who organised the meeting may feel that their judgement is being questioned, and the people who value it most may be the least inclined to say so. It is nevertheless the kind of discomfort that tends to be productive, because it forces the team to articulate what it actually wants from its time together. I suspect that many meetings would survive such scrutiny in a modified form — shorter, less frequent, or reframed around a clearer question — and that a few would be quietly and gratefully retired.

There is a broader lesson here about the way institutions accumulate habits. Very little of what we do at work is designed from scratch; most of it is inherited, layered upon earlier decisions whose rationale has long since been forgotten, and the recurring meeting is simply the most visible example of a pattern that runs through our processes, our tools and our documentation alike. We tend to treat these inheritances as either sacred or absurd, when it may be that the more useful attitude is a kind of patient curiosity — asking what need the habit once served, whether that need still exists, and what we would lose if we let it go. Is it too much to hope that we might bring that curiosity to our calendars? Perhaps not; perhaps the humble weekly meeting is exactly where such a practice ought to begin, because it is small enough to change and familiar enough that changing it teaches us something about everything else.
