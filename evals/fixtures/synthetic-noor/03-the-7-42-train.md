I take the 7.42 train. I have taken it for six years.

The walk to the station is 14 minutes. I have timed it. With a heavy bag it is 16. In rain it is 12, because I walk faster and take the short cut past the bins.

So I leave the house at 7.25. That gives me three minutes of slack. Three minutes is the right amount. Less and I run. More and I stand on the platform in the cold.

The platform has a shape I know by heart. The front two carriages stop by the yellow bench. The middle stop by the clock. The back ones stop past the end of the roof, where you get wet. I stand by the clock. The doors open right in front of me. Most days.

There is a crowd of regulars. We do not speak. But we know each other.

There is the man in the green coat who reads the paper folded in four. There is the woman with two phones, one in each hand. There is a boy with a cello case taller than he is. He gets off after two stops. I have never heard him play. But I think about the cello most mornings.

The train is late one day in five. I have kept count, on and off. Mostly by two or three minutes. Once by 40. That was the day of the fallen tree. We stood on the platform and watched the board change. Delayed. Delayed. Then a new time. Then delayed again.

And the odd thing about that day: nobody left. Nobody went for a bus. We just stood there. A few people rang work. The man in the green coat read his whole paper, both sides.

I have a seat I like. Left side, facing forward, by the window. The left side gets the sun in winter. In summer I move to the right.

The ride is 31 minutes. Seven stops. I know them by the sounds as much as the sights. The first stop has a long curve before it, and the wheels squeal. The third has a bridge, and the carriage goes hollow for a moment. The fifth is by the canal. I look for the heron. It is there about one day in three. Grey and still on the far bank. Then gone.

I used to work on the train. Laptop open, emails done before the office. I stopped two years ago. Now I look out of the window. Or I read. Or I just sit.

It is the only half hour in the day with nothing to do. No one can reach me. Well, they can. But I do not answer.

In winter the ride starts in the dark. By the canal the sky is grey. By the last stop it is light. You can watch it happen. The windows go from black mirrors to glass. Your own face fades out and the fields come in.

In summer it is light the whole way. Less to watch. But the fields are green and there are horses at stop four.

The way home is different. The 17.51. It is full, and hot, and people are tired. I stand most days. That is fine. I stand by the door and hold the rail and look at nothing.

I know this is a small life, in a way. The same train. The same seat. The same heron. But the sameness is useful. It clears a space. When nothing changes, you start to see the small things that do.

The frost on the fence at stop two. The day the canal froze. The new graffiti on the bridge, a yellow fox, one metre tall.

Last week the boy with the cello was not there. Nor the next day. Then on Friday he was back. With a new case. Black this time, not brown.

This morning the train came in on time. The doors opened by the clock. I took my seat on the left. And at the canal the heron stood in the reeds, one leg up, in the first light.
