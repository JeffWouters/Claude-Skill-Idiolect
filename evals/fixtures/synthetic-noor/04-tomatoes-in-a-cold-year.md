This was a bad year for tomatoes. I grew them anyway.

I sowed the seeds in March, on the kitchen windowsill. Two trays. 24 cells each. Three kinds: a red cherry, a yellow pear and a big ribbed one for slicing. The packet for the ribbed one said 80 days to fruit. I laughed at that later.

They came up in six days. Thin green hooks pushing out of the soil. Then two round leaves each. Then the first true leaves, the ones with the ragged edge and that smell. You know the smell. Sharp and green. It gets on your fingers and stays.

So far so good.

But April was cold. Then May was colder. Most nights were 6 or 7 degrees. Some nights were 3. Tomatoes do not like anything under 10. They sulk. They stop growing and go a dull purple under the leaves.

I kept them inside far too long. By late May they were 40 centimetres tall and leaning at the glass. Thin, pale, reaching for the light. Leggy, the books call it.

So I made a choice. I planted them out on the first of June, cold or not.

I dug the holes deep. Deeper than you would think. A tomato will grow roots all along a buried stem. So I laid each plant on its side in a trench and buried the stem up to the top four leaves. The tip bends up toward the sun on its own. In two days it stands straight.

Twelve plants in the bed by the south wall. The wall holds heat. I measured it once on a sunny day. Two degrees warmer at the base than out in the open.

And then it rained. For three weeks. Not hard rain. Just a thin grey rain that never stopped. The soil stayed wet and cold. The plants stood still.

I did what I could. I put a sheet of clear plastic over a frame of bamboo canes. It kept off the worst of it. I pinched out the side shoots every few days. I tied the stems to the canes with soft string. Loose loops. A figure of eight, so the string does not cut.

Then July. Some sun at last. The plants grew 20 centimetres in a week. Yellow flowers came in clusters. I tapped the stems each morning to shake the pollen loose. The bees did the rest.

The first fruit set in the middle of the month. Small and hard and green. Then more. By August the cherry plants were heavy with them.

But green is not red.

August was cool again. Grey days. Short sun. The fruit sat there, full size, and would not turn. I checked every morning. Green. Green. A faint blush on one. Then green again, or so it looked.

September came. Then the first cold night, down to 2 degrees. I knew the end was close.

So I picked them all. Every tomato, ripe or not. It took an hour. I filled two crates and a bowl.

Here is the count. 31 red cherries. 12 yellow pears. And 140 green ones of all sizes. The big ribbed kind gave me four fruits in total. None of them red.

The green ones went into the drawer of an old dresser in the hall. One layer. A ripe banana in the middle. The banana gives off a gas that helps them turn. Over three weeks, about half went red. Not as sweet as sun-ripe. But fine in a sauce.

The rest I cooked green. Chutney, mostly. Green tomatoes, onions, sugar, vinegar, a few raisins. Two hours on a low heat until it went dark and thick. Nine jars.

Next year I will sow later. Or grow them in the greenhouse at the allotment. Or both. I have said this before.

This morning I opened the dresser drawer. Three left. Two orange. One still green, with a dry brown stalk and a little star of leaves on top.
