The fence along the back of my garden is 18 metres long. It has 12 panels and 13 posts. I painted it last weekend. It took two days.

The last time was five years ago. You could tell. The paint had gone from dark green to a dull grey green. On the south facing side it was flaking off in thin curls. Moss grew along the bottom rail. Two panels had gone soft at the base.

So I started on the Friday evening with a walk along it. Notebook in hand. I pressed each panel at the bottom with my thumb. Firm is good. Soft means rot. Two soft ones, as I thought. Panel four and panel nine.

I did not replace them. Not this year. I cut out the soft wood at the base with a chisel. I let it dry overnight under a sheet. Then I filled the gaps with wood filler and a strip of new timber. Not pretty. But solid.

Then the brushing down. This is the dull part. It is also the part that makes the paint last. I used a stiff brush on a long handle. Top to bottom, every panel. Dust, cobwebs, flakes of old paint, dead leaves stuck in the joints. The moss I scraped off the bottom rail with a paint scraper. It came away in green mats.

That took the whole of Saturday morning. Four hours. My arms ached.

I checked the weather three times. Dry until Monday. 17 degrees. A light wind. Good paint weather. Not too hot. Paint that dries too fast in the sun goes on in streaks.

So after lunch I opened the first tin.

I use a water based fence paint. Dark green, the same as before. Five litres covers about 30 square metres, the tin says. My fence is about 32 square metres on my side. So two tins for two coats, and a bit spare. I bought three. You always need the third.

I stirred it with a flat stick for two full minutes. The colour sinks to the bottom. You have to lift it back up. Then I poured some into a small bucket. I never paint straight from the tin. If a leaf gets in, or a fly, it is only the bucket that is spoiled.

The brush was 10 centimetres wide. A good one, with long bristles. A cheap brush drops hairs into the paint. You spend all day picking them out.

And then I just painted.

Top rail first. Then the boards, top to bottom, following the grain. Then the bottom rail. Then the post. One panel at a time. Keep a wet edge. That means you always brush from paint that is still wet, into bare wood. If you go back over paint that is half dry, it leaves marks.

Each panel took about 12 minutes. By six o'clock I had done all 12, first coat. And all 13 posts.

I stood at the back door and looked. It was patchy. The first coat always is. The old wood drinks it up in some places and not in others. But it was green again.

On Sunday I did the second coat. It went on faster. Nine minutes a panel. The paint glides once there is a base under it. By three I was done. I washed the brush in a bucket of warm water with a drop of soap until the water ran clear. Then I shook it out and hung it up by the handle to dry.

Here is the count. Two days. Two and a half tins. One brush, one bucket, one scraper. Two panels patched. About ten hours in all.

It should last five years. Four, on the south side.

My neighbour came out on Sunday evening. He looked at his side of the fence, which is bare wood. Then at mine. He said nothing. But this morning I saw him in the shop, with a tin of the same paint under his arm.

Tonight the fence is dry. The light is low. And along the bottom rail, where the moss was, there is one green fingerprint of mine, set hard in the paint.
