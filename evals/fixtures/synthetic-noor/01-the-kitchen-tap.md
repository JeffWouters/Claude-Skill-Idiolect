The kitchen tap started to drip in March. One drop every four seconds. I timed it with the clock on the oven.

At first I did nothing. A drip is easy to live with. You stop hearing it after a day or two.

But then I put a jug under it. By morning the jug held 600 millilitres. So that was the end of living with it.

I have fixed taps before. Not this one. This one is old, with a brass body and two cross-shaped handles. Hot on the left. Cold on the right. The drip came from the cold side.

So on Saturday I cleared the counter. I laid a tea towel in the sink. That is the first rule: cover the plughole. Small parts fall. They always fall.

Then I turned off the water. There is a stop valve under the sink, behind the bleach and a bag of old sponges. It had not been turned in years. It took both hands and a cloth for grip. Then it gave.

I opened the tap to drain it. A cough of air. A thin run of water. Then nothing.

The handle came off with one screw under a small blue cap. I lifted the cap with the tip of a knife. The screw was a flat head, soft with age. I used the right driver and pushed hard as I turned. It came out whole.

Under the handle sat the headgear. That is the brass part that holds the washer. It takes a spanner. Mine is an adjustable one, 20 centimetres long, from my father's box. I set it snug on the flats and held the tap body with my other hand. You have to hold the body. Or the whole tap turns in the counter and you make a new problem.

It came loose on the third try.

And there was the washer. Black rubber, once. Now it was grey and hard and split on one edge like a dry lip. No wonder. It could not seal against anything.

I took it to the shop on the corner. The man there has a drawer of washers sorted by size. He held mine up to the light. Then he picked one out without looking. Half inch. 30 cents. I bought three.

At home the new washer went on with a small nut. I tightened it by hand, then a quarter turn with pliers. Not more. Rubber needs room to press.

Then back in with the headgear. Snug, then a little more. I wiped the threads first. Grit in the threads is how you strip them.

Handle on. Screw in. Blue cap pressed home with my thumb.

So then the valve under the sink. I turned it slowly. You want the pipes to fill without a bang. There was a hiss. Then a knock somewhere in the wall. Then quiet.

I opened the cold tap. It spat twice and ran clean. I closed it.

And I waited.

I stood at the sink and watched the spout for a full minute. Nothing. I went and made a coffee and came back. Nothing.

The whole job took 40 minutes. Most of it was the stop valve and the walk to the shop. The actual work was ten minutes. That is how it goes with small repairs. The work is short. The getting ready is long.

I put the two spare washers in a jar marked TAP. The jar lives in the drawer by the back door. It holds the old one too. I keep the old one so I remember the size.

That night the kitchen was very quiet. I noticed it when I switched off the light. No tick. No small wet sound in the dark.

In the morning the jug was still under the spout. Dry. A dead fly at the bottom and nothing else.
