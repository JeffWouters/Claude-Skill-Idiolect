I check the weather three times a day. Once on my phone. Twice by looking out of the window.

The phone is fine. It gives me numbers. 14 degrees. 60 percent chance of rain. Wind from the south west at 20 kilometres an hour. But the numbers do not tell me much about the next hour. For that I look up.

I learned this from my grandmother. She kept hens and a big vegetable plot. She had no radio in the kitchen. She had a back door and a pair of eyes.

She taught me three things. I still use all of them.

First: watch the clouds, not the sky. A blue sky tells you nothing. The clouds tell you what is coming. Thin high streaks, like combed hair, mean a change in a day or so. A grey sheet that thickens from the west means rain by evening. Tall white heaps with flat dark bases mean showers, and soon.

Second: watch the wind. Where I live, most weather comes from the west. A wind that swings from south to west means rain is on its way. A wind that swings to the north means cold and clear.

Third: watch the birds. When the swallows fly low, over the grass, rain is close. The insects they eat come down in damp air. When the swallows are high, you are fine for a while.

So that is my kit. Clouds, wind, birds. And the phone, as a check.

It matters because I do a lot outside. The garden. The allotment. Paint on the fence. Washing on the line. I ride my bike to the shops. I get it wrong less than I used to. But I still get it wrong.

Last month I hung out a full load of sheets at nine. The sky was clear. The phone said ten percent. By eleven there was a low grey band in the west. By half past, the first drops. I ran out and pulled everything in. Damp, not wet. A near thing.

I had missed the signs. There was a thin milky haze at nine, high up. And a ring round the sun. My grandmother would have seen it. She used to say a ring round the sun means rain within a day. She was not always right. But she was right that time.

I keep a notebook now. A cheap one, spiral bound, on the shelf by the back door. Each morning I write three lines. The time. What the sky looks like. What I think will happen. Then in the evening I write one line. What did happen.

I have kept it for two years. It is not science. But it has taught me things.

I am good at rain. I call it right about four times in five. I am bad at wind. I always think it will die down. It does not. And I am terrible at frost. The clear, still nights fool me every time. I go to bed thinking it is mild. I wake up to a white lawn and black dahlias.

So now, on a clear night in spring or autumn, I go out at ten. I stand on the grass for two minutes. If I can see a lot of stars, and there is no wind, and my breath shows, I cover the tender plants. An old sheet over the dahlias. A pot turned over the young beans.

I have saved a lot of plants that way.

This morning the phone said sun all day. I opened the back door. The wind was from the south. There was a haze in the west. The swallows were low over the field behind the fence.

I brought the washing basket back inside. And I put the kettle on and watched the first grey edge come over the roofs.
