Once a month I sharpen everything. Every knife in the kitchen. Every chisel in the shed. The garden shears, the secateurs, the axe.

It takes about two hours. I do it on a Sunday afternoon, at the kitchen table, with the radio on low.

I lay out a towel first. An old one, blue, with a burn mark in one corner. Then the stones. I have three. A coarse one, a medium one and a fine one. The coarse is grey and rough like a road. The fine is pale and smooth like soap. They sit in a tray of water for ten minutes before I start. Bubbles rise out of them as they soak. When the bubbles stop, they are ready.

Then I lay out the blades. Kitchen knives on the left. Six of them. Shed tools in the middle. Garden tools on the right.

So I start with the worst one. It is always the bread knife. But you cannot sharpen a bread knife on a flat stone. The teeth need a thin round rod. So I leave it aside, every time. And every time I tell myself I will buy the rod.

Then the chef's knife. This is the one that matters. I use it every day. It is 20 centimetres long and has a small chip near the heel from a frozen pea.

Here is the method. It is simple. Getting it right is not.

Hold the blade at a low angle to the stone. About 15 degrees. That is lower than you think. If you lay a coin under the spine, that is close. Then push the blade away from you along the stone, edge first, as if you were slicing a thin layer off the top. Ten strokes. Then turn it over. Ten on the other side.

The hard part is the angle. Your wrist wants to rock. If it rocks, you round the edge. A round edge is a blunt edge.

So I go slowly. I watch the wet line of steel on the stone. When it is even from heel to tip, the angle is right.

After the coarse stone, I feel for the burr. That is a thin lip of metal along the edge. You feel it by running your thumb across the blade. Not along it. Across it. From the spine out toward the edge. If there is a burr, it catches the skin like a tiny hook.

Once the burr is there on the full length, I move to the medium stone. Same strokes. Fewer of them. Then the fine stone. Fewer again. The burr gets smaller each time. By the end there is almost none.

Then the test. A sheet of newspaper, held up by one corner. A sharp knife goes through it with no sound. A blunt one tears it.

The chef's knife passed. First try.

The chisels are different. They have a flat back and a bevel on the front. The back has to be flat as glass, or the edge will never be true. I did the backs years ago, when I first got them. It took a whole day. Now they only need the bevel. Ten minutes each.

The garden tools I do with a small diamond file. They do not need to be sharp like a knife. Just clean and keen. The secateurs had sap dried on them. I scrubbed that off first with wire wool.

The axe last. Big strokes with a file. It only needs to split logs. Too fine an edge and it chips on a knot.

By five o'clock the towel is grey and wet. The stones are grey too, with a slurry of steel and grit. I rinse them under the tap. I dry every blade and wipe it with a drop of oil.

I put the knives back in the block. The chisels back in their roll. The garden tools back on their nails in the shed.

The bread knife goes back in the drawer. Blunt. Next to it is a note in my own hand that says: rod.
