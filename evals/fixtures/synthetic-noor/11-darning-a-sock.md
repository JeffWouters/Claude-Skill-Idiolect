I have a pair of wool socks that are 12 years old. They have been darned nine times. I can see every repair.

They were grey when I bought them. Now they are grey with patches of green, brown and one of bright red. The red was all I had that night. I do not regret it.

Darning is not hard. It is slow. That is different.

You need three things. A darning needle, which is long with a big eye. Wool, close to the weight of the sock. And something round to hold the sock tight while you work. There are wooden darning mushrooms made for this. I use a light bulb. An old one, dead, the big round kind. It fits inside the heel just right.

So here is how I do it.

First I turn the sock the right way out and find the hole. Heels go first on my socks. Then the ball of the foot. Then the big toe. The hole is rarely one clean hole. There is a hole, and around it a wide ring of thin, worn wool. You have to darn the thin part as well. Or next month the hole is back, one ring wider.

I slip the bulb in and pull the sock tight over it. Then I run a line of small stitches round the thin part, about one centimetre out from the edge. This marks the patch. It also stops it from spreading.

Then I lay the warp. That is a set of long stitches going one way, top to bottom, across the hole. Side by side. Close together. Each one picks up a little of the good wool at the top and bottom. On a hole the size of a coin, that is about 20 lines.

It looks like the strings of a small harp.

And then the weft. This is the part I like. I go across, side to side, over one line and under the next. Over, under, over, under. At the end of each row I turn and come back. Under where I went over. Over where I went under.

Slowly a small square of cloth grows over the hole. A woven patch. Firm and flat.

The rule is to keep it loose. Not slack. Loose. If you pull tight, the patch puckers. Then it rubs a blister on your heel. I learned that on darn number two.

A patch the size of a coin takes me 30 minutes. A big heel hole takes an hour. I do it in the evening, in the chair by the lamp. The radio on. A cup of tea going cold on the arm.

Some people buy new socks. I know. Socks are cheap. A new pair costs less than an hour of my time, if you count it that way.

But I do not count it that way. The hour is not work. It is a quiet hour with my hands busy. And the sock is better after. A darned heel is thicker than the old heel. It lasts longer than the wool around it. So the next hole is always somewhere new.

My grandfather darned his own socks. He was a fisherman. He could darn in the dark by feel. He showed me once, when I was small. I did not pay much attention. Years later I found his mushroom in a box. Dark wood, worn smooth. I tried to use it. I could not remember how. So I taught myself from a book. The bulb works better than the mushroom for me. I kept the mushroom anyway.

These grey socks will not last for ever. The wool at the ankle is getting thin now, all the way round. That is hard to darn. When that goes, they go.

Tonight I finished darn number ten. A small one, on the left toe. I used the brown wool. I pulled out the bulb, turned the sock the right way, and laid it on the arm of the chair next to its pair.
