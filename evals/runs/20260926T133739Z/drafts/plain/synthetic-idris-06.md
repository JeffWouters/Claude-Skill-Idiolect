Look at any calendar and you will find them: repeating meetings, set up long ago, still showing up every week. It can help to see them less as meetings and more as rituals.

Rituals have a particular quality. Their form outlasts the reason they were created. The same slot, the same invite list, the same order of speakers, long after anyone can say exactly why. And yet attendance stays loyal. People keep turning up, partly out of habit, partly because skipping a ritual feels like a small breach of something, even when nobody could say what.

It is worth remembering how these meetings usually begin. Almost always, they start by solving a real, shared problem. A project needs coordination, two teams keep stepping on each other, a decision keeps getting delayed because the right people are never in the same room. Someone sets up a recurring slot to fix that, and for a while it works. The meeting earns its place because everyone in it can see what it is for.

Then the problem changes, or gets solved, or moves elsewhere. The meeting does not. It keeps its slot and its invite list, and it fills its time with whatever is at hand. Updates that could be read. Discussions that belong to smaller groups. Agenda items that exist because an agenda is expected.

What makes this hard is that nobody notices when a meeting stops being useful. There is no single moment when it tips over. It declines slowly, one slightly less valuable session at a time, and each individual session seems fine enough. By the time someone does notice, the meeting has become part of how the team understands itself, and questioning it feels like questioning the team.

That is exactly why it is worth looking at repeating meetings with some deliberate distance. Ask what problem this ritual first solved, and whether that problem is still here. If it is, keep it. If it is not, the loyalty people show it deserves a better object.
