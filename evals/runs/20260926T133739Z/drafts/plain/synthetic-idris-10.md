Most progress reports answer one question: what got done? They list the tickets closed, the features shipped, the milestones passed. That is useful as far as it goes. But it misrepresents what teams actually know, because a list of completed tasks says nothing about the insight gained along the way.

Consider how much of a team's real progress is not a task at all. Discovering that an approach will not work. Learning that a dependency is shakier than it looked. Realising that the problem is different from the one originally described. These are often the most valuable things a team learns in a given period, and none of them fit neatly into a list of completed items. So they get left out, or buried in a note at the bottom that nobody reads.

That omission skews priorities. What gets reported gets rewarded, and what gets rewarded gets done. If reports only count finished tasks, teams learn to favour work that finishes cleanly and visibly. Exploratory work, the kind that reduces risk without producing anything you can tick off, starts to look like a poor use of time. The team ends up optimising for the appearance of progress rather than for understanding.

Better reports would look different. Alongside what was done, they would state confidence levels. How sure are we about the plan from here? Which parts do we understand well, and which are still guesses? They would flag unclear areas explicitly, not as an admission of failure but as useful information for anyone making decisions. A report that says "we are confident about these three things and unsure about this one" is far more useful than one that says everything is on track.

The format is not the hard part, though. You could introduce a confidence field tomorrow and nothing would change if people felt it was unsafe to use it honestly. The fix is cultural. It depends on treating an admission of uncertainty as honest rather than as weak. If a team lead writes "we do not know yet" and the response is concern about their competence, the next report will not say it. If the response is a useful conversation about how to find out, the next report will say it again.

That is the real test of a reporting culture: not how neat the reports look, but whether people can say what they do not know without paying for it. Get that right, and the reports will start to reflect what the team actually knows.
