The conversion of a pagan people is usually described in the language of light breaking upon darkness. The story of how a certain Norwegian king brought his farmers to baptism is told in rather plainer terms, and it is the better for it.

The king came into a district where the old gods were still worshipped and the farmers had no intention of giving them up. He did not begin with a sermon. He went into the temple and knocked the idols off their seats, and while he was doing so his men killed a local chief who had stood against him. [example needed] It was an opening that left very little room for theological debate.

The farmers were then given their choice. They might accept the new faith and be baptized, or they might fight the king and his men. There was no third course. It was put to them fairly enough, in the sense that nothing was concealed: here was the cross, and there were the swords, and they could take whichever they preferred.

They were practical people and they took the cross. But they were not so dazed as to trust the arrangement entirely, and hostages were exchanged as a guarantee that the peace would hold. It is a detail worth pausing on. A people who had truly been won by the beauty of a new religion would hardly have needed hostages to keep them in it. These farmers had been won by something else, and both sides knew it.

That something else was the king's real argument. He may have believed sincerely in the faith he brought, and he may have hoped his farmers would come to believe in it too. But what persuaded them that afternoon was not doctrine. It was the fact that he had more men, better armed, than they did. Superior force was the proof he offered, and they accepted it as proof.

I do not think the story is very unusual. It is only more candid than most.
