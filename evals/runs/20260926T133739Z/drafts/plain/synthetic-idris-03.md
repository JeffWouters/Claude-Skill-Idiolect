A target date starts as a guess. Then someone announces it, and it begins to take on a life of its own.

Part of the reason is simple: once a date has been said out loud, reputations get tied to it. The person who proposed it, the manager who repeated it upward, the team that nodded along. Each of them now has something to lose if the date slips. So when doubts appear, as they usually do, the date gets defended rather than questioned. People work around the evidence instead of reacting to it, and the date keeps standing long after most of those involved privately stopped believing in it.

A more useful habit is to ask what missing the date would actually cost. Not in the abstract, but concretely. Would a customer be let down? Would another team be blocked? Would anything happen at all? Often the honest answer is less dramatic than the anxiety around the date suggests.

It also helps to treat different kinds of dates differently. Some dates come from outside: a contract, a regulation, an event that will happen whether we are ready or not. Those deserve to be taken seriously, and the conversation is about scope and trade-offs. Other dates are chosen. We picked them, often for reasonable motives, but we could also pick again. Treating a chosen date with the gravity of an external one is where much of the unnecessary pain comes from.

When a date is in trouble, renegotiate early. The earlier you raise it, the more options everyone has, and the less it feels like a failure. Raising it late leaves nothing but apologies.

None of this is hard to understand. What makes it hard to do is something else: the fear of letting people down. That fear keeps people quiet until the problem can no longer be hidden. It is the true hurdle, and naming it is the first step toward getting past it.
