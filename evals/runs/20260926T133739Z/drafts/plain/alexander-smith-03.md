There is in most people, however settled they appear, a restless instinct that wants to wander. It shows itself in the glance at a departing train, in the pleasure of a map, in the vague sense that somewhere beyond the next hill things might be different and better. We tend to treat this instinct as a weakness, something to be outgrown or kept in check. I am not so sure it deserves that treatment.

It helps to remember what the age of exploration did. For a few extraordinary generations, the edges of the known world kept moving outward. Every voyage brought back news of coasts and creatures and peoples no one at home had imagined. Wonder was not a rare feeling then; it was ordinary, almost a matter of routine. The odd consequence is that we have used most of it up. The map is filled in. There are few blank spaces left for a traveller to fill. But the instinct did not die when the blanks ran out. It moved. Today we explore through science, and the unknown lies not over the horizon but inside the atom, among the stars, in the workings of our own bodies. The explorer has put down the compass and picked up the instrument.

This is worth bearing in mind when adults fret about the young. Children and young people have an enormous appetite for adventure stories: shipwrecks, treasure, journeys through wild country, heroes who leave home and do not come back the same. Parents sometimes worry that this taste is unhealthy, that it breeds discontent or recklessness. It should not worry them. It is the same instinct that sent the explorers out, showing itself early, and a child who has never dreamed of running away to sea has probably missed something.

The deeper point is less comfortable. Much of civilisation was built by rule-breakers. The people who crossed the unknown ocean, who questioned the settled opinion, who went where they had been told not to go, were rarely respectable in their own time. Against them, in almost every age, stood the cautious citizens: sensible, law-abiding, and reliably opposed to every bold figure who threatened to disturb the arrangement of things. We honour the bold figures now, but we should notice that we would probably have been among those who opposed them. [example needed]

None of this means that everyone should abandon their responsibilities and set out for the horizon. The cautious citizen has his uses; someone has to stay home and keep the lamps lit. But the wandering instinct is not a flaw in human nature to be corrected. It is one of the engines of it. We should be careful not to crush it in others, and a little slower to be ashamed of it in ourselves.
