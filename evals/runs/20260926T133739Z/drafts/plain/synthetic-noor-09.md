After the first hiss of air, the rest of bleeding a radiator is a matter of watching and waiting. The valve is open just a little, the air escapes, and then the sound changes. First it may sputter, and then water appears at the valve. That is the signal to stop. As soon as water shows, I close the valve again.

Closing it needs the same gentleness as opening it. The valve only has to be shut enough to stop the water; there is no need to force it. Overtightening a small bleed valve is an easy way to damage it, and a damaged valve is a far bigger job than a cold radiator. I turn it back until it is snug, wipe away the drips, and move on to the next radiator, working upward through the house as before.

Bleeding does have one side effect. Every bit of air released lets the pressure in the system fall, so once all the radiators are done, I go to the boiler and check the pressure gauge. Usually it has dropped, and the system needs topping up. This is done through the boiler's filling loop. Too little pressure and the heating will not run well; too much and the system will complain in other ways, so I take my time.
