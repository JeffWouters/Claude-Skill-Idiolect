The last stage of darning a sock is the part I enjoy most. The lengthwise threads are already in place across the hole, and now the crosswise threads go in, weaving over and under, back and forth, until the gap closes into a small patch of fabric.

The one thing to watch at this stage is tension. It is tempting to pull each thread snug so that the weave looks neat and tight, but a patch pulled taut will pucker, and a puckered patch is uncomfortable to wear and pulls on the surrounding fabric. I let each crosswise thread lie easily, with just enough tension to sit flat. The weave should look relaxed rather than stretched.

It is slow work, and that is part of its appeal. I have come to find the time spent darning genuinely restful. There is nothing to decide once the pattern is set, only the steady rhythm of the needle going over and under. It asks for enough attention to keep the mind from wandering off into worry, but not so much that it becomes tiring.

It is also worth doing for practical reasons. A well-darned heel often outlasts the original fabric around it. The patch is denser than the knitting it replaces, and it tends to hold up long after the rest of the sock has started to thin.

Darning does not make a sock last forever. Sooner or later a pair wears through at the ankle, where the fabric is harder to support and repair, and that is usually the end of it. But until then, each mended heel buys another season of wear, and a little quiet time as well.
