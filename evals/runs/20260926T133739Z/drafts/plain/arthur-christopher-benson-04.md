Anyone who has lived in a college town will know the peculiar tyranny of the afternoon call. There is a whole code of it: who must call on whom, and when, and for how long, and how soon the call must be returned. New arrivals must be visited; the visit must be repaid; cards must be left in the right order and the right number. It is all very correct, and it seems to me a great waste.

The people most burdened by it are the very people who can least afford the time. An academic's working life depends on long stretches of concentration, and the afternoon is often the best part of the day for it. To break it off in order to put on a good coat, walk across town, sit for a quarter of an hour in a drawing room exchanging remarks about the weather, and then walk back again, is to lose not just the hour itself but most of the energy that would have gone into the evening's work. Multiply that by a season's worth of obligations and the cost is considerable. [example needed]

It would be so much more sensible to do things differently. Suppose a household simply let it be known that on a certain evening each week they would be at home, and that anyone who cared to drop in would be welcome. No cards, no ledger of visits owed, no offence if you did not come. People would turn up when they felt like it and leave when they had had enough. The talk would be better, because everyone there would actually want to be there, and the afternoons could go back to the work they belong to.

That, really, is the heart of it. Obligation is the enemy of pleasure. The moment a gathering becomes something you must attend, it stops being much fun, both for the guest who has come reluctantly and for the host who can see that he has. I remember a senior politician who, on being told that some social function was compulsory, remarked with some feeling that it could hardly be much of a pleasure then.
