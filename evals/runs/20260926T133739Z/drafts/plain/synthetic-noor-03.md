Somewhere in our house there is a stack of old chore lists, and it has become one of the more revealing things we own. The system behind it is simple. Tasks go on a sheet, and when a task is done it gets struck through with a line rather than erased. When a sheet is full, it is not thrown away. It goes onto the pile with the others, and the pile has quietly grown over the years into an archive.

I did not keep them with any purpose in mind. It just seemed wrong to throw away a page that had so much finished work on it. Only recently did I sit down and read through them, and the experience was stranger and more moving than I expected.

The first thing that stands out is repetition. Some tasks appear on sheet after sheet, not because they were never done but because they always come back: [example needed]. Reading them in sequence, you can feel the rhythm of a household, the same jobs rolling round like seasons.

Then there are the postponed tasks, the ones that were copied from one list to the next without ever being crossed off. You can follow them across months, sometimes longer, moving from sheet to sheet like a guest nobody quite gets round to seeing. Some were finally struck through. Others simply stop appearing, and I honestly cannot tell whether they were done, dropped or forgotten. Those forgotten tasks are oddly touching. Each one was urgent enough to write down once.

But the part that caught me most was the grocery entries. Over the years they trace the family itself. Early lists hold the things you buy when children are small: [example needed]. Later the quantities grow, and the items change as tastes change and appetites increase. Then, sheet by sheet, the lists get shorter again. Certain items disappear. The amounts shrink back. Without anyone writing it down directly, the lists record children growing up and, eventually, leaving home.

I had thought I was keeping a record of chores. It turns out I was keeping a record of us.
