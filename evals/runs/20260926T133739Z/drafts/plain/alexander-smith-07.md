Beside the castle ruin stands a small medieval chapel, in rather worse repair than the castle itself. The roof is patched where it has not fallen, the windows have lost most of their glass, and the stone is softening in the damp like old bread. It is easy to walk past without noticing it at all, and most people do.

Whatever religious life once went on here has long since gone. No services are held, and there is no sign that anyone remembers when the last one was. The ornament has gone too: the painted walls are bare plaster, the carving is broken or worn smooth, and whatever furnishings the chapel once had were taken away long ago. The villagers ignore it entirely. It is not a place they visit, or mention, or seem to think about. It is simply there, like a boulder in a field.

Yet inside, if you push open the door, you find that the chapel is not empty. Aristocratic tombs lie along its floor and walls, the resting places of the family who once held the castle next door. Most of the inscriptions are too worn to read. One tomb, though, has kept its figures. It shows a lord and his lady lying side by side, their hands joined, carved as a devoted couple who meant to be seen together for as long as the stone would last. Whoever made it wanted that devotion remembered more than any title or deed.

I found it more moving than I expected. The chapel's purpose has failed, its beauty has gone, its builders are forgotten. But the virtue the tomb honours, that simple faithfulness between two people, does not seem to have faded with the rest. Perhaps such things endure beyond death in a way that ornament and ceremony do not. The carving is crumbling. What it records, I suspect, is not.
