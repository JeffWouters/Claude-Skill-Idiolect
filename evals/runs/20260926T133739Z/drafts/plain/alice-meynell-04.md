In memory, the earliest years of my life stand far apart from one another. Each one is distinct, a separate room with its own light and furniture, and between them lies a great distance. Later years run together; I could not easily tell you what separated one from the next. But the years of early childhood still keep their spacing, as if each had been long enough to be a small lifetime of its own.

It is tempting to explain this by boredom. Children are often bored, and tedious hours do feel long. Anyone who has waited through a dull afternoon knows how time can drag. But the vastness I remember is not the same thing. A tedious hour is long in a narrow, uncomfortable way; it presses on you and you want it to end. The time of early childhood was long in a different way altogether. It had no pressure in it. It simply seemed to have no edge, to go on in every direction without any sense that it would run out.

I think I felt this most clearly when drifting off to sleep. Lying in bed at the end of the day, a child can feel time open up around him, endless and calm, with the night stretching away and the morning unimaginably far off. There is no hurry and no count. Adults rarely notice anything like this. We fall asleep thinking of tomorrow's tasks, or we count the hours we have left before the alarm. The same passage from waking to sleep that once felt like entering boundless time is now just a gap to be got through.

Perhaps that sense of endlessness was an illusion. But it was a real experience, and I suspect it is where most of us first learned what the word forever might mean.
