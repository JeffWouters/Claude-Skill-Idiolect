In a Western mining settlement the art of storytelling is held in high esteem, and it is practised everywhere, from the saloon to the church. The people there have strong views about how a thing ought to be told, and they are not shy of expressing them.

This comes out plainly in their taste in preaching. They want a man who stands up and talks to them. A preacher who reads his sermon from notes, however learned the notes may be, is at a great disadvantage; the congregation feels that it is being read at rather than spoken to. The man they admire is the one who throws his notes away, or never had any, and pours out his sermon with life and heat. They will forgive him a great deal of bad grammar for the sake of a lively delivery.

The same instinct governs their gossip, which is of a peculiar kind. A miner describing a neighbor tells you exactly what he thinks of him, and he does it without the small spiteful qualifications that season gossip in more polished places. He does not say that a man is honest, but. He says the man is honest, and then, in the next breath and in the same tone, he tells you something else about him that is not honest at all.

The effect is that good and bad traits are set down side by side, like goods on a counter, and neither is allowed to cancel the other. The speaker passes no judgment. He is simply giving an inventory. A man may be described as the kindest fellow in the camp, a devoted nurse to anyone who falls sick, and also the best hand at marking a deck of cards, all in one sentence and with equal approval. The result is often very funny, though the speaker never intends it to be. He is not trying to amuse you. He is only telling the truth as he sees it, and the truth, laid out so candidly, turns out to be a comedy.
