I once spent an afternoon with a man who had been a forger for many years and had since given it up. He was entirely frank about his former trade and seemed to take a certain pleasure in explaining it, much as a retired sea captain will explain the finer points of navigation. What interested me most was not his methods but his morals, for it turned out that he had a very definite code and was prepared to defend it.

The first thing I learned was that he despised thieves. I do not mean great thieves, of whom he said little, but the petty kind, the pickpocket and the sneak who lifts a coat from a hallway. He spoke of them with real contempt. They were, he said, a low sort of people, without skill or courage, who took what was not theirs simply because it was there. He would not have sat at a table with one.

I made the mistake of suggesting that forgery was, after all, a kind of theft. He was genuinely offended. He put down his cup and looked at me as a gentleman might look at someone who had confused him with his coachman. There was no comparison, he told me. A thief took; a forger earned.

His view, as he went on to explain it, was that forgery was a contest. On one side stood the forger, with his skill, his patience and his nerve. On the other stood the detectives, men paid by the banks for the express purpose of catching him. It was a match of wits between professionals, fairly entered on both sides. If he won, he had beaten trained men at their own game; if he lost, he went to prison and bore no grudge. The thief entered no contest at all.

I came away uncertain whether I had been talking to a scoundrel or a sportsman. I suspect he would have said there was no difference.
