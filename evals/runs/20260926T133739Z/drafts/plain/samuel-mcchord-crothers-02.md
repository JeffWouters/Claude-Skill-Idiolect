In a mining town, luck is a public affair. When a prospector who has scratched at the hills for years finally breaks into rich ore, the news runs down the gulch faster than he can, and by the time he reaches the saloon the whole camp feels it has a share in his good fortune. So it was when our lucky man struck it and, in the same breath, announced that he meant to marry.

He was not the sort to marry quietly. He sent word that everyone was invited, every soul in the district, and that he would pay for all the transport himself. Wagons, stages, horses and whatever else could be pressed into service were hired on his account, and the town emptied itself onto the road toward the wedding.

It was a procession worth seeing. Officials rode beside gamblers, and the clergy found themselves sharing a seat with laborers who had not seen the inside of a church in years. Nobody minded. A reader of Chaucer would have recognized the company at once: here were his pilgrims again, the respectable and the disreputable jolted along together, each with a tale to tell and each rather enjoying the others. The talk on that road would have filled a book, and very little of it would have passed the censor.

What struck me most was the good nature of the whole affair. A mining town has a curious habit of taking turns with its citizens. One week it is the man who found the lead; the next it is the fellow who lost his claim at cards, or the preacher who gave a rousing sermon, or the woman who kept the boarding house through the hard winter. Each is famous for a little while, talked about in every cabin, and then gently set down so that someone else may have the platform. On this occasion it was the prospector's turn, and the town gave it to him with both hands.
