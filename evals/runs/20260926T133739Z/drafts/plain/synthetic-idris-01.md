Every few months someone suggests scrapping the standing team meeting. The argument is familiar: the meeting runs long, half the updates could have been a message, and people leave with less time than they arrived with. It is a fair complaint. Most of us have sat through a weekly meeting that existed mainly because it was on the calendar.

Still, I think scrapping them outright is the wrong answer, because the value of a standing meeting is rarely in its agenda. It is in the fact that the same people are in the same room, or on the same call, at a predictable time. That regular contact keeps a team connected in ways that are hard to see until they are gone. It is also where minor tensions show early. A clipped reply, a pause before someone agrees, a colleague who has gone quiet for two weeks running: these are small signals, and they tend to surface when people talk to each other, not when they post status updates into a channel. Catch them early and they stay small. Miss them and they grow.

Cut every standing meeting and you may win back some hours, but you also risk a team that feels more isolated. People stop bumping into each other's work. Questions that would have taken thirty seconds in the room turn into threads nobody quite owns. The quiet ones get quieter. None of that shows up in a calendar audit, which is exactly why it is easy to underestimate.

The better move is to make each standing meeting justify itself at intervals. Every so often, ask plainly: what is this meeting for, is it still doing that, and does it need this much time and this many people? Be willing to act on the answer. In my experience, many meetings shrink when you do this. An hour becomes thirty minutes, a large group becomes the few who actually need to be there. Some end altogether, and that is fine too, because they had stopped earning their place.

What you keep after that review is a set of meetings that exist on purpose. That is a far better outcome than keeping everything out of habit, or deleting everything out of frustration.
