Each of us carries a private antiquity, and it is not found in any history book. It is our own childhood. When I reach back to the first years of my life, I find something that feels older than any ancient figure I have read about. The kings and philosophers of the distant past seem, by comparison, oddly recent. They are people I learned about; my early childhood is a place I came from, and it lies at a depth that no date can match.

This is not only a trick of personal memory. I suspect it is also the reason early history seems so remote to us in the first place. We learn it as children. The oldest civilisations, the first empires, the founding stories of our own country: all of these usually reach us when we are very young, at the same time as our earliest memories are being laid down. They are filed in the same deep drawer. So the ancient world takes on the colour of childhood itself, that sense of immense distance and dim, golden light. We think of these periods as far away partly because we first met them when everything was far away.

What interests me is that this enlarged sense of time survives. As an adult I know perfectly well that history is shorter than it once seemed. I can count the generations, and I know that a few centuries is not so very long. My adult years pass quickly, and when I measure history against them it shrinks. Yet the old feeling does not disappear. Somewhere beneath the arithmetic, the far past still feels majestic and remote, because it is still connected to the part of me that first imagined it.

I think this is worth holding on to. The knowledge that history is short is useful, but it is not the whole truth of how we experience it. Our childhood gave us a measure of deep time that adult life cannot quite replace, and every time we look back at the ancient world we are also, without meaning to, looking back at ourselves.
