The arguments against war are very good ones. They have been made for centuries by wise and humane people, and I have never heard a sound reply to most of them. War wastes wealth, destroys the young, rewards cruelty and settles nothing that could not have been settled more cheaply by other means. Every sensible person agrees. And yet the wars go on. It is worth asking why an argument that wins every debate should lose every practical contest.

Part of the answer, I think, can be seen by looking round the room at any meeting held in the cause of peace. The audience is respectable, thoughtful and earnest. It is also, for the most part, no longer young. The grey heads are there in numbers; the young men are not. They are somewhere else, and whatever they are doing, they are not listening to the case for arbitration.

This is not an accident. The case against war is an argument addressed to experience, to the kind of judgment that comes from having lived long enough to count costs. It persuades those who have already been persuaded by life. But wars are not fought by those people. They are fought by the young, who have not counted any costs yet and who find in the prospect of fighting something that no lecture on waste can answer.

One is tempted to a fanciful conclusion. If a society could somehow be kept permanently middle-aged, with nobody under forty and nobody over sixty, it might never go to war at all. Its citizens would be too prudent, too tired and too well acquainted with bills. Such a society cannot be arranged, and perhaps we should not want it if it could.

The difficulty is very old. Scripture tells of a king who, on coming to the throne, was given counsel by the elders who had served his father. They advised moderation. He turned from them to the young men he had grown up with, who advised him to be harsh, and he took their advice. The kingdom split, and civil war followed. The elders had the better argument. The young men had the king's ear. That, in small, is the whole history of the peace movement.
