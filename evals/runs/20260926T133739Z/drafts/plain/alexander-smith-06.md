Shakespeare's plays are full of drifters: people who have slipped, by accident or choice, out of the ordinary machinery of getting and spending, and who seem, for a while at least, none the worse for it. They wander in forests, wash up on islands, and talk a great deal about how much better life would be if only the world were arranged differently. They are some of the most charming people he wrote, and it is worth asking what he meant by them.

In The Tempest a courtier, shipwrecked on a strange island, amuses himself by imagining how he would govern it. His ideal commonwealth would have no trade, no money, no magistrates, no letters, no labour. Nature would bring forth everything of its own accord, and the people would be innocent and idle together. It is an absurd scheme, and the play does not pretend otherwise. But the dream is lovely all the same, and he speaks it with such evident pleasure that it is hard not to share it for a moment.

The Arden forest community goes further, because there we are not merely told about the dream but shown it. A little society of exiles lives among the trees, having fled or been driven from a court full of envy and intrigue. They hunt, they sing, they sit about and moralise. They have no money worth speaking of and no work beyond what the day requires. And they seem, by any measure the play offers, wiser and happier than the people they left behind. Hardship, in the forest, seems to lose its sting. Against the real society of the court, with its jealousy and its plotting, the forest looks like the better way to live.

It is very tempting to take these idylls at their word, to believe that Shakespeare is telling us that the carefree life is the true one, and that we have simply lost our way by building towns and counting-houses. I do not think he is. Such idylls are only a poet's fantasy, and he seems to have known it perfectly well. The courtier's commonwealth is a way of passing time on a strange shore, not a plan anyone intends to carry out. The forest life, for all its charm, is a refuge rather than a destination. That is not a criticism. We need such places. The drifters give us a holiday from our own responsibilities, and we come back from them a little refreshed. But we should not mistake the holiday for a home.
