Reporting progress on finished work is easy. You say it is done. Reporting progress on unfinished work is much harder, and most of the formats we use for it make that harder still.

Take the familiar colour codes: green, amber, red. They are tidy, quick to read, and easy to roll up into a dashboard. They also flatten a messy reality into a single state. Unfinished work is rarely just on track or off track. It is partly understood, partly blocked, partly waiting on a decision someone else has not made yet. A colour cannot hold any of that.

It helps to see these reports as translations. The person doing the work has one picture of it, full of detail and doubt. The person reading the report needs another, short enough to act on. Something is lost in between, and what gets lost tells you a lot about the relationship between worker and manager. What the worker chooses to leave out, and what the manager chooses not to ask, reveal who carries the risk of bad news.

This is why colours tend to shift too late. A project often stays green long after the people closest to it have started to worry. Those worries are usually vague at first: a feeling that an estimate was optimistic, a dependency that looks shaky. Flagging a vague worry carries costs. You might be wrong, you might look anxious, and you might invite scrutiny you do not want. So people wait until the worry is concrete, and by then the colour jumps straight from green to red.

Better reporting starts with admitting that unfinished work is uncertain, and giving people a way to say so without penalty. Until then, the colours will keep telling us what we want to hear, a little later than we needed to hear it.
