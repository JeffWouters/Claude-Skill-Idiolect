It seems to me a plain principle, though it is often forgotten, that everyone has the right to choose their own friends. We do not expect to be told which books to read or which walks to take, and I see no reason why we should be told whose drawing room we must sit in. Society has built up a whole apparatus of calls and cards and return visits which rather assumes the opposite: that once you are introduced to someone, or once they have moved into your street, you are bound to them by a set of obligations you never agreed to.

The calling card is the clearest example. In itself it is a pleasant enough invention. Left at a door, it could simply say: I am here, I should be glad to know you, come and see me if you like. That is how it ought to work, as an invitation that the other person is entirely free to take up or let drop. The trouble begins when it becomes a debt, when leaving one card means another must be returned within a certain time, and a visit paid, and a visit repaid, whether or not either party has the slightest wish to see the other. At that point the card stops being a courtesy and becomes a small tax on everyone's time.

I have noticed something else, too. The people who grumble most about dull social duties are not, as a rule, the busiest people. More often they are the ones with no hobbies or interests of their own. Having nothing in particular to do, they drift into the round of calls because it fills the hours, and then complain that the hours are empty. Those who have something they care about seldom have that problem. They choose their company as they choose everything else, and they are the better company for it.
