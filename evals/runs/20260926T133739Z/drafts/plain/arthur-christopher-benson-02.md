It is a curious thing, and I have never quite got used to it, that some of the cleverest people I know are among the dullest to be with. One assumes that brains and knowledge must make for lively company. A person who has read a great deal and thought hard about it, surely, will have something to offer at the table. Sometimes that is true. But often enough it is the very reverse, and the more a person knows, the more thoroughly they can drain the life out of a room.

The trouble is not the knowledge itself. It is what they do with it. The clever bore does not converse; he lectures. He has no real interest in what anyone else thinks, and if you venture a view of your own he waits, politely or otherwise, for you to finish so that he can carry on where he left off. A question is not an opening to him but an interruption. He mistakes an audience for a companion, and he never seems to notice that the audience has stopped listening some time ago.

I remember a dinner where this was shown to me with unusual clarity. Among the guests was an enthusiast for local history, who evidently knew a great deal about the district and had decided that the rest of us should know it too. The enthusiast fastened on two of the guests and did not let them go. Every attempt to steer the talk elsewhere was met with another story and another detail from the district's past. At one point one of the two saw a chance and began, very quietly, to edge away towards another part of the room. I watched it with some sympathy. But the enthusiast was having none of it. He reached out, drew the poor fellow back by the arm, and resumed exactly where he had stopped, as though nothing had happened. The escape had failed, and both captives spent the rest of the evening where he had put them.

What struck me afterwards was that the man was not stupid, nor, I think, unkind. He simply had no sense that conversation is a shared thing. Everything he said might have been interesting in the right setting, in a book or in answer to a question someone had actually asked. Pressed on unwilling listeners, it became a burden.

I have come to think that good company depends far less on how much a person knows than on how much attention they are willing to give. A person of quite ordinary learning who listens, who is curious about you, who lets the talk go where it wants, will do more for a gathering than any number of experts. Cleverness without that attention is not a gift to others at all. It is only a way of keeping them captive.
