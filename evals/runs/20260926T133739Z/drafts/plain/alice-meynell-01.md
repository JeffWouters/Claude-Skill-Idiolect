There is a habit among biographers that deserves more resistance than it gets: the habit of telling us, in close and lingering detail, how a famous person fell ill and how they died. We have come to treat the final illness as a natural chapter of any life story, as though the reader were owed it along with the birth, the schooling, the marriage and the work. I do not think the reader is owed it at all.

Consider how we would regard the same sickness had the person recovered. If a writer caught a fever at forty, lay in bed for a month and then got up again, no sensible biographer would reconstruct the temperature charts, the sleepless nights and the indignities of the sickroom. We would call that intrusive, and we would be right. A body in distress is a private matter. It does not become less private because the distress happened to be the last one. A fatal sickness is simply a sickness that did not end well, and it belongs to the person who suffered it just as much as a recovered one would.

The case is stronger still when the illness touched the mind. Madness and delirium strip a person of the very thing a biography is meant to honour: their own voice, their judgement, their considered self. To record what someone said while raving, or how they behaved when they no longer knew where they were, is to put on display a figure they would not have recognised and could not have defended. If ordinary illness deserves discretion, the wandering of a failing mind deserves something closer to silence. Whatever was said in that state was not said by the person whose life we are supposedly examining.

Some will answer that deathbed scenes reveal character, that a person's last days show who they really were. I doubt it. A life is revealed in its choices, its work and its long habits, not in the narrow, exhausted hours when choice has mostly been taken away. The deathbed tells us about the disease far more than about the man or woman in the bed. Details of that kind are not real biography; they are medical record dressed up as insight, and they tend to crowd out the parts of a life that were actually lived.

There is one exception, and it is a human one. Those who loved the dying person may be forgiven for dwelling on the end. Grief goes over the last conversations again and again, remembers the final look, the last word, the hour of the change. That is how mourning works, and nobody should begrudge it. But the grieving have a claim that the public does not. What is natural in a widow's memoir or a son's letter becomes something else when a stranger, years later, assembles the same material for general readers.

So I would ask biographers to stop where the life stops being lived and starts merely being endured. A sentence to say that the subject fell ill and died is enough. The rest belongs to the dead, and to those who mourned them.
