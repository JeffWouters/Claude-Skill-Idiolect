The tap had dripped for a long time. I stopped hearing it. Then one night I put a jug under it, just to see. In the morning there was [amount needed] in the jug. That was the end of ignoring it. A drip you can measure is not a small drip any more. It is a bill.

So I started. Not with the tap. With the sink. First I put the plug in. Then I laid a cloth over it. This is the step people skip. Small parts come out of a tap. Screws, a washer, a little clip. They roll. The drain is right there, waiting. A plug and a cloth cost nothing. A part lost down the trap costs an afternoon.

Then the water. There is a shut-off valve under the sink. [detail needed: valve type or location]. I turned it off. Then I opened the tap to let the last of the water out of the pipe. It ran, then spat, then stopped. I left it open. That way I would know straight away if the valve was not holding.

Now the handle. On an old tap this is where the trouble starts. A cap on top hides the screw. I prised it off with a thin blade. Slowly. Old plastic is brittle. Under it the screw was stiff. I held the handle still with one hand and turned the screwdriver with the other.

Then the valve assembly. It sits inside the body of the tap, and it is often tight. This is where you can do real damage. Put a spanner on the valve and just heave, and the whole tap turns with it. Then you are twisting the pipes under the sink. Or cracking the joint at the worktop.
