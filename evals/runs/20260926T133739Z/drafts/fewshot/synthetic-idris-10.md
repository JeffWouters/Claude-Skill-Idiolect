Progress reports occupy a strange position in the life of a team. They are written with some care, read with varying degrees of attention, and relied upon to convey the state of a piece of work to people who are not close enough to see it for themselves. Yet it is worth asking what, exactly, they convey, and whether it corresponds to what the team actually knows.

Most reports, in my experience, are organised around tasks. They list what has been completed since the last report, what is currently under way, and what remains to be done. This has the considerable advantage of being concrete and easily checked. Its disadvantage is less obvious but perhaps more important. A list of completed tasks tells us about activity; it tells us very little about understanding. A team may finish a dozen tasks in a week and learn almost nothing about whether its approach will work, or finish very few while discovering something that changes the whole shape of the project. The report, by its structure, rewards the first and renders the second nearly invisible. Over time this has a subtle effect on priorities, since the work that shows up well in reports is the work that tends to get done, while the slower business of reducing uncertainty is quietly deferred.

What might a better report look like? One possibility would be to include, alongside the familiar list of tasks, some statement of how confident the team is in its current plan, and on what grounds. Another would be to name explicitly the areas that remain unclear — the questions not yet answered, the assumptions not yet tested, the parts of the problem that no one yet fully understands. Such a report would be less tidy than the usual kind. It would also, I think, be considerably more truthful, and far more useful to anyone trying to decide what to do next.

It would be a mistake, however, to imagine that this is chiefly a matter of changing the template. A new format can be adopted in an afternoon; the difficulty lies in what happens when someone uses it honestly. In many organisations, to say that one is uncertain is to invite the suspicion that one is unprepared, and to name an unclear area is to seem to have failed to clarify it. As long as that remains true, people will continue to report what they have done rather than what they know, and no redesign will persuade them otherwise.

The real change, then, is cultural. It depends on coming to regard the admission of uncertainty not as a weakness to be managed but as a form of honesty to be valued — perhaps even as a sign that the person reporting has understood the work well enough to see where its difficulties lie.
