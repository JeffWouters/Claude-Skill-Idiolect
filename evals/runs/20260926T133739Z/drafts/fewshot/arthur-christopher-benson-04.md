There is one custom of our small academic society which I have never been able to regard with any patience, and that is the custom of formal calling. A college town is full of busy people, men who have lectures to prepare and books to write and pupils to see; and their working energy, which is not unlimited, is precious. Yet a great deal of it is dribbled away, afternoon after afternoon, in paying visits of duty; in walking from house to house, sitting for a stated quarter of an hour in drawing-rooms, saying the expected things, and walking away again, with nothing gained on either side. It is a curious waste; and what makes it more curious is that hardly any one, if he were asked, would say that he enjoyed it.

What I should like to see, and what seems to me so obviously sensible that I wonder it has not been tried, is something far easier and more natural. Let there be houses where, on a known evening, people may drop in if they please, and stay as long or as short a time as they like, and go away without ceremony. The work of the day would be done; the mind would be free; and those who came would come because they wished to come, not because a card had been left and must be returned. Such a gathering would have the one quality that the formal call entirely lacks, that of being voluntary.

For that, after all, is the whole secret. The moment a pleasure becomes an obligation it ceases to be a pleasure at all. I remember hearing of a very eminent statesman, not a man given to social diversions, who once said, half in jest, that the only thing which spoilt a party for him was the knowledge that he was obliged to attend it.
