I was staying, some time ago, in the house of a man of considerable public note, [example needed: who he was] and there was another guest there, an old friend of our host's, with whom I found myself alone one evening. Now our host was a very interesting person; there was a great deal in him that puzzled me, a mixture of qualities that did not seem to fit together; and it appeared to me the most natural thing in the world to talk him over, to compare impressions, and to try to arrive at some notion of what he really was. I made a beginning, I remember, with some remark about his manner.

But my companion would have none of it. He said, quite gently but very firmly, that he did not think it right to discuss a friend in his absence, and least of all under his own roof; that it was a matter of loyalty; and that he would rather not go on. I was a little abashed, and a little nettled too; for I had meant no harm, and had intended nothing but a friendly curiosity.

He went further than this, however, as we talked. He held that any criticism of another person, however mild and however just, was a breach of the commandment against bearing false witness; because, as he said, one could never know the whole of another's heart, and so whatever one said was bound to be in some degree untrue.

I have thought of this a good deal since, and I cannot altogether agree with him. It seems to me that to speak of a friend with affection and fairness, and to try to understand him, is not disloyal at all; it is one of the ways in which friendship shows itself. And if every judgment of another is to be counted false witness, then we must give up judging altogether, which is impossible. Yet I respect the scruple; and I confess that it made me look a little more carefully at my own motives.
