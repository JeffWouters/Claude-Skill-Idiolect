I once had a long conversation with a man who had been a forger and had reformed. He had put his old profession behind him, but not his old opinions, and it was these that interested me. For I discovered that he had lived all along by a moral code, and a very strict one. It was not the code of the community, but it had the merit of being his own.

The first article of it was a thorough contempt for petty thieves. He spoke of them as a respectable citizen might speak of the idle poor. They were mean fellows, without skill and without self-respect, who took what lay within reach and ran away with it. There was nothing in their work that called for intelligence. He could not understand how any man could sink so low.

I ventured, with some hesitation, to suggest that there was a certain resemblance between forgery and theft. In both cases, I said, something passed from the pocket of one man into the pocket of another without the consent of the first. He was deeply offended. It was plain that I had touched him on a tender point. To compare the two, he said, showed a want of discrimination which he had not expected of me.

He then explained the matter as he saw it. Forgery was a game, and a game of a high order. On one side was the forger, working with his own brains and his own hands. On the other side were the detectives, who were paid to catch him. It was a fair contest of wits. The detectives had the resources of the law and a salary besides; the forger had only his skill. If he won, it was because he was the better player. If he lost, he paid the forfeit like a sportsman.

I found it impossible to argue with him. He had the advantage of me, for he had thought the matter out, and I had only accepted what I had been told.
