The new washer cost almost nothing. [price needed]. I got it from the hardware shop [location needed]. I took the old one with me. That is the only way to be sure. There are a lot of sizes and they look alike. Match it by eye, in the shop, old next to new.

Then home, and back to the tap. The old washer came off the valve. The new one went on. Check it sits flat. Check it is the right way round. It is a small part. It is the whole fix.

Reassembly is the taking apart, backwards. It sounds simple. It is where people rush. The valve assembly goes back into the body. By hand first. Get the thread started straight before you touch a spanner. If it goes in crooked, you damage the thread. Then the spanner. Brace the body again, same as before. Snug, not forced. The washer does the sealing. You do not need to crush it.

Then the handle. Then the screw. Then the cap.

Now the water. Slowly. Open the shut-off valve a little. Wait. Listen. The pipe fills. Then open it a little more. Then all the way. Watch the joints under the sink the whole time. Run a dry finger along them. A damp fingertip finds a leak before your eyes do.

Turn the tap on. Air first, spitting. Then water, running clean. Then off. Firmly, but not hard.

Then wait. That is the test.

The fix itself took [time needed]. The preparation took longer. Plug in, cloth down, water off, the right tools found, the stiff valve out without twisting the pipes. The actual change of washer is the shortest step of the lot. Most repairs are like that. The job is small. Getting to it is the work.

And then the reward. Nothing. No drip. I stood in the kitchen that night and listened. Silence. [detail needed]. I had not noticed how loud the tap was until it stopped.
