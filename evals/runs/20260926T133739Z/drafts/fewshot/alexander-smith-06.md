Shakespeare, who knew every kind of man, knew the drifter well, and loved him.  His plays are full of people who have slipped out of the harness of the world--banished men, idle courtiers, fools in motley, lovers who have run away into the woods--and who seem, for the space of a few scenes, to have found there something that the harness never gave them.  They are the carefree drifters of literature, and one cannot read of them without a little pang of envy.

In The Tempest an old courtier, cast away upon the enchanted island, falls to dreaming aloud of how he would govern it if it were his.  There should be no traffic in his commonwealth, no money, no riches and no poverty; no labour, no magistrates, no letters; nature should bring forth of her own accord all that men required, and the people should live innocent and idle.  His companions laugh at him, and not without reason, for the scheme has holes in it through which a coach might be driven.  And yet the dream is a pleasant one, and there is hardly a man in the audience who has not, on some wet Monday morning, dreamed it too.

The forest of Arden is the same dream made flesh.  There a little community lives under the greenwood tree, reads its lessons in the running brooks and the changing leaves, and appears, one and all, a great deal wiser and happier than the court they have left behind.  Nobody in Arden is in a hurry.  Nobody is ambitious, or if he is, the forest cures him of it.  They idle a little, and talk a great deal, and moralise at leisure upon the follies of the world they have escaped; and the world, seen from under the oaks, looks very foolish indeed.  Real society, with its envies and its intrigues, seems a poor thing beside this company of cheerful exiles.

But we must not be deceived.  These idylls are the fantasy of a poet, and the poet knew it better than anyone.  The island where no man labours is an island in the sea of dreams; Arden has no winter that cannot be sung away, and no rent-day at all.  Were we to transport ourselves there in the body, we should find the greenwood damp, the dinner uncertain, and the lessons of the brooks remarkably tedious after the first week.  The drifters are delightful because they drift across a stage, and the play ends before the bill comes in.  Shakespeare lets us wander with them for an afternoon, and then sends us home--a little refreshed, a little rebuked, and not at all inclined to throw up our situations.  That is the proper use of an idyll.  It is a holiday for the imagination, and a holiday is only sweet because it ends.
