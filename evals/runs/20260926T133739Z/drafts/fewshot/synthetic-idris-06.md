It is worth looking at the recurring entries in a team's calendar with something of an anthropologist's eye. Seen from that angle, many of them are less like meetings than like rituals: gatherings that take place at a fixed time, in a fixed form, attended faithfully by people who could not always say with any precision what the gathering is for. The form persists, and so does the loyalty of those who attend, long after the reason for the whole arrangement has faded from memory.

This is not because such meetings were foolish to begin with. On the contrary, most of them began as sensible responses to a genuine difficulty. Something kept going wrong that no one could fix alone, and a regular gathering was the obvious remedy [example needed]. In each case the meeting solved a real problem, and it solved a problem that was shared, which is precisely why everyone was willing to come.

What tends to happen next is rather quieter. The problem is solved, or it changes shape, or the people who felt it most keenly move on to other work. The meeting, however, remains in the calendar, because calendars are good at remembering and poor at forgetting. It continues to recur, and people continue to attend, partly out of habit and partly out of a vague sense that absence might be noticed and misread.

I suspect the most striking thing about this process is that no one really sees it happen. There is no moment at which a meeting announces that it has stopped being useful; its usefulness simply drains away, gradually and without ceremony, while its form stays intact. Perhaps the most we can do is to keep asking, now and again, what problem a given ritual was meant to solve — and whether that problem is still with us.
