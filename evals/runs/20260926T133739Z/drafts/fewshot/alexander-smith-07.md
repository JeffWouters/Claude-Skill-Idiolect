Hard by the castle ruin, and sharing its decay, stands the old chapel.  It is a small place, and a sad one.  Whatever life of worship it once held has long since departed; the altar is gone, the images are gone, the painted glass is gone, and the carved work that once made it fair has crumbled away, or been carried off to mend a pigsty.  The wind comes in where the windows were, and the ivy follows the wind.  The villagers pass it every day and never look at it.  To them it is a heap of old stones, a little less useful than a barn; and I do not know that I can blame them, for a thing that has ceased to serve is soon ceased to be seen.

And yet the chapel is not empty.  Beneath its broken floor, and along its mouldering walls, lie the great folk of the castle--lords and ladies who in their day could have bought the whole village and never missed the price.  Their tombs remain, though the hands that tended them are dust.  On one of these a lord and his lady lie side by side in stone, their hands joined, their faces turned a little toward each other, as if even in death they were loth to part.  The sculptor, whoever he was, has made them a devoted couple; and I like to believe he did not lie.

Standing there of an evening, I have sometimes wondered what, of all that was honoured in this place, is left.  The pomp is gone, the ritual is gone, the very names are half effaced.  But the virtues that the stone was set up to commemorate--faithfulness, and constancy, and love that holds on to the end--these are not of the stuff that crumbles.  The chapel may fall, and the tomb with it; but it may be that what those two folded hands were meant to say outlasts them both, and is still saying it somewhere beyond the reach of the weather.
