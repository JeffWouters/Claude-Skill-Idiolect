It seems to me a very simple principle, and yet one that is constantly forgotten, that every one has a right to choose his own friends. We do not permit any one to dictate to us what books we shall read, or what food we shall eat, or how we shall spend our holidays; and yet in the matter of our acquaintance, which touches our happiness far more nearly, we submit to a whole code of rules which were made by nobody in particular and suit nobody at all.

Take the matter of cards. A card left at a house ought, I think, to mean no more than this: that the person who leaves it would be glad to know the household better, if the household is so inclined. It should be an invitation, and a perfectly optional one, which may be taken up or left alone without offence on either side. But what it has come to mean is something quite different; it has become a debt, which must be paid in the same coin within a certain time, and the failure to pay it is regarded as a kind of social dishonesty. So people go on exchanging cards and calls with others for whom they care nothing, simply because the machinery, once started, cannot be stopped.

And yet I have observed one thing which makes me a little less sorry for the sufferers than I might be. It is, as a rule, those who grumble most loudly about the tedium of social duties who have the fewest interests of their own. The man who has a pursuit that he loves, a hobby, a study, a craft, is seldom much troubled by these things; he finds time for what he cares about, and lets the rest go. It is the person with an empty day who finds the calls so wearisome, and who yet cannot bring himself to give them up.
