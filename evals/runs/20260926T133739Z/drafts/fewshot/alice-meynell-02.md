The child makes antiquity.  He is not taught it; he invents it, out of the great length of his own days, and lays it upon whatever he is told of kings and wars and the building of old walls.  A thing that happened before he was born happened, to him, in an immeasurable before.  There is no difference, in his sense of it, between the last generation and the tenth.  All is equally far, because all is beyond the edge of a year that is itself enormous.

But the man measures otherwise.  He measures, whether he will or no, by the years he now lives, and they are short.  They are short not in their count but in their feeling; they pass with a haste that the child never knew.  And having shortened his own years, he finds--to his surprise, and a little to his loss--that he has shortened history as well.  The centuries that once stood so far off have drawn near.  A hundred years is but a few of his own swift spans laid end to end.  He could almost reach back and touch the hand that wrote a letter he once thought incredibly ancient.  It is not that he has learnt anything new of the past.  It is that the rule by which he measures has grown shorter in his hand.

There is a further change, and a subtler.  The grown man has seen things alter.  He has watched streets rebuilt, customs dropped, fashions of speech come and go.  And because he watched them, they seemed to him ordinary.  Nothing marvellous attended them; they happened on weekdays, among other business, without portent.  It is not possible to have seen change so plainly without learning that change is, for the most part, plain.  So he looks back upon the great alterations of the past and suspects that they, too, were done on weekdays.  What was once a mystery--how the old world became the new--is now only the familiar process, of which he has been a witness, carried a little further back.

Who but loses something by this knowledge?  The child's past was vast and strange; the man's is near and reasonable.  He has gained accuracy and given up awe.  Almost--not quite; for there remains in him, beneath the adult arithmetic, some faint memory of that first immensity.
