Anyone who has been asked to report on the progress of unfinished work will know the peculiar difficulty of the task. The work itself is untidy: partly done, partly understood, shadowed by problems that may or may not turn out to matter. The report, by contrast, asks for something clean — a percentage, a set of milestones, or most often a colour, green or amber or red, chosen to summarise the whole state of affairs in a single glance. Something is inevitably lost in the passage from the one to the other.

It may be useful to think of such reports as translations. They carry meaning from the language of those doing the work into the language of those responsible for overseeing it, and like all translations they reveal something about the relationship between the two parties. What the worker chooses to emphasise, what the manager expects to hear, which uncertainties are mentioned and which are quietly left out — all of this reflects the dynamics of trust and power between them as much as it reflects the state of the project.

One consequence is visible in almost every organisation that uses colour-coded status. The colours tend to change too late. A project remains green for week after week and then turns red quite suddenly, as though the trouble had appeared overnight. It rarely has. What has usually happened is that the people closest to the work felt a growing unease for some time, but the unease was vague, difficult to justify, and costly to raise. To mark a project amber on the strength of a feeling is to invite questions one cannot yet answer, and perhaps to be thought alarmist if the feeling proves unfounded. It is easier to wait until the worry has become a fact. By then, of course, the report has stopped being useful for the one thing it was meant to do.
