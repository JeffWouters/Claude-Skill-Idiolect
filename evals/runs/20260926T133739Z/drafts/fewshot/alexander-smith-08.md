The queen, wronged and cast upon the waters through no fault of her own, was carried many days upon the sea with her little son in her arms.  She had no helm and no oar; she had only her patience, and her trust in a hand stronger than her own.  Nor was that trust misplaced.  It chanced that a Roman commander, returning homeward with his ships, came upon the frail vessel tossing in the waves, and drew near, and found in it a woman and a child.  He did not know who she was, and she did not tell him.  He saw only that she was fair and sorrowful and alone, and, being a man of good heart, he took them both aboard and carried them home.  There the queen lived quietly, unknown and unasking, and the boy grew.

Meanwhile the king her husband had repented of the wrong done her, and his remorse was heavy upon him.  It fell out in time that the commander made a banquet for him, and that the boy was sent in among the guests.  The king looked at the child, and looked again.  There was something in the face that he could not put from him--the face of the wife he had lost, looking out of the eyes of a stranger.  He asked whose the boy was.  And when he had heard what little the commander could tell, he could not keep back his tears, and he wept at the table before them all.

The rest is soon told.  The king sought out the mother, and knew her, and fell at her feet; and she forgave him, as the good forgive, without making a merchandise of it.  But one thing remained.  Her father was the emperor himself, and all this while he had not known that his daughter lived.  She went before him and made herself known; and the old man, who had mourned her as dead, took her once more into his arms.  So the wronged were righted and the lost were found, and all ended happily--as, in the old tales, things were permitted to end.
