The arguments against war are excellent. I have heard them many times, and I have never heard them answered. They show that war is wasteful, that it is cruel, that it settles nothing which might not be settled better in some other way. Yet war goes on, and the arguments go on, and the two do not seem to interfere with each other. It is worth while to ask why.

I have attended a good many peace meetings, and I have noticed one thing about the audience. It is composed very largely of persons who have arrived at years of discretion, and some who have passed well beyond them. They are thoughtful people who listen with approval to what is said, and go home convinced of what they believed when they came. But the young men are not there. They are elsewhere, engaged in pursuits that have more of the element of risk in them. The argument is addressed to those who do not need it, and those who might need it are out of hearing.

This suggests a curious reflection. If a society could be kept permanently middle-aged, it might never fight at all. Its citizens would weigh the costs, count the chances, and conclude that the game was not worth the candle. They would have too much to lose and too little appetite for the losing. The trouble is that no society has yet discovered how to prevent its young men from being young.

Nor is the difficulty a new one. Scripture tells of a king who came to the throne and took counsel as to how he should govern. He first consulted the old men who had stood before his father, and they gave him prudent advice. Then he turned to the young men who had grown up with him, and they gave him advice of a more spirited kind. He followed the young men. The result was civil war and a divided kingdom.

The old men were right, as old men frequently are. But it is the misfortune of good counsel that it is usually given in a tone of voice which the young find it impossible to hear. The peace advocate who would succeed must learn not only what to say, but how to say it to those who are not in the habit of listening to him.
