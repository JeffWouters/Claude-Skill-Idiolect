When the angels sang of peace on earth, they did not say how soon it was to come. It is well that they did not, for the Christian nations would find it hard to explain the delay. After all these centuries they are still at war, and still preparing for more war, and the skeptic is not slow to point out the discrepancy between the promise and the performance.

I would not deny the discrepancy. But I think it may be accounted for without concluding that the whole thing was a fraud. The civilization which the Christian nations have spread over the world was not carried by angels. It was carried by men, and men of a very imperfect sort. They took with them their faith, but they took with them also their greed, their pride and their quick tempers. It would have been surprising if the message had not suffered something in the transmission.

Milton, in one of his poems, has noted the long interval that must pass before prophecy comes true. [quotation needed] The poet did not imagine that the world would be made over in a day. He saw the promise and he saw the distance, and he was not discouraged by either.

It seems to me that this is the reasonable view. The Christian nations are not impostors who profess what they do not believe. They are learners, and slow learners at that. They began very far from the gospel ideals. Their ancestors were warriors who worshipped gods of battle, and the habits of centuries are not unlearned in a generation. What is remarkable is not that they have fallen short, but that they have come as far as they have.

A schoolboy who has not yet mastered his lesson is not to be called a hypocrite because his name is on the roll. He is only a schoolboy. The question to ask of him is not whether he knows everything, but whether he is learning.
