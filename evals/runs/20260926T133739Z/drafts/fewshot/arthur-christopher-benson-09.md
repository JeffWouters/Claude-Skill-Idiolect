It is one of the saddest things in history, I think, to watch what befell the teaching of Christ in the centuries that followed Him. The message itself was of the simplest kind; it was addressed to plain people, it dealt with plain things, love and forgiveness and trust and humility; and it could be understood by a child. But it had hardly begun to spread before it fell into the hands of the philosophers; and the philosophers, who could not bear to leave anything simple, set to work upon it. They defined and distinguished and argued; they built up a great structure of doctrine, each part of which had to be held exactly, on pain of exclusion; and the living word became a system, rigid and elaborate, which only the learned could fully understand.

And then came a worse thing. The faith, which had begun among the poor and the despised, attracted at last the notice of the powerful; and the powerful, finding that they could not crush it, did what the powerful always do: they took it over. They gave it wealth and rank and privilege; they made it part of the machinery of the state; and in so doing they tamed it. What had been a spirit became an institution, with offices and revenues and a hierarchy of its own, and with all the interests that institutions have in keeping things as they are.

One has only to think of the papacy in its grandeur to see how far the drift has carried us. I do not say this in any spirit of bitterness; the men who built the system were, many of them, sincere. But it is impossible not to feel that something precious was lost on the way.
