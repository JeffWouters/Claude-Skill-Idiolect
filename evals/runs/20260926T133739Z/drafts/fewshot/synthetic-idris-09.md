There is a small moment, familiar to anyone who has worked in an engineering team, when someone in a meeting uses a term or refers to a system that you do not understand. The choice that follows is usually made in an instant and almost without thought: to ask, and so reveal the gap in one's knowledge, or to nod along and hope to work it out later. Most of us, most of the time, choose the second. It is worth considering what that habit costs, and what it might take to change it.

The case for asking openly is, on the face of it, rather strong. A question spoken aloud helps not only the person who asks it but also the others in the room who were quietly wondering the same thing, and it often brings to light assumptions that the more knowledgeable members of the team had never realised they were making. A team in which confusion is admitted freely tends, I suspect, to learn considerably faster than one in which it is concealed, simply because its misunderstandings are corrected sooner and more often.

Why, then, is this so difficult? Part of the answer lies in the role that knowledge plays within engineering culture. To know things — to be the person others consult, the one who understands how the old system really works — is a source of standing, and sometimes the principal one. Knowledge functions as a kind of status, and status is not easily put at risk. This is felt most acutely by those who have the most of it. A junior engineer who admits to not understanding something is merely behaving as juniors are expected to behave; a senior engineer who does the same may feel, rightly or wrongly, that something of their authority has been surrendered.

It seems to me that this rests on a misunderstanding of what competence actually is. In a field that changes as quickly as ours, competence cannot be a fixed stock of knowledge acquired once and then defended. It is better understood as a capacity for continuing to learn — for meeting the unfamiliar without anxiety and working through it with others. If we think about the engineers we most admire, it is striking how rarely their distinction lies in knowing everything. More often it lies in a persistent curiosity, and in a readiness to say, without apparent discomfort, that they do not yet understand something and would like to.

Making learning open and normal, then, is less a matter of new practices than of changed example. When the most experienced people in a team are seen to ask their questions aloud, everyone else acquires permission to do the same.
