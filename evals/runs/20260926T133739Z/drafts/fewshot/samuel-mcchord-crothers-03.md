The rascals of fiction are never quite free. However lively they may be, there is always the author standing behind them, and the author has his opinion. He lets the rogue have his way for a chapter or two, but in the end he sits in judgment. The reader knows all the time that the scales are being kept, and that the account will be settled before the last page.

It is quite otherwise with the real rogue who writes his own memoir. There is no one behind him. He is his own author and his own judge, and as judge he is singularly indulgent. The swindler whose book I have been reading never once brings himself to trial. It does not occur to him that there is any case to answer. He is not defending himself, for he is not aware of being accused. He is simply relating the adventures of a virtuous man in a world that did not always appreciate him.

His career would have furnished material for half a dozen ordinary lives. He drifted into soldiering, and out of it. He drifted into preaching, and found the pulpit agreeable for a season. He drifted into forgery, which he seems to have regarded as another branch of the literary profession. He tried teaching, and doubtless taught what he knew. And from time to time he drifted into prison, which he accepted as one of the inconveniences of travel. There is no sign that he ever planned any of these changes. He went where the current took him, and found each landing-place as good as the last.

What is remarkable is that through all this he remained perfectly cheerful. Misfortune did not sour him, and disgrace did not humble him. He had one guide whom he never doubted, and that was his own conscience. Other men's opinions he treated as matters of local custom, interesting but not binding. His conscience, on the other hand, was a faithful companion that never found fault with him.

I confess that I envy him a little. Most of us have consciences of a more critical turn. His was the kind that makes a man happy, whatever it does for his neighbors.
