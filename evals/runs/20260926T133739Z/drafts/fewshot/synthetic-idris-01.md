There is a familiar complaint, voiced most often on a crowded Tuesday afternoon, that the standing team meeting has outlived whatever purpose it once served and ought simply to be abolished. The complaint is understandable, and in many cases it is at least partly justified. Most of us have sat through a weekly gathering in which the agenda was read aloud, a few updates were offered that could as easily have been written down, and everyone returned to their desks with the faint sense that an hour had been spent on nothing in particular. It is tempting, from that vantage point, to conclude that the meeting itself is the problem.

I am not sure that conclusion survives closer inspection. What the standing meeting provides is rarely visible on its agenda. It keeps people in one another's company on a regular basis; it gives colleagues a reason to hear each other's voices even in weeks when nothing much is happening; and, perhaps most usefully, it allows the small frictions of working together to surface while they are still small. A slightly sharp remark, a hesitation before agreeing, a silence where there might have been enthusiasm — these are the early signs of tension, and they are far easier to notice in a room than in a status document.

Removing the meeting outright, then, carries a risk that is easy to underestimate. The time is recovered at once, and the benefit is felt at once; the cost arrives later and more quietly, as a team whose members see one another less and understand one another less, and who may not realise how isolated they have become until something goes wrong between them.

A more careful approach would be to ask each recurring meeting to justify itself at regular intervals, rather than assuming either that it is necessary or that it is not. What is this gathering for? Would anyone miss it if it stopped? Could it be shorter, or less frequent? Many meetings, I suspect, would shrink under that questioning, and some would end altogether. But the ones that remained would be there because someone had chosen them, which is a rather different thing from being there because no one had thought to stop them.
