The writers of our day have much to say of the cruelty of nature.  They will not let us walk in a wood without reminding us that it is a place of slaughter--that the hawk is at the finch, and the stoat at the rabbit, and that every leafy quiet covers a war.  It is their favourite discovery, and they return to it with a certain relish, as though the reader had been too long deceived by the poets.  [example needed]

Well, the war may be there; but where are the dead?  Go into the woods on any day of the year, and search as closely as you will.  You shall hardly find a dying creature, and hardly a body.  A feather here and there, perhaps; a little scattering of fur that tells of something that happened and is over.  But the thing itself--the slow ending, the carcass lying where it fell--is so rare that one remembers each instance for years.  The ground is swept.  The cover keeps its counsel.

And consider that the killed are not the half of it.  For every creature taken by another there must be many more that are not taken at all--that grow old, or sicken, or fail in a cold night, and die of no enemy but time.  The small birds, the mice, the whole unreckoned population of the hedges and the grass: these must die, and by the thousand, and die unkilled.  Where do they go?  Doubtless they creep away; doubtless the earth and its small scavengers are quick.  But the fact remains that an innumerable dying goes on about us and is not seen.  It is not merely that we fail to look.  It is that the woods are so ordered that the looking would be vain.

What, then, of the cruelty?  It is not denied.  But it is strange that the writers who find it everywhere should say nothing of this other, and far larger, secret--the secret of an unexposed and decent death.
