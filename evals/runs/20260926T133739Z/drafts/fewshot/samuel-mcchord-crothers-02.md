In a mining town the fortunes of one man are the property of all. When a prospector who had been poking about the hills with nothing but a mule and a hopeful disposition struck a vein of rich ore, the news did not belong to him alone. It belonged to the camp, and the camp took possession of it at once.

What he did next showed that he understood the obligations of his new position. He decided to marry, and, not content with a private ceremony, he invited everybody. More than that, he paid for the transport of all who would come. It was a stroke of genius. A man may be forgiven his wealth if he will only share the fun of it.

The company that set out for the wedding was such as could be gathered nowhere but on the frontier. There were officials, who for the day forgot to be official; gamblers, who for once had nothing at stake; clergy, who found themselves in unaccustomed company and bore it cheerfully; and laborers, who were the most at ease of all. They travelled together, sharing the road and the jokes of the road. I could not help thinking of Chaucer's pilgrims, who set out for Canterbury with no more in common than the way they were going. Here also the Knight and the Pardoner rode side by side, and nobody thought it strange.

There is a kind of democracy in such a place that is not written in any constitution. It is the democracy of opportunity for fame. In older communities celebrity is a scarce article and is reserved for a few. In the mining camp it is distributed in turn. To-day it is the lucky prospector; to-morrow it will be the man who lost his claim in a spectacular manner; the day after, it may be the cook. The town takes turns at making each resident famous, and nobody is kept waiting very long. It is a generous arrangement, and I have sometimes wished that older societies would adopt it.
