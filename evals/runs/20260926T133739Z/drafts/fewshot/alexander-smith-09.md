The religious life of the village runs, for the most part, in a narrow and a plain channel.  Most of the people follow the chapel--a bare, square, whitewashed building, as innocent of ornament as a barn, where they meet to worship in the manner their fathers taught them.  There is no organ, no painted window, no vestment, nothing to please the eye or beguile the ear.  The worship is as bare as the walls.  They sing their hymns lustily, and a little out of tune; they pray at length, and with a fervour that would put many a cathedral to shame.  Whatever else may be said of them, they are in earnest.

I go myself, now and then--more, I fear, from habit than from any warmth of conviction.  I sit in a corner pew and listen, or seem to listen; but I confess that the sermon has seldom the whole of me.  Before the preacher is well into his second head my thoughts have slipped away through the open window, and are wandering in the lanes, or lying on the hillside, or following a bee across the meadow.  I come back with a start at the amen, and hope that no one has observed my absence.  It is not that the preaching is bad; it is only that my mind, like a restless dog, will not stay where it is put.

But the chapel I like best is the chapel empty.  Of a weekday afternoon, when the door stands open and there is no one within, I sometimes step inside and sit down.  The bareness that seemed so chill on the Sunday is restful then.  The sunlight lies in long squares upon the floor; a sparrow chirps in the porch; the silence is of the kind that one can hear.  A full chapel speaks to me in the preacher's voice, and I do not always follow him.  An empty chapel speaks in a voice of its own, and I have never yet failed to understand it.
