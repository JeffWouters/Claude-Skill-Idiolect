I have never been able to believe that God desires to be appeased by offerings. The old notion that He must be placated, that His anger must be turned aside by some gift or some suffering laid upon an altar, seems to me to belong to a very dark and fearful stage of human thought; and I do not think that we mend it much by refining the offerings, and putting forms and ceremonies where once there were bullocks and incense. God is not, I am sure, a being who is pleased by our going without things merely for the sake of going without them.

And yet I do not wish to banish the idea of sacrifice altogether; for there is a true sacrifice, and it is a very noble thing. It is simply the giving up of our own selfishness, our ease and our comforts, for the sake of some end that is worth the cost; the laying aside of what we should like for the sake of what is good. That is a sacrifice which has a meaning, because something real is gained by it; and it is the only kind, I think, that can be acceptable.

Faith, as I understand it, is not the holding of certain opinions, but an inward thing; a quiet and patient bringing of one's own will into line with the will of God. It means accepting what comes, even when what comes is pain, not with a sullen submission, but with a kind of trust; believing that there is a purpose in it, though one cannot see the purpose, and that one is not forgotten.

And worship, if this be so, is a far wider thing than we commonly suppose. Whatever makes us thankful, whatever calms and quiets the restless heart, whatever makes us humble and simple and content to be small, is worship; it may come in a church, but it may come just as truly in a garden, or over a book, or in the company of a friend.
