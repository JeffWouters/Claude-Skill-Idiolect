I hold, as a first principle of social life, that every one has a perfect right to choose his own friends, and to make his own circle as wide or as narrow as he pleases. It seems a simple enough proposition; and yet the whole machinery of visiting and card-leaving appears to be designed, so to speak, to deny it, and to bind us to a round of acquaintance which we did not choose and cannot easily escape.

Take the humble visiting card. What ought it to be? I think it ought to be an invitation, and nothing more; a little signal that one would be glad of further acquaintance, which the receiver may take up or let fall exactly as he likes. But it has become, in too many households, a kind of summons, a debt which must be repaid within a fixed time, on pain of giving offence; and so the cards go to and fro, like shuttles in a loom, weaving a web of obligation in which nobody is particularly happy and from which nobody dares to break loose.

Of course one would not wish to be ungracious, and there is a certain small courtesy in these forms which it would be churlish to despise. But the remedy is not a revolution; it is simply a little honesty, a willingness to let the card mean what it says, and to leave people free to respond or not.

It is a curious thing, and I have perhaps been slow to notice it, that the people who grumble most loudly about the tedium of social duties are very often those who have no hobbies or interests of their own. They complain of their visits; but they would be at a loss, I suspect, if the visits ceased, for they have nothing with which to fill the empty afternoons. The man with a garden, a book, a craft or a pursuit is seldom oppressed by his acquaintances; he simply finds that he has no time for them, and they, like a tide on a quiet shore, slowly withdraw.
