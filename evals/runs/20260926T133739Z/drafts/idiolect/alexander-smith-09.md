It is generally believed that a man goes to church to be made better, and I would not dispute it; but I suspect that a good many of us go chiefly because we went last Sunday, and the Sunday before that. In our small village the greater part of the people follow the nonconformist chapel, a plain, square, unornamented building, as bare within as a barn swept for the harvest. There is no painted window there, no carved screen, no music but the voices of the congregation; the worship is as plain as the walls, and the worshippers are proud of its plainness, as a poor man is occasionally proud of his poverty. I attend with the rest, from habit and not from conviction, and I sit in my accustomed place and look attentive. Yet my attention, I confess, is a deceiver. The preacher begins his discourse, and before he has fairly reached his second head I am far away--in the fields, on the lake, among my books, anywhere but in the theological argument. The one part of me sits upright in the pew; the other has slipped out at the door and gone wandering. [example needed: a particular daydream during a sermon] Is it wicked to dream in chapel? I hope not. The mind that is too tired to follow a sermon may yet be quiet enough to receive a blessing. And, to tell the truth, I like the chapel best when there is nobody in it. On a week-day, when the door stands open and the sun lies across the empty benches, the place has a stillness which no congregation, however devout, can communicate to it; the dust floats in the light, a bird chirps in the eaves, and Silence, like a patient minister, preaches the only sermon I have never slept through. A full chapel speaks to the ear; an empty one speaks to the soul. Of the two, I verily believe, the empty one has done me the more good!
