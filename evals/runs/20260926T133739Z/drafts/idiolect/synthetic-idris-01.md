What is a standing team meeting actually for? The question is asked more frequently now, usually by someone looking at a crowded calendar and wondering which of its fixtures could be quietly removed without anyone noticing, and the standing meeting is the obvious candidate. It may be that the case against such meetings is weaker than it looks, because what they do is rarely written down in any agenda and is therefore easy to overlook. I suspect they matter more than we admit.

Consider what happens in a meeting whose ostensible business could have been settled by a short message. People arrive a few minutes early and talk about something unrelated; someone mentions, almost in passing, that a piece of work is taking longer than expected; someone else notices a slight edge in a colleague's voice and makes a point of asking about it afterwards. None of this appears in the minutes, and none of it would survive being moved into a written update that each person reads alone at a different hour. It is, in some sense, the connective tissue of a team, and the standing meeting is one of the few places where it is regularly exercised, so that minor tensions show themselves early, while they are still minor, rather than surfacing months later as something considerably harder to address.

There is also a quieter risk in scrapping such meetings outright, which is that a team meeting only when there is a stated reason to meet may become more efficient on paper while arguably becoming more isolated in practice, with each person left to infer the mood of the group from fragments. Is it any wonder that people in such teams report feeling less connected, even when the work itself is going well?

It seems to me that the answer is not abolition but periodic scrutiny, in which each standing meeting is asked, at intervals, to justify itself; its purpose restated, its length reconsidered, its attendance reviewed. Many will shrink under this kind of attention, and some will end, which is as it should be. Perhaps the trouble is not that we have too many meetings but that we have stopped asking what each of them is for.
