Why do newer estimation methods so rarely improve the way a team plans? It may be that the method was never the problem, and that each new scheme simply gives an old habit a fresh vocabulary. I suspect the habit is the thing worth examining.

Consider what usually happens when a team abandons hours for points, or points for sizes, or sizes for some other unit that promises to be more honest. The unit changes, the ceremony around it changes a little, and yet the figures produced are still treated, within a few weeks, as though they were dependable measurements rather than informed guesses. The team has swapped its units; it has kept its pretence; it has, arguably, gained nothing except a new set of words in which to be disappointed.

It seems to me that an estimate should be understood as a statement of what we currently know, no more and no less, and that it ought to be revised freely as that knowledge changes, without anyone treating the revision as a small admission of failure. There is also a useful distinction to be drawn between work that is familiar, where past experience genuinely offers some guidance, and work that is truly new, where any figure is in some sense a hope wearing the clothes of a calculation. Treating the two alike tends to make the first look less reliable than it is and the second considerably more reliable than it could ever be.

The real barrier, in the end, is not technical. It is an attachment to exact figures, and to the comfort they offer those who must plan around them. Perhaps what teams need is not a better unit but a greater willingness to say how little they yet know.
