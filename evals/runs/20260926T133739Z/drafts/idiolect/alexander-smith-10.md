A pilgrimage, in the ordinary way of thinking, is a religious exercise; to Chaucer it was something more useful to a poet--an occasion on which every rank of society might meet upon equal terms. The gentleman and the labourer, the churchman and the layman, the scholar and the tradesman, all ride the same road, eat at the same table, and listen to the same stories; and the little company, gathered together by chance, becomes in his hands a miniature of the whole nation. Nowhere else in our older literature do we see so many kinds of men and women together, and nowhere else are they so thoroughly alive. The Prologue in which he introduces them is, I think, his finest achievement. Other poets have told better tales; no poet has ever drawn a gallery of portraits so various, so exact, so crowded with character, in so small a space. He does not describe his travellers at length, for he has no need to. A word about a hood, a line about a horse, a glance at a purse or a pair of shoes, and the whole person stands before you, as a skilful draughtsman, with a few strokes of the pencil, will give you a face you would know again in a crowd. Clothing, in his hands, becomes a kind of involuntary confession. The vain betray their vanity in their finery, the careful their care in their neatness; the one pilgrim is too gay for his calling, the other too shabby for his learning; and the small details, set down with an air of perfect innocence, tell you more of each traveller's nature than a chapter of analysis. It is both, and neither; it is simply the truth, seen by an eye that missed nothing and forgave almost everything. The pilgrims set out, and we go with them gladly; for we have met them all before, in the street and the market, and we are delighted to find that a poet has met them too!
