There is a particular hesitation that settles over a room when someone is about to ask a question and suspects, perhaps rightly, that everyone else already knows the answer. You may have felt it yourself, the brief calculation of whether the question is worth the small loss of standing it might cost, and the quiet decision, more often than not, to look it up later instead. It may be that this hesitation, repeated across a team day after day, does more to slow its learning than any shortage of documentation or training. I suspect it is also one of the easier things to change.

Consider what happens when someone does admit their confusion in public. The question, once asked, turns out to be shared by several others who had been waiting for someone else to ask it; the answer, once given, is heard by everyone at once rather than rediscovered separately by each of them; and the person who asked has shown, in some sense, that asking is permitted. A team in which this happens routinely learns considerably faster than one in which each member struggles privately, not because its people are cleverer but because its knowledge moves more freely.

There is also a reason this is harder than it sounds. In most engineering organisations knowledge functions as a kind of status, arguably the most important kind, and seniority is understood to mean knowing things that others do not. For a senior engineer to say openly that they do not understand something therefore feels like spending a currency they have taken years to accumulate. Is it any wonder that those with the most to lose are often the most reluctant to be seen learning, even when their example would do the most good?

It seems to me that the way out lies in a different understanding of competence, one that treats it not as a store of settled knowledge but as a continuing capacity to learn. This means leaders asking basic questions where others can hear them; it means treating an admission of ignorance as a contribution rather than a confession; it means, perhaps, noticing and saying aloud when someone has learned something new, so that learning becomes something a team recognises rather than something each person hides. None of this requires a programme or a budget.

When you think of the engineers you have most admired in your working life, I suspect what you remember is not how much they knew but how curious they remained. Perhaps that is what seniority ought to mean in the end: not having stopped needing to learn, but never having wanted to.
