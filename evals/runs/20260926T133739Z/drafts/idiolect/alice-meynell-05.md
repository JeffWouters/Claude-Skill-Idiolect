The writers of our day are much occupied with the cruelty of nature. They dwell upon the talon and the tooth, the pursuit and the capture, the perpetual violence of the hedgerow and the wood, and they insist, with a kind of relish, that every green place is a battlefield, and that the song of the thrush is sung over a field of the slain. It is the prevailing fashion, and the fashion has its robustious eloquence. We are not permitted, for a single paragraph, to forget the universal slaughter.

Yet what does the walker actually see? He may go through the woods season after season and hardly once come upon a dying creature, or upon a corpse. The paths are clean, the moss is undisturbed, and the multitudinous life goes about its business, and the death that is said to attend it so closely is nowhere to be found. Where, then, is the battlefield? Where are the slain? If nature were indeed the shambles the authors describe, the evidence would lie thick along every track, and it does not.

And the puzzle deepens when we consider not the killed but the unkilled. For the creatures that fall to the hawk or the weasel are, I suppose, a minority. Countless small animals--the innumerable inhabitants of the undergrowth--must needs die at last of age, of cold, of mere exhaustion, without any enemy whatever. They perish continually, and they perish somewhere within a few yards of the frequented path. Yet they remain unseen, as completely as if they had never lived. Nature, it appears, is not so much cruel as secret; she does not parade her dead, but hides them with a care that the authors who accuse her have never troubled to remark. Perhaps they were too preoccupied with the talon to consider the thicket, while the woods, meanwhile, keep their counsel.
