There is a familiar kind of calendar entry that repeats every week at the same hour, carries a title nobody quite remembers choosing, and draws the same faces into the same room long after anyone can say precisely why. It may be that such meetings are best understood not as meetings at all but as rituals, practices whose form has outlived the reason they were created. I suspect most organisations have more of them than they would care to count.

Consider how they usually begin, because almost always there was a real problem, shared by several people, that could not be solved without regular conversation; a system that kept failing, a handover that kept going wrong, a group of teams whose work kept colliding. The meeting was a sensible response, and for a time it did exactly what it was meant to do, which is arguably why it earned its place in the calendar in the first place.

What happens next is quieter and harder to see, since the problem is solved, or fades, or moves elsewhere, and yet the meeting continues, because nothing in its structure asks whether it should. The agenda becomes a matter of habit; the updates become a matter of form; the attendance, remarkably, remains loyal, since to stop attending would feel like a small act of disloyalty to colleagues who are still turning up. Who among us has not sat through such a meeting and wondered, briefly, whether anyone else was wondering the same thing?

It seems to me that the difficulty is not that these meetings exist but that nobody notices when they stop being useful. There is no moment of failure to mark the change, only a gradual thinning of purpose that is, in some sense, invisible from the inside. Perhaps the most useful thing a team can do with its rituals is not to abolish them but to ask, from time to time, what they were ever for.
