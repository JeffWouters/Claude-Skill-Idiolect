I was dining with a companion who fell, in the course of the evening, to discussing a certain friend, and discussing him with some severity. The charge was that he avoided the society of women; that he would not take the trouble to go where they were, or to make himself agreeable when he found himself among them; and the verdict was that this was mere laziness, a kind of moral slackness, since every one, as my companion put it, owes society a certain effort, and has no right to withhold it for the sake of his own comfort. It was said with conviction, and I could see that it was meant as a serious judgement upon character.

I did not agree with it then, and I do not agree with it now. Is sociability, then, a duty at all? I am inclined myself to believe that it is not, though I know that the opposite view is perhaps the more respectable. Of course it is good for a man to mix with his fellows, and good for him to mix with women as well as men; it widens his sympathies, it rubs off his angles, it saves him from the narrowness and oddity which grow so easily upon the solitary. But these are benefits to himself; they are reasons why he may be wise to seek company, not reasons why he is wicked to avoid it; and if he chooses to forgo them, the loss, so far as I can see, is chiefly his own.

The worst of it is that the doctrine of social duty produces a very unlovely frame of mind. There is a type of person who goes out into company, so to speak, with a moral purpose, in order to do good, to cheer the dull, to draw out the shy, to discharge an obligation to the community; and I confess that I find something rather smug in the attitude. One feels oneself, perhaps, being improved, or patronised, or made the object of a benevolent exercise. The talk loses its freedom; the laughter becomes a little conscious; and the whole occasion takes on the air of a charitable bazaar, where everybody is virtuous and nobody is amused.

Surely the true reason for seeking company is simply that one enjoys it. We go to our friends as we go to a fire on a cold evening, because it warms us, not because we owe the fire a visit. If this friend does not find that warmth in the society of women, I think one may regret it for his sake, and hope that he will find it in time; but it is no part of anybody else to scold him into the drawing-room. He would sit there, I fear, like a cat carried into a strange kitchen, stiff, watchful, and longing only for the door.
