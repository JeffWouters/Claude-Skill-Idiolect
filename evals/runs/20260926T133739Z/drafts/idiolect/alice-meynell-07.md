We have no measure for the length of history except the length of our own lives. The historian may give us his dates, and we may subtract the one from the other with perfect accuracy; but the figure we arrive at means nothing until we have translated it into the only currency we possess, which is the felt duration of our own years. A century is so many lifetimes, and a lifetime is so many of the years we have known; there is, I think, no other scale.

In childhood that scale is magnificent. The first decade of a life is huge--a whole world of seasons, each one wide and slow and distinct, so that the child who has lived ten years has lived, in his own reckoning, through an immense succession of ages. When he learns of kingdoms that rose and fell long before his birth, he measures them by this generous standard, and they become majestic accordingly. The past is vast because his own brief past is vast; its dynasties stretch away like the endless afternoons of his summers, and its centuries are almost beyond imagining.

But the man grows up, and the years grow short. His decades begin to pass with an alarming swiftness; the interval between one event and another, which once seemed an age, is now a moment, and he looks back upon ten years as he once looked back upon a week. By this diminished measure the centuries lose their weight. So many lifetimes? It is perhaps nothing--a few of these hurried decades placed end to end. The ancient kingdoms move closer, their grandeur thins, and the whole long procession of history contracts into something almost within reach. Is this wisdom, or is it merely accuracy of a kind? It is, at any rate, an accuracy that has cost him the majesty of the past, and I know not that the exchange was a good one. The child measured wrongly, and saw truly.
