Most poets, it is supposed, begin with the real world and wander off into dreams as they grow older. Chaucer did precisely the reverse. He began among dreams, and he ended among men and women. In his youth he wrote as the fashion of his time required, and that fashion was allegory. It had come across the sea from the courts of France, where lords and ladies, having little to do, amused themselves with elaborate fancies of love, in which the passions of the heart, dressed up as persons, walked and conversed like courtiers. The taste was a graceful one, and it was tenacious; it lingered in our literature for centuries after the courts that bred it had passed away, as the scent of a flower lingers in a room long after the flower is withered. The young poet, with his quick ear and his love of the fashionable, took to it naturally. But there was something in him which the garden could not hold. The abstractions would not satisfy him for ever, and perhaps they had never quite satisfied him. What are these painted passions, after all, but the ghosts of real persons, drained of blood and dressed in allegorical robes? He had seen the persons themselves--in the street, at the inn, in the counting-house and the court--and by degrees he laid the ghosts aside and took up the living. In his later work the personified virtues and vices are gone, and in their place stand ordinary, solid, grounded people, who eat and drink and quarrel, who have trades and tempers and bad habits. The one manner shows you what a man ought to feel; the other shows you what a man is. Nobody who has read both can doubt which Chaucer came to prefer.

There remained a difficulty. A poet who deals in real persons deals in separate stories, and separate stories, however excellent, will not of themselves make a book; they lie side by side like beads without a string. It was here that his invention was happiest. He hit upon a pilgrimage. Just as a road draws travellers of every kind into one company for a few days, so the pilgrimage drew his tales into one design, and gave each a teller, an occasion and an audience. The frame is simple; nay, it is almost commonplace. Yet it is exactly what was wanted, and with it the poet of dreams became, once and for all, the poet of the road. Was ever a change of fashion better made?
