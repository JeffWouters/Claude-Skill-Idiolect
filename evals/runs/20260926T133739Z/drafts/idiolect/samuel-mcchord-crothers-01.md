We are often told that the Christian nations conduct their wars in a more elevated spirit than the ancients did. This is all very well, and I should be sorry to deny any people the credit of good intentions. But when I turn to the old chronicles of conquest I find in them a candour which I miss in the dispatches of to-day, and which I have come to regard, perhaps unreasonably, as a kind of primitive virtue. The ancient historian did not pretend. His king wanted the land on the other side of the river because it was good land and he had not got it; he took it, and the record says so in words that a child could understand.

The modern conqueror has learned a more refined trade. He does not seize a country; he relieves it. He arrives, as he is careful to explain, out of pure solicitude for the inhabitants, who have been misgoverned by their own rulers and stand in sore need of enlightened management. That this management should happen to include their fields, their harbours and their mines is a coincidence which he would think it ungenerous to mention. The victims are not robbed. They are improved.

Where, then, is the difference? It is chiefly a matter of tailoring. The old violence went about naked and was not ashamed of its appearance, while the new violence has been to an excellent tailor, who has done his work with a professional skill that I cannot help admiring even as I distrust it. There is a garment of piety cut to fit every occasion, a lining of benevolence for the cold weather of public opinion, and seams so neatly turned that you would hardly suspect a sword underneath.

"But surely," says my reader, "it is some gain that men should feel the need of an excuse." Perhaps it is. I would not grudge virtue whatever small income the pious phrase brings her. Only I should prefer that it be paid in some currency other than the lives of the people who are being so considerately improved.
