Every year, at the season of Christ's birth, the Christian nations repeat the promise of peace on earth, and every year the critic of those nations points out, with some justice, that the promise has not been kept. The nations that sing the carols are, it must be confessed, the same nations that keep the armies. "Is not this," asks the critic, "the plainest hypocrisy?"

I should answer him more gently than he answers them. The civilization that was to carry the gospel over the world had to be carried by somebody, and the only persons available for the work were human beings. They were not saints but merchants and soldiers and statesmen, with the usual appetites and the usual imperfectnesses, and they carried their faith as a traveller carries a letter of introduction whose contents he has not entirely read. It would have been a miracle if the message had arrived without some damage in the post.

There is a poem of Milton's, [quote needed: the relevant lines], in which the poet, having celebrated the coming of the Prince of Peace, pauses to observe that the fulfilment is not yet. The prophecy is true, but it is not in a hurry. Between the promise and its keeping there lies a long and weary interval, and we are living in it. That is not the language of disillusion but, perhaps, the language of a man who knows how long it takes to learn anything worth knowing.

The Christian nations, then, are not frauds. They are learners, and backward ones. But we should remember where they began. They did not start from the gospel and fall away from it; they started a very great distance from it, among fighting tribes and conquering empires, and they have been travelling toward it ever since, slowly, with frequent relapses, and not always in the right direction. A pupil who has not yet mastered his lesson is not an impostor, but simply a pupil.
