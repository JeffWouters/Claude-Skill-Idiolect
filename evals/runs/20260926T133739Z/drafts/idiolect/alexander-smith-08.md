The old story-tellers had no fear of misfortune; they heaped sorrow upon their heroines as a mason heaps stone upon stone, knowing that the higher the pile, the finer the view from the top. It is even so with this wronged queen. Cast adrift upon the sea with her little son, betrayed, exiled, and all but forgotten, she floats upon the waters as helpless as a leaf upon a mill-stream; and Providence, which has apparently deserted her, is only biding its time. At length a Roman commander comes upon the boat, takes the mother and child aboard, not knowing who they are, and gives them the shelter of his protection. Meanwhile the king, her husband, has learned the truth of the wrong that was done her. Remorse has laid its heavy hand upon him, and he goes about his affairs like a man who carries a stone in his breast; and at a banquet, among strangers, the boy is set before him. The king looks at the child, and looks again. Something in that young face--the eyes, the brow, the grave sweetness of the mouth--brings back to him the wife he thinks he has lost; and the strong man, in the midst of the feast, bows his head and weeps. It is perhaps the finest moment in the tale--verily, the old poet knew where the heart is tenderest! Recognition follows, and forgiveness follows recognition. Then comes the last and happiest turn, when the queen goes before the emperor, who is her own father, and reveals herself to him; and the old man, who had mourned her as dead, receives her as one risen from the grave. Husband, wife, son and father are gathered together at last. Nothing is wanting, and everything ends happily. We smile at so complete a reconciliation, and say that life is never so obliging; and it is not. The story, however, does not pretend to be life. It pretends to be better than life, and it succeeds!
