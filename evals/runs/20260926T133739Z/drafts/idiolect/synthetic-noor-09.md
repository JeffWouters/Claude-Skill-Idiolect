The hiss stops. Then the water comes. That is the moment to close the valve.

Not before. If you close it while it still hisses, the air stays in. Not long after either. You want water, and you want it only briefly. So I keep the cloth under the valve and my eyes on the hole. The first drop of water, and I turn the key back.

Gently. This is the part people get wrong. The valve is small, and the key gives you more force than you think. Turn it until it stops. Then stop. Do not lean on it. A valve forced shut is a valve that leaks next year.

I have done that. [example needed: the time a valve went wrong]. I do not do it now.

Here is the round. Ground floor first, each radiator in turn. Then the stairs. Then the top floor. Close each valve as the water shows. Wipe it dry. Move on.

So to the boiler. Every radiator you bleed lets out a little water. Together it adds up. The pressure drops. The gauge on the front of the boiler shows it. [number needed: the pressure it should read]. If the needle sits below that, the system needs topping up.

That is the job of the filling loop. It is a short hose, or a pair of small taps, under the boiler. Open it slowly. Watch the gauge. The needle creeps up. When it reaches the right mark, close the loop. Too much pressure is as bad as too little.

Then the heating goes back on. And you wait.

The radiators warm evenly this time. Top to bottom. No cool strip along the top. The pipes go quiet. No knocking, no trickle in the walls at night.

It does not last. The air comes back. It always does. By next year I will be looking for the key again.

Tonight the house is warm. The key is put away. For now.
