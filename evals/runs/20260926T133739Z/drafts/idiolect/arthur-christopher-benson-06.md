I find it more and more difficult, the longer I consider the matter, to believe in a God who needs to be appeased, who must be bought off, so to speak, with offerings and observances, as a surly neighbour is quieted with a present at the new year. The whole idea of propitiation seems to me to belong to an older and darker world, where the divine was a thing to be feared and managed rather than trusted; and I am inclined myself to believe that it lingers among us chiefly because we have inherited the words without examining what they mean.

What, then, is sacrifice, if it is not an offering laid upon an altar? I think it is something at once simpler and harder, the giving up of our own selfishness, our comforts and our small indulgences, for the sake of ends which are worth the cost. It is not a transaction with heaven; it is a discipline of the heart. The mother who spends herself for her children, the worker who sets aside his ease for a cause he believes in, the friend who surrenders a pleasure that another may be spared a pain, these, perhaps, make the only offerings which have any real value.

And faith, as I conceive it, is not the acceptance of a set of propositions, but an inward aligning of one's own will with the will of God; a slow, patient, often painful effort to desire what He desires, and to accept what He sends, even when it is sorrow, with a kind of trustful quietness. One does not understand the pain; one does not pretend to; but one believes that it is not wasted, and one goes on.

Of course this leaves little room for ceremony as such. But I would not say that worship is abolished; I would rather say that it is enlarged. Whatever makes us thankful, whatever calms and steadies us, whatever humbles us and makes us conscious of our smallness and our dependence, is worship, whether it comes to us in a cathedral or on a lonely hillside at evening, when the light fades slowly from the fields and the first star appears.
