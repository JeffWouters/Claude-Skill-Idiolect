I once had a long conversation with a reformed forger, a man who had retired from his profession with, I believe, a sincere intention of never practising it again. What interested me was not his repentance, which was conventional enough, but the moral code he had observed while he was still in business. For he had a code, and he spoke of it with the quiet pride of a craftsman describing the traditions of his guild.

Nothing in his former life had been so distasteful to him as the petty thief. He described the pickpocket and the sneak with a contempt that would have done credit to a magistrate. They were, in his opinion, a low and unskilful class of persons, who took what lay within reach and had neither the patience nor the intelligence to take anything else.

I made the error of suggesting that forgery was, after all, only a more elaborate form of theft. He was genuinely offended. "Theft!" he said, and for a moment I thought he would leave the room. A thief, he explained when he had recovered his composure, simply took advantage of carelessness and could claim no more merit than a man who picks up a purse in the road, whereas a forger matched his wits against the best minds that the banks and the government could hire. The detectives were paid professionals, trained for the purpose and well supplied with resources. The forger was alone. When he won, he had beaten them fairly at their own game. When he lost, he went to prison, and he considered that the rules had been properly enforced.

I confess I found the argument difficult to answer on its own ground. It was the reasoning of a sportsman, not a criminal. He did not think of the men whose names he had signed, or of the money that had passed through his hands. He thought of the contest. Remove the victims, and what remained was a very honourable game.
