It is the custom of biographers to follow a famous man to his bed, to sit there through the last weeks, and to report to the world how he coughed, how he wandered, and how at length he failed. They do it without apology, and with a certain pride in their thoroughness. The reader is told the name of the malady and the progress of the fever, what the physicians said and what they could not say, and at last the day, the hour, the final breath; and all this accumulation is called completeness. Yet no one would think of publishing, in the same particularity, the history of an illness from which the man recovered, for that illness would be considered his own affair. Why, then, should the one that killed him be ours? A sickness does not become public property merely because it happened to be the last. It was private while he suffered it, and it is private still.

Of all such privacies, the privacy of a disordered mind should be guarded the most scrupulously. If the body in its weakness deserves a veil, how much more the intelligence in its wandering! Madness is not the man, and delirium is not the man; what he said in fever he did not say--or would not have said, had he been himself. To set down those broken words, to weigh them, to discover in them some revelation of the secret character, is to take the one moment when he could not defend himself and make it the moment by which he is remembered. I fear this is done more often than we suppose, and with a multitudinous industry that mistakes the gathering of particulars for the understanding of a person. It is not knowledge but trespass.

Nor are deathbed details biography at all, in any true sense of the word. A life is the thing a man accomplished with his health and his wits--his work, his friendships, his decisions, his long habitualness of thought and conduct. The dying is not the life but the conclusion of the life, and very frequently the least characteristic portion of it. The strong man grows weak, the eloquent man grows silent, and the gentle man, it may be, grows fretful and suspicious. Which of these is the portrait? Surely none of them. A biographer who lingers in that chamber has confused the event that closed a career with the career itself, and has devoted his final chapter to the one season in which his subject was least himself.

There are those, indeed, who may be forgiven for dwelling upon these hours. The wife, the child, the friend who watched--they must needs remember, and they remember because they loved. Grief has its own right to the sickroom, since it was present there and kept the vigil, and its lingering is only the lingering of an affection that does not know how to depart. But the biographer was not present, and he kept no vigil. His lingering is not grief but curiosity, and curiosity has no such excuse. Let him leave the room to those who mourn, for it was never his.
