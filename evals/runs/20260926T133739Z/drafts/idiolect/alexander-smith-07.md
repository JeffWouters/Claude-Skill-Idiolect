We are apt to suppose that a building dies when its use departs from it; but a building, like a man, may outlive its occupation by a long while, and grow more interesting in its decay. Beside the castle ruin stands a little chapel of the same age, crumbling quietly into the grass. Its religious life has long since gone out of it. No bell calls to it; no priest serves in it; the carving and colour that once adorned it have fallen or faded, and the walls stand bare to the weather. The villagers pass it every day without a glance--it is part of the commonplace to them, like the hedge and the milestone--and I suspect that hardly one of them could tell you what lies within. Yet within lie the old lords of the castle, sleeping in their tombs while their towers tumble overhead. On one of these the sculptor has pictured a husband and wife lying side by side, hand clasped in hand, as though Death himself, who parts most couples, had been persuaded for once to let this pair go on together. [example needed: a detail of the carving] The stone has worn; the faces are blurred; but the devotion is plain. Is it all over with them? I cannot think so. Their titles are forgotten, their castle is a heap, their chapel is a shell. But the virtues honoured on that tomb--faithfulness, tenderness, constancy--are not things that crumble with mortar. Just as the scent of a crushed herb clings to the hand long after the herb is thrown away, so these qualities cling about the place; and it may be that they endure, beyond the chapel and the tomb, wherever such qualities go. Love was the one possession of theirs that the centuries could not steal!
