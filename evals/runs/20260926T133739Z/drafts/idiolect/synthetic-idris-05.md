How do you report progress on work that is not yet finished, and whose shape is not yet entirely clear even to the people doing it? It may be that the standard answers, the colour codes and percentages and neat status fields, are less a solution to this problem than a way of avoiding it. I suspect most people who have filled in such a report have felt the gap between what they wrote and what they knew.

Consider what a single colour is asked to carry. The work itself is untidy; some parts are going well, some are stalled, some have not been properly understood, and one or two may be worrying in ways that are hard to name. A colour code flattens all of this into one of three states, and in doing so it arguably discards precisely the information that a reader would most need.

There is also something revealing in the act of reporting itself. A status report is, in some sense, a translation from the language of the people doing the work into the language of the people overseeing it, and like any translation it exposes the relationship between the two parties, what each expects, what each fears, and what each has learned is safe to say.

It seems to me that this explains why colours so frequently change too late. To turn a report amber on the strength of a vague worry carries real costs, since it invites questions that cannot yet be answered and attention that may not be welcome; to leave it green costs nothing until the moment it costs a great deal. Perhaps a report that changes colour late is not a failure of honesty but an accurate record of what honesty has been made to cost.
