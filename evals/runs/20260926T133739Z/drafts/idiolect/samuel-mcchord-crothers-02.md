When fortune comes to a mining town it does not come privately. A prospector who had searched the hills for [number needed: how many] years without reward struck at last upon a vein of remarkably rich ore. He then did what any sensible man in his position ought to do, and decided to marry. But he was not content to marry in a corner. He invited the entire population to the wedding, and since the ceremony was to be held at some distance, he paid for the transport of every guest who cared to come.

I should like to have seen that company on the road. The officials of the county sat beside the gamblers whose tables they were supposed to regulate, and the clergy, who perhaps entertained some private misgivings about their neighbours, travelled with the laborers from the mines, who entertained no misgivings about anybody. It was a pilgrimage in the ancient manner. I could not help thinking of Chaucer's pilgrims, that miscellaneous fellowship which shared one dusty road and told its tales without much regard for rank or reputation. The mining camp, I suspect, could have furnished the poet with most of his characters and one or two he had overlooked.

"But what," you ask, "was the purpose of it all?" The purpose, I think, was that everybody should have a turn. A mining town is a democracy of celebrity. It does not keep one great man on a pedestal for a generation; it lifts each of its residents into prominence for a day or a week, admires him thoroughly, and then sets him down again to make room for the next candidate. To-day it was the prospector and his bride. To-morrow it would be somebody else, and nobody considered the arrangement unjust.
