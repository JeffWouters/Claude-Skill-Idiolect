Every age complains that it works too hard, and every age consoles itself with a dream of people who do not work at all. Shakespeare, who knew most things about us, knew this too, and his plays are full of pleasant drifters--men and women who have slipped out of the harness of society and seem none the worse for it. In The Tempest, a courtier, finding himself upon an island, begins idly to legislate for it. Were he king there, he says, he would have no traffic, no riches, no poverty, no labour; money should be unknown, and nobody should toil, since Nature herself would bring forth all things in abundance for the people. His companions mock him, and he takes their mockery with a good grace. Yet one feels that the poet did not wholly mock him. The dream is too sweetly spoken for that.

Go next into the forest of Arden. There a small community has set up house among the trees, and appears, to the spectator's considerable surprise, happier than any assembly in palaces. Nobody there flatters; nobody there schemes; the weather is inclement, but it is honestly inclement. The foresters wander, and sing, and philosophise, and fall in love, and out of love, and in again, with nothing to hinder them but the weather. Compared with ordinary society, this little community appears not merely happier but wiser; it has shaken off the foolishest parts of civilisation and kept, one would say, only the gracefulest. What should they want with offices and lawsuits? Why should they return? And the reader, sitting by his own fire with his own cares about him, half wishes that he might join them to-morrow.

But he knows, and Shakespeare knew, that he cannot. Such idylls are the pure invention of a poet; they exist nowhere but on the stage, and they would not survive a week of real rain. The island has no neighbours and the forest no tax-gatherer; the one is imaginary, the other is rather conveniently free of everything that makes life hard. Care, the old tyrant, has simply been left out of the cast. Just as a painter may leave the smoke out of his picture of a city, and give you roofs and spires shining in a clear air that no city ever breathed, so the dramatist leaves out of his greenwood the want and weariness that would have followed any real community into it. Is he to be blamed for that? Surely not. He does not deceive us. He lets us walk for an hour in a world that is better than our own, and then, very kindly, sends us home. The dream is a fantasy; verily, it is a fantasy we could ill spare!
