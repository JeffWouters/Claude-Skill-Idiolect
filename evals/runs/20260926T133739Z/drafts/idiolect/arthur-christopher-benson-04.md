I have often wondered, when I consider the life of a college town, at the extraordinary solemnity with which the custom of calling is maintained. Here is a society made up, for the most part, of busy people, whose real business is thought and study, and whose working energy is the most precious thing they possess; and yet the afternoons are frittered away, week after week, in a round of dutiful visits, paid not because anybody desires to pay them, but because it would be thought discourteous to leave them unpaid. One puts on a decent coat, walks half-a-mile to a house where one is not particularly wanted, sits for a quarter of an hour upon the edge of a chair, exchanges a few remarks about the weather and the term, and walks home again, a little tired, a little bored, and with the best hours of the day gone.

What, I ask myself, is the sense of all this laborious ceremony? I do not believe, when I have turned the matter over as fairly as I can, that there is any sense in it at all. Of course one would not abolish hospitality, or pretend that neighbours ought to live like strangers; the pleasures of society are real, and a learned community has perhaps more need of them than most. But why should they be taken in so stiff and formal a shape? It would be far more reasonable, I think, if a household were simply known to be at home upon a certain evening, when friends might drop in or not as they pleased, without ceremony and without reproach; the work of the day would be done, the mind would be ready for recreation, and those who came would come because they wished to.

The worst of it is that obligation spoils the very thing it is meant to secure. A senior politician, reluctant to attend some social occasion, once joked that compulsory attendance takes the fun out of anything, [the politician's exact words needed]; and the jest, like most good jests, had a solid truth at the heart of it. A pleasure that is exacted ceases to be a pleasure at all; it becomes a tax, and is paid, like other taxes, with a sigh.
