In a mining settlement the art of conversation is taken seriously, and so is the art of the sermon. The miners, as I found, are exacting critics of both. A preacher who comes among them with his discourse written out and reads it from his notes will be listened to politely and forgotten before he has reached the door. What they want is a man who speaks as if the thing had just occurred to him and could not wait. They value a lively sermon as they value a lively story, and for the same reason: it sounds like somebody talking.

Their own talk about one another has a quality I have rarely met elsewhere. It is perfectly candid, for when a miner describes his neighbour he tells you what the man is, and he does not trouble to soften it with those spiteful qualifications which polite society uses to make a compliment do the work of an insult. There is no "of course he means well" and no "I would not say a word against him." He simply states the facts as he has observed them.

The result is sometimes startling. Good and bad traits are set down side by side, like entries in a ledger that nobody intends to balance. A man will be described as [example needed: a virtue and a vice set side by side], and the speaker will pass from one item to the next without any change of tone. He is not judging, but reporting.

"But is not this rather comic?" asks the reader. It is, though the humour is entirely unintended. The miner has no notion that he has said anything amusing; he is merely being accurate, and accuracy about human nature, when it is not arranged into a moral, has a way of sounding like a joke. I should not wish to correct him. Most of us could do with a neighbour who describes us so fairly.
