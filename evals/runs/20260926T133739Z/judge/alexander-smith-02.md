# Judge packet alexander-smith-02

## The passage (by the author)

Hazlitt, writing of the four great English poets, tells us, Chaucer's characteristic is intensity, Spenser's remoteness, Milton's sublimity, and Shakspeare's everything. The sentence is epigrammatic and memorable enough; but so far as Chaucer is concerned, it requires a little explanation. He is not intense, for instance, as Byron is intense, or as Wordsworth is intense. He does not see man like the one, nor nature like the other. He would not have cared much for either of these poets. And yet, so far as straightforwardness in dealing with a subject, and complete though quiet realisation of it goes to make up intensity of poetic mood, Chaucer amply justifies his critic. There is no wastefulness or explosiveness about the old writer. He does his work silently, and with no appearance of effort. His poetry shines upon us like a May morning; but the streak over the eastern hill, the dew on the grass, the wind that bathes the brows of the wayfarer, are not there by haphazard: they are the results of occult forces, a whole solar system has had a hand in their production. From the apparent ease with which an artist works, one does not readily give him credit for the mental force he is continuously putting forth. To many people, a chaotic "Festus" is more wonderful than a rounded, melodious "Princess." The load which a strong man bears gracefully does not seem so heavy as the load which the weaker man staggers under. Incompletion is force fighting; completion is force quiescent, its work done. Nature's forces are patent enough in some scarred volcanic moon in which no creature can breathe; only the sage, in some soft green earth, can discover the same forces reft of fierceness and terror, and translated into sunshine, and falling dew, and the rainbow gleaming on the shower. It is somewhat in this way that the propriety of Hazlitt's criticism is to be vindicated. Chaucer is the most simple, natural, and homely of our poets, and whatever he attempts he does thoroughly. The Wife of Bath is so distinctly limned that she could sit for her portrait. You can count the embroidered sprigs in the jerkin of the squire. You hear the pilgrims laugh as they ride to Canterbury. The whole thing is admirably life-like and seems easy, and in the seeming easiness we are apt to forget the imaginative sympathy which bodies forth the characters, and the joy and sorrow from which that sympathy has drawn nurture. Unseen by us, the ore has been dug, and smelted in secret furnaces, and when it is poured into perfect moulds, we are apt to forget by what potency the whole thing has been brought about.

## Draft A

We are accustomed to think of intensity as a noisy virtue. The intense poet, in the common imagination, is the poet who raves and gesticulates, who tears his passion to tatters, who cannot mention a sunset without setting the heavens on fire. When, therefore, a critic declared that the peculiar and distinguishing quality of Chaucer is intensity, many readers must have smiled; for what is there of storm and fury in that genial, observant, unhurried old story-teller? And yet the critic was right, and the smile is misplaced. Chaucer's intensity is of another kind than the one we look for. It is calm; it is direct; it is complete. It does not shout at you, and it does not need to--it simply sees, and sets down what it sees, with a concentration so entire that nothing is left over. As a clear stream shows you every pebble in its bed, and yet seems to have no depth at all until you put your arm into it, so his verse looks shallow only because it is transparent. The one kind of intensity advertises its labour; the other conceals it altogether.

That concealment is, indeed, the whole secret of the matter. Effortless art is the most laborious thing in the world. The line that reads as though it had fallen upon the page of its own accord has commonly cost its maker more thought than many of those elaborate passages which proclaim their difficulty in every syllable; and the reader, admiring the ease, never suspects the sweat. Nay, the better the work, the less it is suspected. Consider his pilgrims. You have met them; you have ridden beside them; you know the tone of their voices, the cut of their garments, the tricks of their tempers, as well as you know the habits of your neighbours. [example needed: a particular pilgrim and the detail that makes him live] They are so vivid, so solid, so entirely real, that we take them for granted, as we take for granted the daylight; it never occurs to us that somebody made them. Were they less alive, we should praise the maker more. That is the ironical reward of perfection--it is mistaken for nature, and the artist is thanked by being forgotten. What is this but intensity of the highest order? A mind that could hold so many human beings steadily in view, and render each of them without a wasted stroke, was not a lazy or a lukewarm mind. It burned, but it burned without smoke!

## Draft B

A critic once claimed that the defining trait of Chaucer is intensity. At first the word seems wrong. We tend to think of intensity as something loud: the rhetoric of a tragedian, the heightened pitch of a poet who wants us to know how hard he is feeling. Chaucer is none of that. He is easy, genial, conversational. And yet the claim holds up, and it holds up precisely because his intensity is of another kind.

It is calm. Chaucer does not strain after his effects or announce them. It is direct. He looks at a thing and sets it down without fuss, without the ornament that so often stands between a writer and his subject. And it is complete. When he has looked, he has seen the whole of what is there, and nothing essential has been missed. This is intensity not as heat but as concentration: the full attention of a mind brought to bear on a single object and held there until the object is fully understood.

The difficulty is that this kind of intensity hides itself. Art that looks effortless is almost always the result of enormous effort, and the better it is, the less of that effort shows. A line that seems to have fallen naturally into place has usually been weighed, tested and rejected in a dozen other forms before the right one was found. Chaucer's verse reads as if he were simply talking, and it is exactly this ease that tricks us into underrating the labour behind it. The mental effort is there; it has just been absorbed into the finished work so thoroughly that we no longer notice it.

The best evidence is his pilgrims. They are so vivid, so plainly real, that readers take them for granted. We meet them as we would meet people on a road: this one talkative, that one proud, another shy or greedy or kind. We do not stop to ask how they were made, any more than we ask how our acquaintances were made. They simply exist. But no character exists simply. Each of those portraits required a writer to see a human being whole, to choose, out of everything that might be said, the few details that would make that person live, and to set those details down so lightly that the choosing disappears.

That is what the critic meant. It is not the intensity of the man who shouts, but of the man who looks, and keeps looking, until he has seen everything, and then tells us what he saw in the plainest words available. It is a quieter virtue than the other kind, and easier to miss. But it is the rarer of the two, and it is the reason his people are still alive.

## Draft C

It has been said by a critic--and I think truly--that the quality which above all others distinguishes Chaucer is intensity. The word startles at first. We associate intensity with the fevered poets, with men who write as if their hair were on fire; and Chaucer, of all our writers, seems the least fevered. He is cheerful, he is easy, he is never in a hurry. But the critic is right, and the reason why he is right is worth some consideration.

There are two kinds of intensity. There is the intensity that shouts, that gesticulates, that tears a passion to rags; and there is the intensity that simply sees. The first is a fire of straw--great blaze, great smoke, and presently a handful of ashes. The second is the steady glow of the forge. Chaucer's is of the second kind. It is calm, it is direct, it is complete. He looks at a thing, and he looks at it so entirely that nothing is left over; he sets it down, and it stands there for ever. He does not tell you that his subject is wonderful; he makes no parade of his own emotion; he merely gives you the thing itself, and leaves you to be astonished if you please.

And here lies the trap for the unwary reader. Art that looks effortless is commonly supposed to have cost no effort. We watch the skater's long curve upon the ice, and think it a simple matter to go round like that; we try, and find ourselves upon our backs. The ease is the last fruit of the labour, not the absence of it. Behind every line that seems to have dropped from Chaucer's pen as lightly as a leaf drops from a tree there lies a prodigious exercise of mind--of observation, of selection, of rejection. The things he left out would furnish forth a dozen lesser poets. What he kept, he kept because it was the one right thing; and to find the one right thing among a thousand is the hardest work in the world.

The proof of all this is to be found in his pilgrims. They are more real to us than most of the people we meet in the street. We know how they talk, how they ride, what they would say at table, what they would think of us. And we take all this for granted--which is the highest compliment we could pay him, and the least deserved by our indolence. We do not wonder that the pilgrims are alive, any more than we wonder that the sun rises. But let any man try to make a single living figure with ink and paper, and he will learn soon enough what the wonder is. The pilgrims live because Chaucer looked at them with an intensity that burned away everything false; and they will go on living when the shouting poets are silent.
