# Judge packet alexander-smith-03

## The passage (by the author)

Ah, me! what a world this was to live in two or three centuries ago, when it was getting itself discovered--when the sunset gave up America, when a steel hand had the spoiling of Mexico and Peru! Then were the "Arabian Nights" commonplace, enchantments a matter of course, and romance the most ordinary thing in the world. Then man was courting Nature; now he has married her. Every mystery is dissipated. The planet is familiar as the trodden pathway running between towns. We no longer gaze wistfully to the west, dreaming of the Fortunate Isles. We seek our wonders now on the ebbed sea-shore; we discover our new worlds with the microscope. Yet, for all that time has brought and taken away, I am glad to know that the vagabond sleeps in our blood, and awakes now and then. Overlay human nature as you please, here and there some bit of rock, or mound of aboriginal soil, will crop out with the wild-flowers growing upon it, sweetening the air. When the boy throws his Delectus or his Euclid aside, and takes passionately to the reading of "Robinson Crusoe" or Bruce's "African Travels," do not shake your head despondingly over him and prophesy evil issues. Let the wild hawk try its wings. It will be hooded, and will sit quietly enough on the falconer's perch ere long. Let the wild horse career over its boundless pampas; the jerk of the lasso will bring it down soon enough. Soon enough will the snaffle in the mouth and the spur of the tamer subdue the high spirit to the bridle, or the carriage-trace. Perhaps not; and, if so, the better for all parties. Once more there will be a new man and new deeds in the world. For Genius is a vagabond, Art is a vagabond, Enterprise is a vagabond. Vagabonds have moulded the world into its present shape; they have made the houses in which we dwell, the roads on which we ride and drive, the very laws that govern us. Respectable people swarm in the track of the vagabond as rooks in the track of the ploughshare. Respectable people do little in the world except storing wine-cellars and amassing fortunes for the benefit of spendthrift heirs. Respectable well-to-do Grecians shook their heads over Leonidas and his three hundred when they went down to Thermopylae. Respectable Spanish churchmen with shaven crowns scouted the dream of Columbus. Respectable German folks attempted to dissuade Luther from appearing before Charles and the princes and electors of the Empire, and were scandalised when he declared that "Were there as many devils in Worms as there were tiles on the house-tops, still would he on." Nature makes us vagabonds, the world makes us respectable.

## Draft A

There is in most men, deep down beneath the crust of habit and respectability, a restless and wandering instinct--a something that looks out of the window of a morning and wishes it were elsewhere. It is the gipsy in the blood. Society does its best to lay it; the counting-house, the pew and the fireside are all arrayed against it; and yet it is never quite laid. It stirs at the sound of a far-off bell, or at the smell of the sea, and for a moment the most sober citizen is a vagabond at heart.

In former times this instinct had a whole world to feed upon. There was an age when every ship that came home brought news of lands unheard of, when the map was a thing of guesses and fables, and when a man might sail over the edge of the known and find there whatever his fancy had promised him. But the age of exploration did its work too thoroughly. It made wonder ordinary. The islands were charted, the rivers traced to their sources, the savage and the palm-tree catalogued; and what had been marvellous became merely geographical. The wandering instinct, thus robbed of its old pasture, has turned elsewhere. We explore now through science. The microscope and the telescope are our caravels; we voyage into the drop of water and the depths of the sky, and bring back tidings as strange as any the old navigators told.

Let no parent, therefore, be alarmed when his boy devours tales of adventure with a hunger that leaves his lessons untouched. The appetite is a healthy one. It is the same appetite that sent men across unknown oceans, only it is young, and must feed on what it can find. The lad who reads of shipwrecks under the bedclothes is not on the high road to ruin; he is merely proving that he is alive. Time and the world will tame him soon enough--too soon, perhaps.

For it should be remembered that civilisation owes more to its rule-breakers than it is ever willing to confess. The men who built it were not, for the most part, the men who kept the rules; they were the men who broke them, and made new ones in their place. The cautious citizen has always been against the bold figure. He opposed him while he lived, and he opposed him on excellent grounds--the thing had never been done, the thing was dangerous, the thing would unsettle the parish. And when the bold figure had prevailed, and the thing was done, the cautious citizen settled down in the house the other had built, and wondered that anyone could ever have been so unreasonable as to oppose it. The wanderer makes the road; the citizen walks upon it, and complains of the dust.

## Draft B

It is commonly held that the settled man is the useful man, and that the wanderer is at best an ornament and at worst a nuisance; I confess I have never been able to believe it. There is in most of us, buried under rent-days and routine, a restless and vagabond instinct--a desire to be up and away, to see what lies beyond the hill, to sleep one night under a strange roof; and though we keep it chained, it rattles its chain now and then, and we hear it in the small hours. That instinct has done more for the world than all the prudence of all the parishes. There was an age when the earth itself was the great unknown, when every returning ship brought news of islands and empires, and wonder was the daily bread of men. That age is over. Its very success undid it; it made the marvellous ordinary, and filled up the blank places on the map with names that nobody now thinks strange. But the instinct did not die with the age that fed it; it has merely changed its road, as a river, dammed in one valley, finds its way into another. We explore to-day with the telescope and the microscope, with the crucible and the calculation; and the man of science, bent over his instruments in a quiet room, is as true a voyager as ever sailed westward into the unknown.

Parents are sometimes troubled when they find a boy devouring tales of adventure--shipwrecks, pirates, deserts, lost cities--as a hungry man devours bread. Why should they be troubled? The appetite is a healthy one, and it is the peculiar privilege of youth to possess it. Just as the young bird beats its wings in the nest long before it can fly, so the boy rehearses in fancy the journeys he may never take; and the rehearsal does him no harm--nay, it may do him much good. Let the grown man, who has forgotten his own hunger, remember at least that civilisation owes him nothing for his caution. The world was not built by the cautious. It was built, verily, by those who broke the rules, by men who went where they were forbidden to go, and said what they were forbidden to say. [example needed: a bold figure who defied the rules] And the cautious citizens of every age opposed them. They shook their heads, they wrote letters, they prophesied ruin; the one party made the future, the other did its utmost to prevent it. Posterity, with a fine irony, raises statues to the rebels and forgets the objectors. It is a pleasant thought for the vagabond, sitting by his fire on a winter night, that the wandering instinct in him is no vice, but the very spring of progress; and he might do worse than keep it bright!

## Draft C

There is in most people, however settled they appear, a restless instinct that wants to wander. It shows itself in the glance at a departing train, in the pleasure of a map, in the vague sense that somewhere beyond the next hill things might be different and better. We tend to treat this instinct as a weakness, something to be outgrown or kept in check. I am not so sure it deserves that treatment.

It helps to remember what the age of exploration did. For a few extraordinary generations, the edges of the known world kept moving outward. Every voyage brought back news of coasts and creatures and peoples no one at home had imagined. Wonder was not a rare feeling then; it was ordinary, almost a matter of routine. The odd consequence is that we have used most of it up. The map is filled in. There are few blank spaces left for a traveller to fill. But the instinct did not die when the blanks ran out. It moved. Today we explore through science, and the unknown lies not over the horizon but inside the atom, among the stars, in the workings of our own bodies. The explorer has put down the compass and picked up the instrument.

This is worth bearing in mind when adults fret about the young. Children and young people have an enormous appetite for adventure stories: shipwrecks, treasure, journeys through wild country, heroes who leave home and do not come back the same. Parents sometimes worry that this taste is unhealthy, that it breeds discontent or recklessness. It should not worry them. It is the same instinct that sent the explorers out, showing itself early, and a child who has never dreamed of running away to sea has probably missed something.

The deeper point is less comfortable. Much of civilisation was built by rule-breakers. The people who crossed the unknown ocean, who questioned the settled opinion, who went where they had been told not to go, were rarely respectable in their own time. Against them, in almost every age, stood the cautious citizens: sensible, law-abiding, and reliably opposed to every bold figure who threatened to disturb the arrangement of things. We honour the bold figures now, but we should notice that we would probably have been among those who opposed them. [example needed]

None of this means that everyone should abandon their responsibilities and set out for the horizon. The cautious citizen has his uses; someone has to stay home and keep the lamps lit. But the wandering instinct is not a flaw in human nature to be corrected. It is one of the engines of it. We should be careful not to crush it in others, and a little slower to be ashamed of it in ourselves.
