# Judge packet alexander-smith-04

## The passage (by the author)

Having discoursed so long about Dreamthorp, it is but fair that I should now introduce you to her lions. These are, for the most part, of a commonplace kind; and I am afraid that, if you wish to find romance in them, you must bring it with you. I might speak of the old church-tower, or of the church-yard beneath it, in which the village holds its dead, each resting-place marked by a simple stone, on which is inscribed the name and age of the sleeper, and a Scripture text beneath, in which live our hopes of immortality. But, on the whole, perhaps it will be better to begin with the canal, which wears on its olive-coloured face the big white water-lily already chronicled. Such a secluded place is Dreamthorp that the railway does not come near, and the canal is the only thing that connects it with the world. It stands high, and from it the undulating country may be seen stretching away into the gray of distance, with hills and woods, and stains of smoke which mark the sites of villages. Every now and then a horse comes staggering along the towing-path, trailing a sleepy barge filled with merchandise. A quiet, indolent life these bargemen lead in the summer days. One lies stretched at his length on the sun-heated plank; his comrade sits smoking in the little dog-hutch, which I suppose he calls a cabin. Silently they come and go; silently the wooden bridge lifts to let them through. The horse stops at the bridge-house for a drink, and there I like to talk a little with the men. They serve instead of a newspaper, and retail with great willingness the news they have picked up in their progress from town to town. I am told they sometimes marvel who the old gentleman is who accosts them from beneath a huge umbrella in the sun, and that they think him either very wise or very foolish. Not in the least unnatural! We are great friends, I believe--evidence of which they occasionally exhibit by requesting me to disburse a trifle for drink-money. This canal is a great haunt of mine of an evening. The water hardly invites one to bathe in it, and a delicate stomach might suspect the flavour of the eels caught therein; yet, to my thinking, it is not in the least destitute of beauty. A barge trailing up through it in the sunset is a pretty sight; and the heavenly crimsons and purples sleep quite lovingly upon its glossy ripples. Nor does the evening star disdain it, for as I walk along I see it mirrored therein as clearly as in the waters of the Mediterranean itself.

## Draft A

The sights of this village are few and modest, and a man who came in search of wonders would go away grumbling. But I did not come in search of wonders; I came to be let alone, and in that respect the village has never failed me. Such sights as it has I have learned to love, as one learns to love the plain features of an old friend; and of these the first, if not the finest, is the canal.

The canal is our one link with the outer world. There is no railway within reach; the roads are of the kind that the traveller curses in winter and forgets in summer; and so it is by the slow water that we hold communion with the towns. Whatever comes to us comes by the canal--coals, and timber, and the rare parcel, and, above all, news. It is a thin thread, but it is enough. The village hangs upon it as a spider hangs upon its line, and swings gently in the airs of the great world without ever being blown away.

The barges go by at the pace of a walking horse, which is the pace at which a sensible man would wish all things to go. Their crews are a race apart, half of the water and half of the land, and wholly of neither. They bring us gossip as bees bring pollen, gathering it at one lock and shaking it off at the next; and I have known a rumour to travel the whole length of the waterway, growing at every stage, until it arrived among us so swollen that its own parents would not have owned it. Now and then a bargeman, finding me on the towpath, will ask me for a trifle--to drink my health, as he puts it; and I have never had the heart to refuse him, for he is the postman of the village, and a postman should not go unrewarded.

I will not pretend that the canal is a thing of beauty in itself. Its water is murky, and moves, when it moves at all, with the reluctance of a thing that has no wish to arrive anywhere. Weeds trail in it, and it keeps its secrets well. In broad day it is merely useful. But at sunset it undergoes a change. The level light strikes along it, and the dull channel becomes a road of gold running out into the west; the sedges stand up black against the glow; a barge coming slowly home seems to be sailing out of the sky itself. For half an hour the poor ditch is transfigured. Then the light fails, and it is a ditch again. Much in life is like that; and we are fortunate if we happen to be on the towpath at the right moment.

## Draft B

Travellers who seek the sublime go to the mountains or the sea, and the man who lives in a quiet village must learn to be content with smaller wonders, and he will find, perhaps to his surprise, that they are wonders nevertheless. I have no cataract to show, no cathedral, no field of battle. I have only a canal. Yet I begin with it willingly, for it is the one road by which my village communicates with the world. Other villages have their highways and their iron roads, and mine has none of these. The canal, slow, patient and unambitious, creeps up to our very doors, and links us, by a thread of brown water, to the distant towns. Whatever comes to us from beyond the hills comes by barge. The heavy necessities of existence arrive in that leisurely fashion, and with them arrives something lighter and more welcome--the news.

The barge crews are the carriers of gossip as well as of cargo. They have seen the towns, they have heard the talk of the wharves and the taverns, and while the barge lies idle at the bank, they deliver it to anybody who will stand and listen, with a freedom and an embroidery that would do credit to a professional historian. The villagers gather round them as an older generation gathered round the pedlar, and carry away rumour enough to last until the next arrival. Now and then, it must be owned, the transaction is not wholly disinterested. A bargeman, having told his story, will hint that a story is thirsty work, and look hopefully at your pocket. You pay him, as you pay the newspaper, for the privilege of knowing what you could very well have lived without. [example needed: a piece of news brought up the canal] I do not grudge the coin. The one party gets a drink; the other gets the world.

I will not pretend that the canal is beautiful by day. Its water is murky and sluggish, weeds trail in it, and it reflects nothing clearly, and seems to have forgotten that it was ever meant to reflect. You might pass it at noon and think it the dullest thing in the parish. Come to it, however, at sunset. Then Nature, who has neglected it all day, remembers it at last, and lays across its surface a sheet of gold and rose; the dark channel burns like a strip of stained glass laid between the meadows; the barge, lying at its moorings, becomes a thing of romance; and the very weeds are jewelled. The glory lasts but a little while, and then the grey comes back. I have seen it, however, and I am rich!

## Draft C

Our village does not have much in the way of sights, and I would not want to oversell the few it has. There is no great house open to visitors, no famous view, nothing a guidebook would bother to mention. What there is, is modest, and it is best approached modestly. I will start with the canal, because in a sense everything else here starts with it too.

The canal is our only real link to the outside world. There is no railway, and the road, such as it is, leads mainly to other villages much like this one. The towns that supply us, and to which we send whatever we have to sell, are reached by water. Coal, grain, timber and the occasional piece of furniture that someone has ordered from a catalogue all arrive the same slow way. When the barges stop, as they do now and then for repairs or bad weather, the village feels its isolation at once, the way a house feels the cold when the fire goes out.

The barges bring more than cargo. The crews are the nearest thing we have to a newspaper. They stop at the lock, they tie up for a meal or a night, and they talk. From them we learn which town has a new mill and which an old scandal, who has married, who has died, what the prices are doing further down the line. Much of this news is second-hand and some of it is plainly invented, but it is received with gratitude all the same. A village that sees few strangers is not inclined to be fussy about the accuracy of its gossip.

I should be honest about the crews, though. They are friendly, and they are good company, and they are also not above asking for a tip. It is done cheerfully, with no suggestion of a threat, usually after some small service such as working the lock gates or carrying a message. You learn to keep a few coins handy, and you learn that refusing does no lasting harm, though it does cost you a certain amount of warmth the next time that barge comes through.

As for the water itself, I will not pretend it is clear. The canal is murky, brown and slow, with a surface that seems to hold things rather than reflect them. By day it is not much to look at. But at sunset it is transformed. The low light comes along it from the west, the dull water turns to copper and then to a deep, glowing red, the moored barges stand out black against it, and for a quarter of an hour our plain channel becomes the loveliest thing in the parish. Most evenings I walk down to see it, and most evenings I am not the only one there.
