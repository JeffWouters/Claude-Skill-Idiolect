# Judge packet alexander-smith-05

## The passage (by the author)

Chaucer's early poems have music and fancy, they are full of a natural delight in sunshine and the greenness of foliage; but they have little human interest. They are allegories for the most part, more or less satisfactorily wrought out. The allegorical turn of thought, the delight in pageantry, the "clothing upon" of abstractions with human forms, flowered originally out of chivalry and the feudal times. Chaucer imported it from the French, and was proud of it in his early poems, as a young fellow of that day might be proud of his horse furniture, his attire, his waving plume. And the poetic fashion thus set retained its vitality for a long while,--indeed, it was only thoroughly made an end of by the French Revolution, which made an end of so much else. About the last trace of its influence is to be found in Burns' sentimental correspondence with Mrs. M'Lehose, in which the lady is addressed as Clarinda, and the poet signs himself Sylvander. It was at best a mere beautiful gauze screen drawn between the poet and nature; and passion put his foot through it at once. After Chaucer's youth was over, he discarded somewhat scornfully these abstractions and shows of things. The "Flower and the Leaf" is a beautiful-tinted dream; the "Canterbury Tales" are as real as anything in Shakspeare or Burns. The ladies in the earlier poems dwell in forests, and wear coronals on their heads; the people in the "Tales" are engaged in the actual concerns of life, and you can see the splashes of mire upon their clothes. The separate poems which make up the "Canterbury Tales" were probably written at different periods, after youth was gone, and when he had fallen out of love with florid imagery and allegorical conceits; and we can fancy him, perhaps fallen on evil days and in retirement, anxious to gather up these loose efforts into one consummate whole. If of his flowers he would make a bouquet for posterity, it was of course necessary to procure a string to tie them together. These necessities, which ruin other men, are the fortunate chances of great poets. Then it was that the idea arose of a meeting of pilgrims at the Tabard in Southwark, of their riding to Canterbury, and of the different personages relating stories to beguile the tedium of the journey. The notion was a happy one, and the execution is superb. In those days, as we know, pilgrimages were of frequent occurrence; and in the motley group that congregated on such occasions, the painter of character had full scope. All conditions of people are comprised in the noisy band issuing from the courtyard of the Southwark inn on that May morning in the fourteenth century. Let us go nearer, and have a look at them.

## Draft A

Chaucer did not begin as the writer we now think of first. The poet of vivid, recognisable people started out writing in quite another mode, and the path from one to the other is one of the most interesting journeys in English literature.

His early work is allegorical. That was not a personal choice so much as an inheritance. Allegory came to him from French courtly culture, which for a long time set the fashion for any English poet who wanted to be taken seriously. In that tradition, the stuff of poetry was dreams, gardens and personified qualities. A lover wandered into an enclosed garden and met not people but abstractions, virtues and vices each walking about and speaking as though it were a person. The form was elegant and refined, and it was built to last; it lingered in European writing for centuries, long after Chaucer himself had outgrown it. A young poet at court could hardly avoid it, and Chaucer did not try. His first poems show him working skilfully inside those conventions, learning his craft on the approved models.

What is remarkable is that he did not stay there. As he matured, he dropped the abstractions and turned to ordinary, grounded people. Instead of a personified Pride, he gave us a proud man; instead of Avarice in a dream-garden, a greedy one riding along a road with mud on his boots. The shift sounds simple when described, but it amounts to a change in what poetry was for. The allegorist looks through people to the ideas behind them. The realist looks at people themselves and finds them interesting enough. Chaucer came to trust that a miller or a merchant, set down honestly, could carry as much meaning as any figure out of a dream.

That left him with a practical problem. He had many stories to tell, and they were of every kind: noble romances, bawdy jokes, pious legends, animal fables. Left as they were, they would have been a heap of separate pieces. What he needed was a frame that could hold them together, and he found it in a pilgrimage. A company of travellers sharing a road has every reason to pass the time with tales, and every reason to be a mixed company, drawn from all sorts of backgrounds. The frame let each tale belong to a teller, so that the stories revealed the people and the people explained the stories. It let the pilgrims quarrel and interrupt and answer one another. It turned a collection into one work.

In that single device you can see how far he had travelled. The courtly allegorist would never have needed it, because his figures were never meant to be people. Chaucer needed it because, by the end, people were what he cared about most.

## Draft B

Every poet begins by imitating, and Chaucer was no exception. In his youth he wrote as the fashion of his day required him to write; and the fashion of his day was allegory. It came to him, as it came to every cultivated Englishman of the time, out of the courtly culture of France--a culture of gardens and dreams and debates, in which the lover wandered through a painted landscape and met, not men and women, but Pity and Hope and Jealousy and Scorn, each with a label round its neck. The mode was graceful and ingenious, and it had a wonderful hold upon the minds of men. It lingered for centuries. Long after the courts that bred it had passed away, poets were still falling asleep and dreaming of abstractions in a garden.

To this fashion the young Chaucer paid his tribute, and paid it handsomely. But one feels, reading him, that he was never quite at home in it. He had too keen an eye for the actual. The personified virtues and vices were well enough, but they did not eat, or sweat, or quarrel about the reckoning; and it was precisely the eating and sweating and quarrelling that interested him. An abstraction has no digestion and no temper, and a man who has once looked with delight upon real faces will not be content for long with masks.

And so, as he grew older, he let the abstractions go. It was a quiet revolution, but it was a revolution. In place of Pity he gave us a particular person who was pitiful; in place of Avarice, a particular person who counted his money. He turned from the garden of the dream to the high road, and found there ordinary, grounded people--people with bodies and histories and opinions of their own--and he found them infinitely more entertaining than any figure in a tapestry. The allegorist had been a decorator; the new Chaucer was a painter from the life.

There remained a difficulty. He had a great many stories to tell, and they were of every kind--grave and gay, pious and ribald, tender and coarse. How were they to be bound together into one work? A heap of tales is not a book. The answer he found was a stroke of genius, and like most strokes of genius it looks obvious once it has been made. He sent his people on a pilgrimage. A pilgrimage brought together men and women of every sort, who would never otherwise have sat at one table; it gave them a road to travel and time to fill; and it gave each of them a reason to talk. The frame held the separate tales as a string holds beads, and the string itself--the talk, the quarrels, the interruptions by the way--became not the least precious part of the necklace.

## Draft C

Most poets, it is supposed, begin with the real world and wander off into dreams as they grow older. Chaucer did precisely the reverse. He began among dreams, and he ended among men and women. In his youth he wrote as the fashion of his time required, and that fashion was allegory. It had come across the sea from the courts of France, where lords and ladies, having little to do, amused themselves with elaborate fancies of love, in which the passions of the heart, dressed up as persons, walked and conversed like courtiers. The taste was a graceful one, and it was tenacious; it lingered in our literature for centuries after the courts that bred it had passed away, as the scent of a flower lingers in a room long after the flower is withered. The young poet, with his quick ear and his love of the fashionable, took to it naturally. [example needed: an early allegorical poem of his] He dreamed his dreams in the approved manner, and dreamed them well.

But there was something in him which the garden could not hold. The abstractions would not satisfy him for ever, and perhaps they had never quite satisfied him. What are these painted passions, after all, but the ghosts of real persons, drained of blood and dressed in allegorical robes? He had seen the persons themselves--in the street, at the inn, in the counting-house and the court--and by degrees he laid the ghosts aside and took up the living. In his later work the personified virtues and vices are gone, and in their place stand ordinary, solid, grounded people, who eat and drink and quarrel, who have trades and tempers and bad habits. The one manner shows you what a man ought to feel; the other shows you what a man is. Nobody who has read both can doubt which Chaucer came to prefer.

There remained a difficulty. A poet who deals in real persons deals in separate stories, and separate stories, however excellent, will not of themselves make a book; they lie side by side like beads without a string. It was here that his invention was happiest. He hit upon a pilgrimage. Just as a road draws travellers of every kind into one company for a few days, so the pilgrimage drew his tales into one design, and gave each a teller, an occasion and an audience. The frame is simple; nay, it is almost commonplace. Yet it is exactly what was wanted, and with it the poet of dreams became, once and for all, the poet of the road. Was ever a change of fashion better made?
