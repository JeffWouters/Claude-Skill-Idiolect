# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-04

## The passage (by the author)

It is evident that there were some points in Christianity that King Olaf did not appreciate. To cultivate these fruits of the spirit required men of a different temper. Their work is not all done yet. It is progressing.

There is one complication in the work of peacemaking which has not been sufficiently considered. It is the recurrence of Youth. I have listened to the arguments against war at a great Peace Congress. The reasoning was strong, the statement of facts conclusive. War was shown to be cruel and foolish, and incredibly expensive. The audience, consisting of right-minded and very intelligent people, was convinced of the justice of the cause of Peace. Why, then, does not the cause triumph?

In such cases I am in the habit of looking about with the intent to fix the responsibility where it belongs, on those who were not at the meeting. Mature life was well represented, but there was a suspicious absence of young men in the twenties. Ah! I said, there is the difficulty. We can’t be sure of lasting peace until we make it more interesting to these young absentees. They’ll all be peace men by and by, but meanwhile there is no knowing what trouble they may get us into.

John Fiske traced the influence which the prolongation of infancy has had on the progress of civilization. I am inclined to think that equally great results would flow from any discovery by which the period of middle age could be prolonged beyond its present term. War would be abolished without any more ado. A uniformly middle-aged community would be immune from any attack of militant fever.

It happens, however, that every once in a while the hot passions of youth carry all before them. The account of what happened at the beginning of the civil wars in Israel is typical. King Rehoboam called a meeting of the elder statesmen of his kingdom. They outlined a policy that was eminently conciliatory. But we are told, “He forsook the counsel of the old men which they had given him, and consulted with the young men that were grown up with him and which stood by him.”

## Draft A

The arguments against war are very good ones. They have been made for centuries by wise and humane people, and I have never heard a sound reply to most of them. War wastes wealth, destroys the young, rewards cruelty and settles nothing that could not have been settled more cheaply by other means. Every sensible person agrees. And yet the wars go on. It is worth asking why an argument that wins every debate should lose every practical contest.

Part of the answer, I think, can be seen by looking round the room at any meeting held in the cause of peace. The audience is respectable, thoughtful and earnest. It is also, for the most part, no longer young. The grey heads are there in numbers; the young men are not. They are somewhere else, and whatever they are doing, they are not listening to the case for arbitration.

This is not an accident. The case against war is an argument addressed to experience, to the kind of judgment that comes from having lived long enough to count costs. It persuades those who have already been persuaded by life. But wars are not fought by those people. They are fought by the young, who have not counted any costs yet and who find in the prospect of fighting something that no lecture on waste can answer.

One is tempted to a fanciful conclusion. If a society could somehow be kept permanently middle-aged, with nobody under forty and nobody over sixty, it might never go to war at all. Its citizens would be too prudent, too tired and too well acquainted with bills. Such a society cannot be arranged, and perhaps we should not want it if it could.

The difficulty is very old. Scripture tells of a king who, on coming to the throne, was given counsel by the elders who had served his father. They advised moderation. He turned from them to the young men he had grown up with, who advised him to be harsh, and he took their advice. The kingdom split, and civil war followed. The elders had the better argument. The young men had the king's ear. That, in small, is the whole history of the peace movement.

## Draft B

The arguments against war are excellent. I have heard them many times, and I have never heard them answered. They show that war is wasteful, that it is cruel, that it settles nothing which might not be settled better in some other way. Yet war goes on, and the arguments go on, and the two do not seem to interfere with each other. It is worth while to ask why.

I have attended a good many peace meetings, and I have noticed one thing about the audience. It is composed very largely of persons who have arrived at years of discretion, and some who have passed well beyond them. They are thoughtful people who listen with approval to what is said, and go home convinced of what they believed when they came. But the young men are not there. They are elsewhere, engaged in pursuits that have more of the element of risk in them. The argument is addressed to those who do not need it, and those who might need it are out of hearing.

This suggests a curious reflection. If a society could be kept permanently middle-aged, it might never fight at all. Its citizens would weigh the costs, count the chances, and conclude that the game was not worth the candle. They would have too much to lose and too little appetite for the losing. The trouble is that no society has yet discovered how to prevent its young men from being young.

Nor is the difficulty a new one. Scripture tells of a king who came to the throne and took counsel as to how he should govern. He first consulted the old men who had stood before his father, and they gave him prudent advice. Then he turned to the young men who had grown up with him, and they gave him advice of a more spirited kind. He followed the young men. The result was civil war and a divided kingdom.

The old men were right, as old men frequently are. But it is the misfortune of good counsel that it is usually given in a tone of voice which the young find it impossible to hear. The peace advocate who would succeed must learn not only what to say, but how to say it to those who are not in the habit of listening to him.

## Draft C

The arguments against war are excellent. They have been stated with great eloquence and at considerable length, and I have never heard a sensible answer to them. Yet war goes on, entirely unmoved by the logic that ought to have abolished it long ago. Why?

Look at the audience of any meeting for the promotion of peace. It is composed of middle-aged and elderly persons, men and women of settled habits and moderate appetites, who agree with every word the speaker utters and who had agreed with it, for that matter, before he began. The young men are not present. They are elsewhere, doing whatever young men do on an evening when there is nothing in particular to fight about, and if you were to go out and invite them in to hear the case for international arbitration you would be received with a politeness that did not promise much. The arguments are addressed to the very people who least need them; the people who need them are not listening.

This suggests a remedy, though not a practical one. If a society could be kept permanently middle-aged, it would never go to war at all. Its citizens would weigh the costs, count the taxes, and consider the inconvenience to trade. They would feel their rheumatism at the mere thought of a long march. War would seem to them what it seems to the peace society, an obviously unprofitable enterprise. But nature has made no provision for such a society. Every year a new generation arrives in excellent health, with no taxes to pay, and it has not attended the meetings.

The difficulty is an ancient one. Scripture tells of a king who, on coming to the throne, first took counsel with the elders who had served his father, and they advised him to deal gently with the people. Then he turned from the old men and asked the young men who had grown up with him. They advised severity, and the kingdom was split by civil war. It is a short story. It is also a very modern one. The elders had the better argument, the young men had the king's ear, and the argument went home unheard.
