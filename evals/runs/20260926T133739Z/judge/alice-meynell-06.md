# Judge packet alice-meynell-06

## The passage (by the author)

What, then? Is the record of the race nothing but a bundle of such little times? Nay, it seems that childhood, which created the illusion of ages, does actually prove it true. Childhood is itself Antiquity--to every man his only Antiquity. The recollection of childhood cannot make Abraham old again in the mind of a man of thirty-five; but the beginning of every life is older than Abraham. There is the abyss of time. Let a man turn to his own childhood--no further--if he would renew his sense of remoteness, and of the mystery of change.

For in childhood change does not go at that mere hasty amble; it rushes; but it has enormous space for its flight. The child has an apprehension not only of things far off, but of things far apart; an illusive apprehension when he is learning "ancient" history--a real apprehension when he is conning his own immeasurable infancy. If there is no historical Antiquity worth speaking of, this is the renewed and unnumbered Antiquity for all mankind.

And it is of this--merely of this--that "ancient" history seems to partake. Rome was founded when we began Roman history, and that is why it seems long ago. Suppose the man of thirty-five heard, at that present age, for the first time of Romulus. Why, Romulus would be nowhere. But he built his wall, as a matter of fact, when every one was seven years old. It is by good fortune that "ancient" history is taught in the only ancient days. So, for a time, the world is magical.

Modern history does well enough for learning later. But by learning something of antiquity in the first ten years, the child enlarges the sense of time for all mankind. For even after the great illusion is over and history is re-measured, and all fancy and flight caught back and chastised, the enlarged sense remains enlarged. The man remains capable of great spaces of time. He will not find them in Egypt, it is true, but he finds them within, he contains them, he is aware of them. History has fallen together, but childhood surrounds and encompasses history, stretches beyond and passes on the road to eternity.

## Draft A

Each of us has his own antiquity, and it is not the antiquity of the books. It is his childhood. There, and not in any buried city, lies the oldest thing he knows. The heroes of the ancient world, the founders and the conquerors, are not so old to him as the child who once stood at a window in his own house and watched the rain. They are dated; that child is not. They have their centuries set down against them; his first years have no date but "long ago," and "long ago" is a greater distance than any number.

Nor is this a mere fancy of the old man looking back. It is the very means by which he came to believe in the age of history at all. For he learnt his history as a child, and learnt it in the time-scale of a child. When he was first told of some king who reigned in a far age, or of some city that fell before any record of his own people, he placed these things in a past already immense--a past that was immense because his own few years were immense. [example needed] The remoteness of early history is, in great part, the remoteness of the schoolroom in which it was heard. We did not measure those ages; we lent them our own infinity.

It is true that, as we grow, history shortens. The centuries come nearer. The grown man knows, by a simple sum, that a few long lives laid end to end would carry him back to an age he once thought fabulous. He knows it, and the knowledge is real. And yet it does not wholly prevail. There remains beneath the arithmetic an older sense, a sense that was formed first and has never been undone. It is not possible to have been a child without keeping, somewhere, a child's scale of time. The man may reason that the past is short; he will still feel, now and then, that it is vast. Almost--not quite--his reason has conquered; and the part it has not conquered is the part that makes antiquity worth the name.

## Draft B

There is no antiquity like the antiquity of one's own beginning. The first years of a life, looked back upon from the middle of it, are older than the oldest name in the chronicles--older than any king, any prophet, any hero of whom the schoolbooks tell. The man who reads of some ancient figure knows well enough, by the arithmetic, how many centuries divide him from that figure; but he does not feel those centuries as he feels the distance between himself and the child he was. That distance is the genuine measure of deep time for every one of us, and it is the only measure that is felt rather than calculated.

Why does early history seem so remote? Not because it is remote, but because we learned it when we were children. The first dynasties, the first empires, the first wars, were taught to us in years that were themselves immense, and they took on the scale of the years in which we received them. They were placed at the far end of a long corridor, and the length of the corridor was the length of a childhood. We have never since been able to bring them nearer. What was learned in the country of great time keeps the dimensions of that country.

And this enlarged sense survives, in a curious fashion, into adult life. The grown man knows that history has grown shorter to him; his decades pass quickly, the centuries are to him now only a handful of lifetimes, and the past has lost its old majesty. Yet beneath this reasonable contraction the childish scale persists. The earliest things he learned are still, somewhere within him, as far away as they ever were--or farther. He carries two clocks, and they do not agree. The one tells him that the past is near; the other, older and more obstinate, tells him that it is immeasurably distant. It is the second, I suspect, that he believes.

## Draft C

Each of us carries a private antiquity, and it is not found in any history book. It is our own childhood. When I reach back to the first years of my life, I find something that feels older than any ancient figure I have read about. The kings and philosophers of the distant past seem, by comparison, oddly recent. They are people I learned about; my early childhood is a place I came from, and it lies at a depth that no date can match.

This is not only a trick of personal memory. I suspect it is also the reason early history seems so remote to us in the first place. We learn it as children. The oldest civilisations, the first empires, the founding stories of our own country: all of these usually reach us when we are very young, at the same time as our earliest memories are being laid down. They are filed in the same deep drawer. So the ancient world takes on the colour of childhood itself, that sense of immense distance and dim, golden light. We think of these periods as far away partly because we first met them when everything was far away.

What interests me is that this enlarged sense of time survives. As an adult I know perfectly well that history is shorter than it once seemed. I can count the generations, and I know that a few centuries is not so very long. My adult years pass quickly, and when I measure history against them it shrinks. Yet the old feeling does not disappear. Somewhere beneath the arithmetic, the far past still feels majestic and remote, because it is still connected to the part of me that first imagined it.

I think this is worth holding on to. The knowledge that history is short is useful, but it is not the whole truth of how we experience it. Our childhood gave us a measure of deep time that adult life cannot quite replace, and every time we look back at the ancient world we are also, without meaning to, looking back at ourselves.
