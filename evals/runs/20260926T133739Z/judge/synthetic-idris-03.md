## The passage (by the author)

There is a curious psychology to all this. A deadline that is widely understood to be arbitrary can nevertheless exert enormous force, because once it has been announced it acquires a reputational dimension; missing it would mean admitting that the original plan was wrong, and the people who made the plan may be more invested in its correctness than in its outcome. You have perhaps seen this dynamic play out, in which a date that everyone privately believes is unrealistic is publicly defended until the last possible moment, at which point it is quietly moved and the whole exercise begins again. The consequence is a kind of institutional theatre, in which the deadline functions less as a planning tool than as a performance of confidence. I suspect that most participants in this theatre understand its nature perfectly well; they simply feel unable to be the one who stops the performance.

Is there a better way to relate to deadlines? It seems to me that the most important step is simply to be honest about their origins — to ask, whenever a date is proposed, what would actually happen if it were missed, and to allow the answer to shape how seriously the date is treated. A deadline tied to an unmovable external event deserves one kind of response; a deadline chosen because it seemed like a reasonable target deserves another, and conflating the two does a disservice to everyone involved. Perhaps we might also learn to renegotiate dates earlier and more openly, treating a revised deadline not as a failure of planning but as the natural result of learning more about the work. None of this is technically difficult, and none of it requires new tools or elaborate methods. What makes it difficult is the reluctance to disappoint, which is, in some sense, a reluctance to be honest.

## Draft A

There is a particular moment in the life of a project when a target date stops being an estimate and becomes something closer to a public commitment, usually the moment it is spoken aloud in a meeting or written into a presentation that others will remember. It may be that this is where most of our trouble with dates begins. I suspect the date itself matters less than what becomes attached to it.

Consider what happens once a date has been announced. Reputations begin to gather around it; the person who proposed it becomes associated with it, the team that accepted it feels bound by it, and the manager who repeated it upwards now has something personal at stake in its survival. A date that was always doubtful is then defended, not because anyone believes in it more than before but because abandoning it would feel like an admission. Is it any wonder that such dates are held long after the evidence has turned against them?

It seems to me that the more useful question is what missing a given date would actually cost, and to whom. Some dates are imposed from outside, by a contract or an event that will not move, and these deserve considerable respect; others were chosen, arguably somewhat arbitrarily, and can be moved without much harm if the conversation happens early enough. Treating the two alike is a mistake, since it lends chosen dates an authority they never had.

The earlier a date is renegotiated, the cheaper the renegotiation tends to be. What prevents it, in the end, is rarely the practical difficulty of changing a plan but the fear of letting people down, which is a far more personal hurdle. Perhaps the courage required is not to hit the date but to say, while there is still time, that it will not be hit.

## Draft B

A target date, once announced, has a curious way of becoming something more than a date. It begins as a reasonable guess about when a piece of work might be finished; it is shared in a meeting, written into a slide, mentioned to a customer; and before long it has acquired a life of its own, carried along by people who had no part in setting it and who may not know how tentative it originally was. By the time doubts begin to appear, the date is no longer simply a date. It has become attached to the reputations of those who first spoke it aloud, and a date that carries a reputation is a date that will be defended long after the evidence has turned against it.

What might a more honest relationship with such dates look like? One useful question is simply to ask what missing the date would actually cost. The answer is sometimes considerable, and sometimes, on reflection, surprisingly modest. It matters, too, where the date came from. A date imposed from outside, by a regulation or a contract or an event that will happen regardless, deserves to be treated differently from one the team chose for itself, which is a commitment of its own making and can, in principle, be remade.

Where a date does need to move, it is almost always better to say so early, while there is still room for others to adjust their own plans. Late renegotiation is costly precisely because it leaves no such room.

Yet I suspect the real obstacle here is neither analytical nor procedural. Most of us know perfectly well when a date has become unrealistic. What holds us back is the fear of letting people down — of being the one who has to admit that the promise will not be kept — and that fear, rather than any lack of information, is what allows doubtful dates to survive for as long as they do.

## Draft C

A target date starts as a guess. Then someone announces it, and it begins to take on a life of its own.

Part of the reason is simple: once a date has been said out loud, reputations get tied to it. The person who proposed it, the manager who repeated it upward, the team that nodded along. Each of them now has something to lose if the date slips. So when doubts appear, as they usually do, the date gets defended rather than questioned. People work around the evidence instead of reacting to it, and the date keeps standing long after most of those involved privately stopped believing in it.

A more useful habit is to ask what missing the date would actually cost. Not in the abstract, but concretely. Would a customer be let down? Would another team be blocked? Would anything happen at all? Often the honest answer is less dramatic than the anxiety around the date suggests.

It also helps to treat different kinds of dates differently. Some dates come from outside: a contract, a regulation, an event that will happen whether we are ready or not. Those deserve to be taken seriously, and the conversation is about scope and trade-offs. Other dates are chosen. We picked them, often for reasonable motives, but we could also pick again. Treating a chosen date with the gravity of an external one is where much of the unnecessary pain comes from.

When a date is in trouble, renegotiate early. The earlier you raise it, the more options everyone has, and the less it feels like a failure. Raising it late leaves nothing but apologies.

None of this is hard to understand. What makes it hard to do is something else: the fear of letting people down. That fear keeps people quiet until the problem can no longer be hidden. It is the true hurdle, and naming it is the first step toward getting past it.
