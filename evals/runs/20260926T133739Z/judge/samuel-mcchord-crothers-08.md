# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-08

## The passage (by the author)

The manner of narration savored of the soil. The Honest Miner under such circumstances would subordinate everything to emphasis on the correct homiletical method. No matter how able the minister might be, it was evident that if he were closely confined to his notes, his delivery could not be effective.

A good woman described the way in which her minister, a young man fresh from the theological school, made one of his first parish calls. He found his parishioner, who had been extolled as one of the pillars of the church, in a state of intoxication, and he was chased out of the house and some distance down the street.

“We were sorry it happened, for it gave him an unpleasant impression of the congregation. You know Mr. ---- met with several rebuffs.”

The unconventional episode was related with all the prim propriety of “Cranford.”

The perfect democracy of a mining camp develops a certain naïve truth-telling, which has all the unexpectedness which belongs to the observations of a boy. There is no attempt to reduce everything to uniformity, or to prove any particular thesis. The gossip of a conventional village where people know each other too well is apt to be malicious. A creditable action is narrated, and then comes the inevitable “but.” The subject of conversation falls in the estimation of the hearers with a sudden thud.

The Honest Miner does not attempt to pass final judgment or to arrange his fellow men according to any sort of classification. He speaks of them as he sees them, and so virtues and failings jostle one another and take no offense. The result is a moral inconsequence which has all the effect of studied wit. This is what delights us in the characterization of Thompson of Angel’s:

Frequently drunk was Thompson, but always polite to the stranger.

## Draft A

In a Western mining settlement the art of storytelling is held in high esteem, and it is practised everywhere, from the saloon to the church. The people there have strong views about how a thing ought to be told, and they are not shy of expressing them.

This comes out plainly in their taste in preaching. They want a man who stands up and talks to them. A preacher who reads his sermon from notes, however learned the notes may be, is at a great disadvantage; the congregation feels that it is being read at rather than spoken to. The man they admire is the one who throws his notes away, or never had any, and pours out his sermon with life and heat. They will forgive him a great deal of bad grammar for the sake of a lively delivery.

The same instinct governs their gossip, which is of a peculiar kind. A miner describing a neighbor tells you exactly what he thinks of him, and he does it without the small spiteful qualifications that season gossip in more polished places. He does not say that a man is honest, but. He says the man is honest, and then, in the next breath and in the same tone, he tells you something else about him that is not honest at all.

The effect is that good and bad traits are set down side by side, like goods on a counter, and neither is allowed to cancel the other. The speaker passes no judgment. He is simply giving an inventory. A man may be described as the kindest fellow in the camp, a devoted nurse to anyone who falls sick, and also the best hand at marking a deck of cards, all in one sentence and with equal approval. The result is often very funny, though the speaker never intends it to be. He is not trying to amuse you. He is only telling the truth as he sees it, and the truth, laid out so candidly, turns out to be a comedy.

## Draft B

In a mining settlement the art of conversation is taken seriously, and so is the art of the sermon. The miners, as I found, are exacting critics of both. A preacher who comes among them with his discourse written out and reads it from his notes will be listened to politely and forgotten before he has reached the door. What they want is a man who speaks as if the thing had just occurred to him and could not wait. They value a lively sermon as they value a lively story, and for the same reason: it sounds like somebody talking.

Their own talk about one another has a quality I have rarely met elsewhere. It is perfectly candid, for when a miner describes his neighbour he tells you what the man is, and he does not trouble to soften it with those spiteful qualifications which polite society uses to make a compliment do the work of an insult. There is no "of course he means well" and no "I would not say a word against him." He simply states the facts as he has observed them.

The result is sometimes startling. Good and bad traits are set down side by side, like entries in a ledger that nobody intends to balance. A man will be described as [example needed: a virtue and a vice set side by side], and the speaker will pass from one item to the next without any change of tone. He is not judging, but reporting.

"But is not this rather comic?" asks the reader. It is, though the humour is entirely unintended. The miner has no notion that he has said anything amusing; he is merely being accurate, and accuracy about human nature, when it is not arranged into a moral, has a way of sounding like a joke. I should not wish to correct him. Most of us could do with a neighbour who describes us so fairly.

## Draft C

In a Western mining settlement talk is the chief amusement, and the people are good judges of it. They know what they like, and they are not shy about saying so. I found that they had very decided views about preaching. They wanted the preacher to talk to them, not to read to them. A man who came into the pulpit with his notes was at a disadvantage from the start. They listened to him politely, but it was the politeness of men waiting for something better. The preacher who could stand up and speak out of his head, with a little fire in him, had them at once. They did not care so much whether he was learned as whether he was alive.

Their own conversation had the same quality. When a miner undertook to describe one of his neighbors, he did it with a candor that took my breath away. He said just what he thought, and he said it all. But there was no malice in it. He did not lower his voice, or look over his shoulder, or add those little qualifications which in more polished society are used to make a compliment carry a sting. He was not trying to injure anybody. He was only giving information.

The result was a kind of portraiture that I have never met elsewhere. The good traits and the bad were set down side by side, with no attempt to reconcile them or to pass judgment on either. A man would be described as the best hand with a drill in the camp and the worst hand with other people's property, all in one breath, and in the same tone of voice. [example needed] The speaker saw nothing odd in the combination. He had simply reported what he had observed.

The humor of it was entirely unintended, and for that reason all the more delightful. The miner did not know that he was being funny. He was only being truthful, and truthfulness, when it is thorough enough, is one of the most amusing things in the world.
