# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-08

## The passage (by the author)

I was staying the other day in the house of an old friend, a public man, who is a deeply interesting character, energetic, able, vigorous, with very definite limitations. The only male guest in the house, it so happened, was also an old friend of mine, a serious man. One night, when we were all three in the smoking-room, our host rose, and excused himself, saying that he had some letters to write. When he was gone, I said to my serious friend: "What an interesting fellow our host is! He is almost more interesting because of the qualities that he does not possess, than because of the qualities that he does possess." My companion, who is remarkable for his power of blunt statement, looked at me gravely, and said: "If you propose to discuss our host, you must find some one else to conduct the argument; he is my friend, whom I esteem and love, and I am not in a position to criticise him." I laughed, and said: "Well, he is my friend, too, and I esteem and love him; and that is the very reason why I should like to discuss him. Nothing that either you or I could say would make me love him less; but I wish to understand him. I have a very clear impression of him, and I have no doubt you have a very clear impression too; yet we should probably differ about him in many points, and I should like to see what light you could throw upon his character." My companion said: "No; it is inconsistent with my idea of loyalty to criticise my friends. Besides, you know I am an old-fashioned person, and I disapprove of criticising people altogether. I think it is a violation of the ninth commandment; I do not think we are justified in bearing false witness against our neighbour."

## Draft A

I was staying, on one occasion, in the house of a public man, a figure of some mark in the world, and I found myself greatly interested in his character. It was not a malicious interest; it was rather the natural curiosity which one feels about any striking and complicated nature, the desire to understand how the qualities fitted together, where the strength lay and where the weakness. There was another guest in the house, and one evening I proposed to him, half in jest and half in earnest, that we should sit down and analyse our host together.

He declined at once, and with some warmth. It would be disloyal, he said, to discuss a friend behind his back, and above all a friend under whose roof we were sleeping; and he went further, and maintained that to criticise anybody at all was a breach of the commandment against bearing false witness, since no one could ever know enough of the heart of another to judge him truly.

I respected the scruple, but I could not share it. Is it really disloyal to try to understand a friend? I do not think so, and I have never been able to think so; one may discuss a man's character with perfect affection, as one might discuss the lines of a hill one loves, or the moods of a familiar sea; the talk need not diminish him, and it may very well deepen the regard in which he is held. Of course there is a kind of analysis which is merely a polite name for backbiting, and my fellow-guest was right to be on his guard against it. But the application of the commandment seemed to me to prove far too much; for if every judgement of another is false witness, then all biography, all history, and half of friendship itself must be condemned together. Yet I think of his refusal, when I recall it, with a certain uneasy admiration, as one thinks of a door closed quietly in the face of an intruder.

## Draft B

I once stayed for some days in the house of a well-known public figure. [name needed] There was another guest staying at the same time, and at one point, out of our host's hearing, I suggested that we talk him over. I did not mean anything malicious by it. I was simply interested. Here was a man whom most people knew only from a distance, and we had been given the chance to see him close to, at his own table and in his own habits. It seemed natural to compare notes: what we had noticed, what surprised us, how the private man squared with the public one.

The other guest would not do it. He said, quite pleasantly but firmly, that we were our host's guests and that it would be disloyal to sit in his house and take him to pieces behind his back. I was a little taken aback, and a little ashamed, though I was not sure I agreed.

He went further than that, too. It was not only the host he would not discuss. He held that to criticise anybody at all, host or not, was to break the commandment against bearing false witness. He was quite serious about it. [his reasoning needed]

I have thought about that conversation a good deal since. I respect the loyalty, and I think he was right that there is something ugly in accepting a man's hospitality and then dissecting him. But I cannot quite go with him the whole way. Friends do talk about one another, and not always unkindly. To try to understand a person, including their faults, seems to me one of the ways we take them seriously. The line, I suppose, lies in the spirit of it: whether we talk in order to understand or in order to diminish.

## Draft C

I was staying, some time ago, in the house of a man of considerable public note, [example needed: who he was] and there was another guest there, an old friend of our host's, with whom I found myself alone one evening. Now our host was a very interesting person; there was a great deal in him that puzzled me, a mixture of qualities that did not seem to fit together; and it appeared to me the most natural thing in the world to talk him over, to compare impressions, and to try to arrive at some notion of what he really was. I made a beginning, I remember, with some remark about his manner.

But my companion would have none of it. He said, quite gently but very firmly, that he did not think it right to discuss a friend in his absence, and least of all under his own roof; that it was a matter of loyalty; and that he would rather not go on. I was a little abashed, and a little nettled too; for I had meant no harm, and had intended nothing but a friendly curiosity.

He went further than this, however, as we talked. He held that any criticism of another person, however mild and however just, was a breach of the commandment against bearing false witness; because, as he said, one could never know the whole of another's heart, and so whatever one said was bound to be in some degree untrue.

I have thought of this a good deal since, and I cannot altogether agree with him. It seems to me that to speak of a friend with affection and fairness, and to try to understand him, is not disloyal at all; it is one of the ways in which friendship shows itself. And if every judgment of another is to be counted false witness, then we must give up judging altogether, which is impossible. Yet I respect the scruple; and I confess that it made me look a little more carefully at my own motives.
