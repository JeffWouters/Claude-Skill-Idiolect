# Judge packet synthetic-noor-07

## The passage (by the author)

I take the 7.42 train. I have taken it for six years.

The walk to the station is 14 minutes. I have timed it. With a heavy bag it is 16. In rain it is 12, because I walk faster and take the short cut past the bins.

So I leave the house at 7.25. That gives me three minutes of slack. Three minutes is the right amount. Less and I run. More and I stand on the platform in the cold.

The platform has a shape I know by heart. The front two carriages stop by the yellow bench. The middle stop by the clock. The back ones stop past the end of the roof, where you get wet. I stand by the clock. The doors open right in front of me. Most days.

There is a crowd of regulars. We do not speak. But we know each other.

There is the man in the green coat who reads the paper folded in four. There is the woman with two phones, one in each hand. There is a boy with a cello case taller than he is. He gets off after two stops. I have never heard him play. But I think about the cello most mornings.

The train is late one day in five. I have kept count, on and off. Mostly by two or three minutes. Once by 40. That was the day of the fallen tree. We stood on the platform and watched the board change. Delayed. Delayed. Then a new time. Then delayed again.

And the odd thing about that day: nobody left. Nobody went for a bus. We just stood there. A few people rang work. The man in the green coat read his whole paper, both sides.

I have a seat I like. Left side, facing forward, by the window. The left side gets the sun in winter. In summer I move to the right.

My mornings run on a timetable, and the most important part of it is the train. Over time I have tuned my departure from home so that I reach the platform with a small margin to spare, just a few minutes, not so many that I stand around for ages and not so few that a slow traffic light or an untied shoelace puts the whole day at risk. One morning the wait went on far longer than usual. [Details of the delay needed.] The announcements came and went, and the minutes of margin were long gone. What struck me was that nobody gave up. No one headed for the exit to find another way in. The regulars stayed where they were, the musician included, all waiting with the same patience, as if the routine itself held us together. Eventually the train came, and we got on as we always did, in silence.

I think about that wait more than I would have expected. It showed me that a routine is not just a schedule. It is a small, shared agreement among strangers.

That is the part I think of most. Not the wait. The way nobody left.

This morning I left on the minute. The musician was at the door. We did not speak. The train was on time.
