## The passage (by the author)

Here is the part I like most. When a job is done, I do not rub it out. I cross it through. One firm line. So by the end of a week the list is full of lines. Some weeks, a lot of lines. Some weeks, two.

When the page is full, I take it down. I put it in a box file in the hall cupboard. Then I start a new one. I carry over the jobs that are not done.

The box file has about 200 old lists in it now. I looked through them last winter. It was strange. Like a diary, but only the dull parts.

And the dull parts told a story.

The same jobs came back again and again. Clean the gutter: twice a year, every year. Bleed the radiators: every October. The back gate: oiled 11 times in 15 years. It still squeaks.

Some jobs sat on the list for a long time. Paint the hall was carried over 34 times. That is more than two years. I did it in the end, in one weekend. It took less time than it took to keep writing it down.

Some jobs just vanished. Fix the doorbell. It sat there for months. Then one list it was gone. I have no memory of fixing it. I checked. The doorbell works. So I must have done it. Or someone did.

And the food part was the saddest and the best. You can see the years in it. Baby food, then fish fingers, then crisps and cola in large amounts. Then, for a few years, oat milk. Then less of everything, as the house got quieter.

I keep the lists for no good reason. I will not read them often. But I do not throw them out.

This morning the new list went up. Top part: milk, onions, bin bags. Middle part: fix the drip in the shower, sort the screw jars. Bottom part: paint the hall. Again.

## Draft A

I keep every chore list we have ever made. All of them. In a box.

It started by accident. A list goes up in the kitchen. Jobs get done and I strike them out. One line through each. Not a scribble. A line, so you can still read what it was. When the sheet is full, it comes down. And I could never throw a full one away. So it went in a drawer. Then the drawer filled up. Then the box.

I do not read them often. But when I do, they tell me things I did not know about us.

First the jobs that come back. The same task, struck out, then there again a few weeks later. [example needed: a job that keeps coming back]. You do a job, and it does not stay done. That is most of a house.

Then the jobs that move. Written on one sheet, not struck out. Copied onto the next. And the next. Some of them crossed sheet after sheet before anyone did them. Some never got done at all. They stop turning up. Nobody decided that.

And then the jobs we forgot. They were on one list only. Never struck out. Never copied. Gone.

But the grocery lines are the best part. They are a record of the family. The early sheets are short. Then there is more of everything as the children grow. Longer lists. Bigger packs. And then, sheet by sheet, less again. Smaller packs. Fewer lines. One by one, they left home. You can see it in the shopping.

Tonight there is a new sheet in the kitchen. A few lines so far. None struck out yet.

## Draft C

Somewhere in our house there is a stack of old chore lists, and it has become one of the more revealing things we own. The system behind it is simple. Tasks go on a sheet, and when a task is done it gets struck through with a line rather than erased. When a sheet is full, it is not thrown away. It goes onto the pile with the others, and the pile has quietly grown over the years into an archive.

I did not keep them with any purpose in mind. It just seemed wrong to throw away a page that had so much finished work on it. Only recently did I sit down and read through them, and the experience was stranger and more moving than I expected.

The first thing that stands out is repetition. Some tasks appear on sheet after sheet, not because they were never done but because they always come back: [example needed]. Reading them in sequence, you can feel the rhythm of a household, the same jobs rolling round like seasons.

Then there are the postponed tasks, the ones that were copied from one list to the next without ever being crossed off. You can follow them across months, sometimes longer, moving from sheet to sheet like a guest nobody quite gets round to seeing. Some were finally struck through. Others simply stop appearing, and I honestly cannot tell whether they were done, dropped or forgotten. Those forgotten tasks are oddly touching. Each one was urgent enough to write down once.

But the part that caught me most was the grocery entries. Over the years they trace the family itself. Early lists hold the things you buy when children are small: [example needed]. Later the quantities grow, and the items change as tastes change and appetites increase. Then, sheet by sheet, the lists get shorter again. Certain items disappear. The amounts shrink back. Without anyone writing it down directly, the lists record children growing up and, eventually, leaving home.

I had thought I was keeping a record of chores. It turns out I was keeping a record of us.
