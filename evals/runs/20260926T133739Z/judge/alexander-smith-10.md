# Judge packet alexander-smith-10

## The passage (by the author)

Had Chaucer spent his whole life in seeking, he could not have selected a better contemporary circumstance for securing variety of character than a pilgrimage to Canterbury. It comprises, as we see, all kinds and conditions of people. It is the fourteenth-century England in little. In our time, the only thing that could match it in this respect is Epsom down on the great race-day. But then Epsom down is too unwieldy; the crowd is too great, and it does not cohere, save for the few seconds when gay jackets are streaming towards the winning-post. The Prologue to the "Canterbury Tales," in which we make the acquaintance of the pilgrims, is the ripest, most genial and humourous, altogether the most masterly thing which Chaucer has left us. In its own way, and within its own limits, it is the most wonderful thing in the language. The people we read about are as real as the people we brush clothes with in the street,--nay, much more real; for we not only see their faces, and the fashion and texture of their garments, we know also what they think, how they express themselves, and with what eyes they look out on the world. Chaucer's art in this Prologue is simple perfection. He indulges in no irrelevant description, he airs no fine sentiments, he takes no special pains as to style or poetic ornament; but every careless touch tells, every sly line reveals character; the description of each man's horse-furniture and array reads like a memoir. The Nun's pretty oath bewrays her. We see the bold, well-favoured countenance of the Wife of Bath beneath her hat, as "broad as a buckler or a targe"; and the horse of the Clerk, "as lean as is a rake," tells tales of his master's cheer. Our modern dress is worthless as an indication of the character, or even of the social rank, of the wearer; in the olden time it was significant of personal tastes and appetites, of profession, and condition of life generally. See how Chaucer brings out a character by touching merely on a few points of attire and personal appearance:--

## Draft A

Of all the frames a poet ever devised, none was happier than the pilgrimage. Rank and calling were no bar to it. The great and the small, the pious and the worldly, the learned and the ignorant, took the same road and put up at the same inn; and for the space of a journey they were fellows. A pilgrimage was a nation in little. Gather such a company together, set them on horseback, and you have the whole of society riding past your window--every class represented, every trade, every temper; and you have only to look and set down what you see.

This is what Chaucer did in the prologue to his pilgrimage poem, and it is, I think, the finest thing he ever did. Other parts of the work may be more moving, or more amusing, or more sweetly told; but nowhere else is there such a wealth of character packed into so small a room. One by one the pilgrims come forward, and one by one they are drawn--not with a flourish, but with a few quiet strokes that leave nothing to be added. When he has done, they are no longer names; they are acquaintances. We could pick them out of a crowd.

And the secret of his art lies very much in the small things. He does not analyse his people; he dresses them. He tells us what they wore, how they rode, what they carried, what they ate and how they ate it--and out of these trifles, as out of so many coloured threads, the whole nature of the wearer is woven before our eyes. A garment too fine for the purse that bought it, a hood worn a certain way, a habit at table [example needed]--each is a window through which we look straight into a soul. It is the method of the born observer, who knows that a man betrays himself far more in his buttons than in his professions. We may lie with our tongues; our clothes are apt to tell the truth.

So the prologue stands at the gate of the poem like a gallery of portraits at the door of a great house. We linger in it, and are loth to pass on; and when at last we do pass on, it is with the pleasant sense of travelling in excellent company.

## Draft B

A pilgrimage, in the ordinary way of thinking, is a religious exercise; to Chaucer it was something more useful to a poet--an occasion on which every rank of society might meet upon equal terms. The gentleman and the labourer, the churchman and the layman, the scholar and the tradesman, all ride the same road, eat at the same table, and listen to the same stories; and the little company, gathered together by chance, becomes in his hands a miniature of the whole nation. Nowhere else in our older literature do we see so many kinds of men and women together, and nowhere else are they so thoroughly alive. The Prologue in which he introduces them is, I think, his finest achievement. Other poets have told better tales; no poet has ever drawn a gallery of portraits so various, so exact, so crowded with character, in so small a space. He does not describe his travellers at length, for he has no need to. A word about a hood, a line about a horse, a glance at a purse or a pair of shoes, and the whole person stands before you, as a skilful draughtsman, with a few strokes of the pencil, will give you a face you would know again in a crowd. Clothing, in his hands, becomes a kind of involuntary confession. The vain betray their vanity in their finery, the careful their care in their neatness; the one pilgrim is too gay for his calling, the other too shabby for his learning; and the small details, set down with an air of perfect innocence, tell you more of each traveller's nature than a chapter of analysis. It is both, and neither; it is simply the truth, seen by an eye that missed nothing and forgave almost everything. The pilgrims set out, and we go with them gladly; for we have met them all before, in the street and the market, and we are delighted to find that a poet has met them too!

## Draft C

Chaucer's pilgrimage poem opens with a prologue, and the prologue is the place to begin any account of what he achieved.

The idea behind it is simple and brilliant. A pilgrimage was one of the few occasions on which people of every social class might find themselves on the same road, in the same inn, at the same table. The great and the humble, the religious and the worldly, the learned and the ignorant, all set out together for the same shrine. By choosing a pilgrimage as his frame, Chaucer gave himself a whole society to work with. His company of travellers is a miniature nation, and in describing them one by one he describes the country they came from.

The prologue is, I think, his finest achievement. The tales that follow have their own great strengths, but it is here, in these introductions, that his gift for character shows most richly. Each pilgrim is given only a short portrait, and yet each is distinct. We could not confuse one with another. They have their own vanities, habits, weaknesses and quiet virtues, and by the end of the prologue we feel we have met them, not merely read about them.

Much of this is done through small things. Chaucer rarely tells us outright what a person is like. Instead he shows us what they wear, how they ride, what they carry, how they keep themselves. Clothing does a great deal of the work. A garment that is too fine for its owner's station, or too carefully kept, or worn and neglected, tells us more than any direct judgement could. So does the small detail that seems at first to be thrown in by chance: a habit of speech, a piece of jewellery, a particular way of eating. [example needed] Each of these details is chosen, and each reveals something of the traveller's nature, often something the traveller would rather have kept hidden.

The effect is that we come to know the pilgrims the way we know people in life: from the outside in, by noticing. Chaucer trusts his readers to notice, and his readers have been rewarded for it ever since.
