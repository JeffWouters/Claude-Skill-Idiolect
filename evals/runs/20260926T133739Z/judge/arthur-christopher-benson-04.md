## The passage (by the author)

There is less excuse in a University town than in any other for adopting this pompous and formal view of the duties of society, because there are very few unoccupied people in such a place. My own occupations, such as they are, fill the hours from breakfast to luncheon and from tea to dinner; men of sedentary lives, who do a good deal of brainwork, find an hour or two of exercise and fresh air a necessity in the afternoon. Indeed, a man who cares about his work, and who regards it as a primary duty, finds no occupation more dispiriting, more apt to unfit him for serious work, than pacing from house to house in the early afternoon, delivering a pack of visiting-cards, varied by a perfunctory conversation, seated at the edge of an easy-chair, on subjects of inconceivable triviality. Of course there are men so constituted that they find this pastime a relief and a pleasure; but their felicity of temperament ought not to be made into a rule for serious-minded men. The only social institution which might really prove beneficial in a University is an informal evening salon. If people might drop in uninvited, in evening dress or not, as was convenient, from nine to ten in the evening, at a pleasant house, it would be a rational practice; but few such experiments seem ever to be tried.

Moreover, the one thing that is fatal to all spontaneous social enjoyment is that the guests should, like the maimed and blind in the parable, be compelled to come in. The frame of mind of an eminent Cabinet Minister whom I once accompanied to an evening party rises before my mind. He was in deep depression at having to go; and when I ventured to ask his motive in going, he said, with an air of unutterable self-sacrifice, "I suppose that we ought sometimes to be ready to submit to the tortures we inflict on others." Imagine a circle of guests assembled in such a frame of mind, and it would seem that one had all the materials for a thoroughly pleasant party.

## Draft A

Anyone who has lived in a college town will know the peculiar tyranny of the afternoon call. There is a whole code of it: who must call on whom, and when, and for how long, and how soon the call must be returned. New arrivals must be visited; the visit must be repaid; cards must be left in the right order and the right number. It is all very correct, and it seems to me a great waste.

The people most burdened by it are the very people who can least afford the time. An academic's working life depends on long stretches of concentration, and the afternoon is often the best part of the day for it. To break it off in order to put on a good coat, walk across town, sit for a quarter of an hour in a drawing room exchanging remarks about the weather, and then walk back again, is to lose not just the hour itself but most of the energy that would have gone into the evening's work. Multiply that by a season's worth of obligations and the cost is considerable. [example needed]

It would be so much more sensible to do things differently. Suppose a household simply let it be known that on a certain evening each week they would be at home, and that anyone who cared to drop in would be welcome. No cards, no ledger of visits owed, no offence if you did not come. People would turn up when they felt like it and leave when they had had enough. The talk would be better, because everyone there would actually want to be there, and the afternoons could go back to the work they belong to.

That, really, is the heart of it. Obligation is the enemy of pleasure. The moment a gathering becomes something you must attend, it stops being much fun, both for the guest who has come reluctantly and for the host who can see that he has. I remember a senior politician who, on being told that some social function was compulsory, remarked with some feeling that it could hardly be much of a pleasure then. ## Draft B

There is one custom of our small academic society which I have never been able to regard with any patience, and that is the custom of formal calling. A college town is full of busy people, men who have lectures to prepare and books to write and pupils to see; and their working energy, which is not unlimited, is precious. Yet a great deal of it is dribbled away, afternoon after afternoon, in paying visits of duty; in walking from house to house, sitting for a stated quarter of an hour in drawing-rooms, saying the expected things, and walking away again, with nothing gained on either side. It is a curious waste; and what makes it more curious is that hardly any one, if he were asked, would say that he enjoyed it.

What I should like to see, and what seems to me so obviously sensible that I wonder it has not been tried, is something far easier and more natural. Let there be houses where, on a known evening, people may drop in if they please, and stay as long or as short a time as they like, and go away without ceremony. The work of the day would be done; the mind would be free; and those who came would come because they wished to come, not because a card had been left and must be returned. Such a gathering would have the one quality that the formal call entirely lacks, that of being voluntary.

For that, after all, is the whole secret. The moment a pleasure becomes an obligation it ceases to be a pleasure at all. I remember hearing of a very eminent statesman, not a man given to social diversions, who once said, half in jest, that the only thing which spoilt a party for him was the knowledge that he was obliged to attend it. ## Draft C

I have often wondered, when I consider the life of a college town, at the extraordinary solemnity with which the custom of calling is maintained. Here is a society made up, for the most part, of busy people, whose real business is thought and study, and whose working energy is the most precious thing they possess; and yet the afternoons are frittered away, week after week, in a round of dutiful visits, paid not because anybody desires to pay them, but because it would be thought discourteous to leave them unpaid. One puts on a decent coat, walks half-a-mile to a house where one is not particularly wanted, sits for a quarter of an hour upon the edge of a chair, exchanges a few remarks about the weather and the term, and walks home again, a little tired, a little bored, and with the best hours of the day gone.

What, I ask myself, is the sense of all this laborious ceremony? I do not believe, when I have turned the matter over as fairly as I can, that there is any sense in it at all. Of course one would not abolish hospitality, or pretend that neighbours ought to live like strangers; the pleasures of society are real, and a learned community has perhaps more need of them than most. But why should they be taken in so stiff and formal a shape? It would be far more reasonable, I think, if a household were simply known to be at home upon a certain evening, when friends might drop in or not as they pleased, without ceremony and without reproach; the work of the day would be done, the mind would be ready for recreation, and those who came would come because they wished to.

The worst of it is that obligation spoils the very thing it is meant to secure. A senior politician, reluctant to attend some social occasion, once joked that compulsory attendance takes the fun out of anything, [the politician's exact words needed]; and the jest, like most good jests, had a solid truth at the heart of it. A pleasure that is exacted ceases to be a pleasure at all; it becomes a tax, and is paid, like other taxes, with a sigh.
