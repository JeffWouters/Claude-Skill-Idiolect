# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-03

## The passage (by the author)

Yet this great thing, which lies so near us that we can take it into our grasp by merely reaching out a hand; which is as close to us as the air and the sunlight, has been by the sad, misguided efforts, very often of the best and noblest-minded men, who knew how precious a thing it was, so guarded, so wrapped up, made so remote from, so alien to, life and thought, that many people who live by its light, and draw it in as simply as the air they breathe, never even know that they have come within hail of it. "Is he a good man?" said a simple Methodist once, in reply to a question about a friend. "Yes, he is good, but not religious-good." By which he meant that he lived kindly, purely, and unselfishly as a Christian should, but did not attend any particular place of worship, and therefore could not be held to have any religious motive for his actions, but was guided by a mere worthless instinct, a preference for unworldly living.

Now, if ever there was a Divine attempt made in the world to shake religion free of its wrappings, it was the preaching of Christ. So far as we can gather from records of obscure and mysterious origin, transcriptions, it would seem, of something oral and traditional, Christ aimed at bringing religion within the reach of the humblest and simplest souls. Whatever doubt men may feel as to the literal accuracy of these records in matters of fact, however much it may be held that the relation of incidents was coloured by the popular belief of the time in the possibility of miraculous manifestations, yet the words and sayings of Christ emerge from the narrative, though in places it seems as though they had been imperfectly apprehended, as containing and expressing thoughts quite outside the range of the minds that recorded them; and thus possess an authenticity, which is confirmed and proved by the immature mental grasp of those who compiled the records, in a way in which it would not have been proved, if the compilers had been obviously men of mental acuteness and far-reaching philosophical grasp.

## Draft A

It has often seemed to me a strange thing that religion, which ought to be the nearest and most familiar thing in the world, should have come to seem to so many people a thing remote and difficult, belonging to special places and special days and special persons. I do not think that this has come about through any ill-will. The guardians of religion have, for the most part, been good and earnest men, anxious to protect what they held precious; but in their anxiety they have fenced it about with so many forms and observances and solemnities that the ordinary man has come to feel that it is not for him, or at least not for his daily life. He goes to it, as one goes to a public building, on the proper occasions; and then he comes away and returns to the real business of living.

The result is a very odd confusion of judgment. There are many people, kind, patient, generous, unselfish, who take no part in the worship of any church; and it is commonly said of them that they are not religious. But what, after all, is religion, if it is not just this spirit of kindness and patience and unselfishness? I do not say that forms are worthless; but I do say that to deny the name of religion to a good life, merely because it is lived outside a certain building, is to mistake the shell for the kernel.

It seems to me that the whole effort of Christ was to bring religion near; to take it out of the hands of the learned and the ceremonious, and to put it into the homes and hearts of simple people. His sayings have about them a homeliness, a directness, a kind of startling freshness, which is unlike anything else. And one of the things which most convinces me of their truth is that the men who recorded them were plainly not the men to have invented them. They were honest, simple, often puzzled folk, who set down what they had heard without always understanding it; and the words stand out from their narrative like gold in a common setting.

## Draft B

It has often seemed to me that faith was meant to be near at hand, a thing of daily life, and that we have somehow contrived to bury it under a heap of formality. I do not think this was done out of malice. The people who brought me up in religion were, for the most part, kind and well-meaning. They wanted to give me something they held precious. But in handing it on they wrapped it in so much solemnity, so many special words and special days and special manners, that it came to seem a thing apart, something kept in a building and taken out on occasions, rather than something to live by.

One result of this is a strange confusion about who is religious and who is not. We are apt to judge by attendance. A person who is seen at church is counted among the faithful; a person who is not is quietly set down as irreligious. Yet I know people who never go near a church and who are gentler, more patient and more honest than many who go every week. Their goodness is plain in the way they treat others. It seems perverse to say that they lack faith simply because they do not observe its forms, and I suspect that many of them would be surprised to be told how close they stand to what religion was meant to be.

What strikes me most, reading the gospels, is how hard Jesus himself worked against this very distance. He spoke of ordinary things and talked to people where they were. [example needed] He seemed to want faith to be something anyone could pick up and use.

And his sayings have, to my ear, a ring of truth that I find hard to explain away. Part of the reason, I think, is that the men who wrote them down plainly could not have invented them. They were not men of that kind of genius. The sayings are too fresh, too surprising, too unlike what such writers would have produced had they been left to themselves. They set down what they had heard, sometimes without fully grasping it, and in doing so preserved something far larger than themselves. That, more than any ceremony, is what faith should draw on.

## Draft C

I have come to believe, slowly and not without some reluctance, that religion is a far nearer and simpler thing than most of us were taught to think it. It lies close at hand, in the common acts and affections of every day; and yet it has somehow been buried, so to speak, under a heap of forms and observances, until one can hardly find it for the ceremonies which surround it. The fault is not, I think, a wicked one; the guardians of our youth were for the most part excellent and conscientious people, who desired nothing so much as that we should be good; but they made belief seem a remote and solemn affair, a matter of particular days, particular buildings and particular phrases, which had very little to do with the ordinary business of living.

The worst of it is that this confusion leads us into strange injustices. We are apt to call a man religious if he attends church regularly and repeats the proper words, whatever his temper at home may be; and we are apt to call another irreligious because he stays away, though he may be patient, generous and kind beyond most of his neighbours. Is that a just verdict? I cannot believe that it is, however respectable the authority which pronounces it. The kindly person outside the churches is, I am inclined myself to think, often nearer the heart of the matter than the punctual worshipper who has mistaken the scaffolding for the building.

Of course forms have their use; they are a shelter for devotion, and many people find in them a genuine help. But it is worth remembering that the whole effort of Jesus was to bring faith within reach of ordinary people, to strip it of its mysteries and make it a thing of daily conduct. And his sayings ring true to me, partly because they are so plainly beyond the power of those who recorded them. The gospel writers were honest, simple men; they could not, one feels, have invented such words, any more than a child could invent a sunrise. They set them down, often without fully understanding them, and there they remain, fresh and startling, rather like wild flowers growing through the cracks of an old and ornate pavement.
