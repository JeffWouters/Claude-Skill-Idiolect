# Judge packet synthetic-idris-05

## The passage (by the author)

How do you describe the state of a piece of work that is neither finished nor failing, but somewhere in the uncertain territory between? The question confronts most of us regularly, whenever we are asked to provide a status update, and it is considerably harder to answer honestly than the familiar formats would suggest. The traffic-light colours, the estimate of completion, the brief summary of progress and risks — these devices promise to compress a complicated reality into something a busy reader can absorb at a glance, and in doing so they inevitably discard much of what makes the reality complicated. It may be that the status update is less a report than a translation, and like any translation it involves choices about what to preserve and what to let go. I suspect that the choices we make, often without noticing, reveal a great deal about the relationship between those who do the work and those who oversee it.

Consider the familiar drift of colours in a project that is quietly going wrong. The status remains green for a long time, not because everything is well but because nobody is quite certain enough of the trouble to change it; it becomes amber only when the difficulty can no longer be ignored, and it turns red, if it ever does, at a point when very little can still be done. Why does this pattern recur so consistently? Part of the answer, arguably, is that the colours carry consequences — attention, scrutiny, sometimes an uncomfortable meeting — and people are understandably reluctant to invite those consequences on the basis of a suspicion. The format, which was designed to surface problems early, ends up encouraging people to report them late, since it offers no graceful way of saying that something feels wrong without yet being demonstrably so.

## Draft A

Anyone who has been asked to report on the progress of unfinished work will know the peculiar difficulty of the task. The work itself is untidy: partly done, partly understood, shadowed by problems that may or may not turn out to matter. The report, by contrast, asks for something clean — a percentage, a set of milestones, or most often a colour, green or amber or red, chosen to summarise the whole state of affairs in a single glance. Something is inevitably lost in the passage from the one to the other.

It may be useful to think of such reports as translations. They carry meaning from the language of those doing the work into the language of those responsible for overseeing it, and like all translations they reveal something about the relationship between the two parties. What the worker chooses to emphasise, what the manager expects to hear, which uncertainties are mentioned and which are quietly left out — all of this reflects the dynamics of trust and power between them as much as it reflects the state of the project.

One consequence is visible in almost every organisation that uses colour-coded status. The colours tend to change too late. A project remains green for week after week and then turns red quite suddenly, as though the trouble had appeared overnight. It rarely has. What has usually happened is that the people closest to the work felt a growing unease for some time, but the unease was vague, difficult to justify, and costly to raise. To mark a project amber on the strength of a feeling is to invite questions one cannot yet answer, and perhaps to be thought alarmist if the feeling proves unfounded. It is easier to wait until the worry has become a fact. By then, of course, the report has stopped being useful for the one thing it was meant to do.

## Draft B

How do you report progress on work that is not yet finished, and whose shape is not yet entirely clear even to the people doing it? It may be that the standard answers, the colour codes and percentages and neat status fields, are less a solution to this problem than a way of avoiding it. I suspect most people who have filled in such a report have felt the gap between what they wrote and what they knew.

Consider what a single colour is asked to carry. The work itself is untidy; some parts are going well, some are stalled, some have not been properly understood, and one or two may be worrying in ways that are hard to name. A colour code flattens all of this into one of three states, and in doing so it arguably discards precisely the information that a reader would most need.

There is also something revealing in the act of reporting itself. A status report is, in some sense, a translation from the language of the people doing the work into the language of the people overseeing it, and like any translation it exposes the relationship between the two parties, what each expects, what each fears, and what each has learned is safe to say.

It seems to me that this explains why colours so frequently change too late. To turn a report amber on the strength of a vague worry carries real costs, since it invites questions that cannot yet be answered and attention that may not be welcome; to leave it green costs nothing until the moment it costs a great deal. Perhaps a report that changes colour late is not a failure of honesty but an accurate record of what honesty has been made to cost.

## Draft C

Reporting progress on finished work is easy. You say it is done. Reporting progress on unfinished work is much harder, and most of the formats we use for it make that harder still.

Take the familiar colour codes: green, amber, red. They are tidy, quick to read, and easy to roll up into a dashboard. They also flatten a messy reality into a single state. Unfinished work is rarely just on track or off track. It is partly understood, partly blocked, partly waiting on a decision someone else has not made yet. A colour cannot hold any of that.

It helps to see these reports as translations. The person doing the work has one picture of it, full of detail and doubt. The person reading the report needs another, short enough to act on. Something is lost in between, and what gets lost tells you a lot about the relationship between worker and manager. What the worker chooses to leave out, and what the manager chooses not to ask, reveal who carries the risk of bad news.

This is why colours tend to shift too late. A project often stays green long after the people closest to it have started to worry. Those worries are usually vague at first: a feeling that an estimate was optimistic, a dependency that looks shaky. Flagging a vague worry carries costs. You might be wrong, you might look anxious, and you might invite scrutiny you do not want. So people wait until the worry is concrete, and by then the colour jumps straight from green to red.

Better reporting starts with admitting that unfinished work is uncertain, and giving people a way to say so without penalty. Until then, the colours will keep telling us what we want to hear, a little later than we needed to hear it.
