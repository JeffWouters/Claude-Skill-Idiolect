# Judge packet alice-meynell-02

## The passage (by the author)

If there were no child, there would be nothing old. He, having conceived old time, communicates a remembrance at least of the mystery to the mind of the man. The man perceives at last all the illusion, but he cannot forget what was his conviction when he was a child. He had once a persuasion of Antiquity. And this is not for nothing. The enormous undeception that comes upon him still leaves spaces in his mind.

But the undeception is rude work. The man receives successive shocks. It is as though one strained level eyes towards the horizon, and then were bidden to shorten his sight and to close his search within a poor half acre before his face. Now, it is that he suddenly perceives the hitherto remote, remote youth of his own parents to have been something familiarly near, so measured by his new standard; again, it is the coming of Attila that is displaced. Those ten last years of his have corrected the world. There needs no other rod than that ten years' rod to chastise all the imaginations of the spirit of man. It makes history skip.

To have lived through any appreciable part of any century is to hold thenceforth a mere century cheap enough. But, it may be said, the mystery of change remains. Nay, it does not. Change that trudges through our own world--our contemporary world--is not very mysterious. We perceive its pace; it is a jog-trot. Even so, we now consider, jolted the changes of the past, with the same hurry.

The man, therefore, who has intelligently ceased to be a child scans through a shortened avenue the reaches of the past. He marvels that he was so deceived. For it was a very deception. If the Argonauts, for instance, had been children, it would have been well enough for the child to measure their remoteness and their acts with his own magnificent measure. But they were only men and demi-gods. Thus they belong to him as he is now--a man; and not to him as he was once--a child. It was quite wrong to lay the child's enormous ten years' rule along the path from our time to theirs; that path must be skipped by the nimble yard in the man's present possession. Decidedly the Argonauts are no subject for the boy.

## Draft A

When I was a child, the past was enormous. A hundred years ago was not a number; it was a country so far away that people there must have been a different kind of creature. I suspect most children feel this, and I suspect they largely invent it. Nobody teaches a child that history is vast. The child supplies that feeling from within, because a single year is already so long for them that anything measured in centuries seems to sink into a fog of great age.

Growing up quietly undoes this. As an adult I measure time by years that feel much shorter than they used to. A year now passes like a season did at eight. And once I measure history with that shorter yardstick, the past shrinks alarmingly. A century is only a few lifetimes of the kind I am now living through at speed. Events I once thought of as ancient turn out to be almost within reach, separated from me by a chain of grandparents rather than by an abyss. The sudden closeness of history is not a discovery about history. It is a discovery about how I now feel a year.

There is a second, subtler loss. When I was young, I believed that the world of the past was fundamentally strange, full of changes that must have been dramatic and mysterious to those who lived through them. Then I lived through changes myself. I watched ordinary things appear and disappear, habits shift, whole ways of doing things become old-fashioned. None of it felt remarkable at the time. It simply happened, a little each day, and I adjusted without noticing. Change seen firsthand is almost always ordinary.

Having learned that, I cannot help applying it backwards. The people of earlier centuries presumably felt their own changes in the same flat, daily way. So the mystery drains out of the past as well as the distance. What once looked like a sequence of grand transformations now looks like a long series of ordinary days, each one no stranger to those living it than my days are to me.

I am not sure this is wisdom. It may only be a narrowing, the adult mind trading a child's sense of wonder for a more accurate but smaller picture. Either way, it is hard to get the old feeling back.

## Draft B

The child who is told that a certain king reigned long ago receives the phrase and furnishes it himself, out of his own imagination, with an antiquity that no historian could supply. He has no measure for it but the length of his own brief years, and those years, to him, are enormous; a single summer is an age, and the interval between one birthday and the next is something like an epoch. So he invents the great age of the past, and he invents it generously. The ancient world is ancient indeed, remote beyond remoteness, dim with a dimness that belongs rather to his own phantasy than to any record. It is a majestic error, and perhaps the most fruitful error of his life.

But the man who has grown up measures by another rule. His years have shortened--not in the almanac, but in the feeling of them--and he perceives, with something like dismay, that a decade passes almost as quickly as a season once did. By this diminished measure history is suddenly close at hand. A century is only a few lifetimes laid end to end, and a few lifetimes are nothing; the king who reigned long ago reigned, it now appears, the day before yesterday. Who has not felt this contraction, and felt it with a kind of loss? The majesty is gone out of the distance. And there is a second loss, subtler than the first. The adult has seen change with his own eyes: he has watched customs alter, towns enlarge, and manners give way to other manners, and all of it seemed perfectly ordinary while it was happening. It was only daily life, going on. Having witnessed so much change without any sense of wonder, he can no longer believe that the changes of former ages were wonderful either. The transformations of the past lose their mystery precisely because he knows, from experience, how commonplace a transformation feels from within. I suppose this is what we call knowledge. It is, at any rate, a decivilising sort of knowledge, for it takes away the grandeur and gives nothing in return but a correct and melancholy arithmetic. The child was wrong, and the child was richer.

## Draft C

The child makes antiquity. He is not taught it; he invents it, out of the great length of his own days, and lays it upon whatever he is told of kings and wars and the building of old walls. A thing that happened before he was born happened, to him, in an immeasurable before. There is no difference, in his sense of it, between the last generation and the tenth. All is equally far, because all is beyond the edge of a year that is itself enormous.

But the man measures otherwise. He measures, whether he will or no, by the years he now lives, and they are short. They are short not in their count but in their feeling; they pass with a haste that the child never knew. And having shortened his own years, he finds--to his surprise, and a little to his loss--that he has shortened history as well. The centuries that once stood so far off have drawn near. A hundred years is but a few of his own swift spans laid end to end. He could almost reach back and touch the hand that wrote a letter he once thought incredibly ancient. It is not that he has learnt anything new of the past. It is that the rule by which he measures has grown shorter in his hand.

There is a further change, and a subtler. The grown man has seen things alter. He has watched streets rebuilt, customs dropped, fashions of speech come and go. And because he watched them, they seemed to him ordinary. Nothing marvellous attended them; they happened on weekdays, among other business, without portent. It is not possible to have seen change so plainly without learning that change is, for the most part, plain. So he looks back upon the great alterations of the past and suspects that they, too, were done on weekdays. What was once a mystery--how the old world became the new--is now only the familiar process, of which he has been a witness, carried a little further back.

Who but loses something by this knowledge? The child's past was vast and strange; the man's is near and reasonable. He has gained accuracy and given up awe. Almost--not quite; for there remains in him, beneath the adult arithmetic, some faint memory of that first immensity.
