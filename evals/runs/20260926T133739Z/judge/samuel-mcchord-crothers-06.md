# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-06

## The passage (by the author)

At last I said, “You have told me what you did before you concluded to reform. I am curious to know how, in those days, you looked at things. Was there anything which you wouldn’t have done, not because you were afraid of the law, but because you felt it would be wrong?”

“Yes,” he said, “there is one thing I never would do, because it always seemed low down. I never would steal.”

It was evident that further discussion would be unprofitable without definition of terms. I found that by stealing he meant petty larceny, which he abhorred. In our condemnation of the sneak thief and the pickpocket we were on common ground. His feeling of reprobation was, if anything, more intense than that which I felt at the time. He alluded to the umbrellas and other portable articles he had noticed in the hallway. Any one who would take advantage of an unsuspecting householder by purloining such things was a degenerate. He had no dealings with such moral imbeciles.

It seemed to me that I might press the analogy which instantly occurred to me between “stealing” and forgery.

“Do they not,” I said, “seem to you to amount to very much the same thing?”

I had struck a wrong note. Analogies are ticklish things to handle, for things which are alike in certain respects are apt to be quite different in other respects. His mind was intent on the differences. The sneak thief, he told me, is a vulgar fellow of no education. The forger and the check-raiser are experts. They are playing a game. Their wits are pitted against the wits of the men who are paid high salaries for detecting them. They belong to quite different spheres. If we are looking for analogies we should look up and not down.

## Draft A

I once had a long conversation with a man who had been a forger and had reformed. He had put his old profession behind him, but not his old opinions, and it was these that interested me. For I discovered that he had lived all along by a moral code, and a very strict one. It was not the code of the community, but it had the merit of being his own.

The first article of it was a thorough contempt for petty thieves. He spoke of them as a respectable citizen might speak of the idle poor. They were mean fellows, without skill and without self-respect, who took what lay within reach and ran away with it. There was nothing in their work that called for intelligence. He could not understand how any man could sink so low.

I ventured, with some hesitation, to suggest that there was a certain resemblance between forgery and theft. In both cases, I said, something passed from the pocket of one man into the pocket of another without the consent of the first. He was deeply offended. It was plain that I had touched him on a tender point. To compare the two, he said, showed a want of discrimination which he had not expected of me.

He then explained the matter as he saw it. Forgery was a game, and a game of a high order. On one side was the forger, working with his own brains and his own hands. On the other side were the detectives, who were paid to catch him. It was a fair contest of wits. The detectives had the resources of the law and a salary besides; the forger had only his skill. If he won, it was because he was the better player. If he lost, he paid the forfeit like a sportsman.

I found it impossible to argue with him. He had the advantage of me, for he had thought the matter out, and I had only accepted what I had been told.

## Draft B

I once had a long conversation with a reformed forger, a man who had retired from his profession with, I believe, a sincere intention of never practising it again. What interested me was not his repentance, which was conventional enough, but the moral code he had observed while he was still in business. For he had a code, and he spoke of it with the quiet pride of a craftsman describing the traditions of his guild.

Nothing in his former life had been so distasteful to him as the petty thief. He described the pickpocket and the sneak with a contempt that would have done credit to a magistrate. They were, in his opinion, a low and unskilful class of persons, who took what lay within reach and had neither the patience nor the intelligence to take anything else.

I made the error of suggesting that forgery was, after all, only a more elaborate form of theft. He was genuinely offended. "Theft!" he said, and for a moment I thought he would leave the room. A thief, he explained when he had recovered his composure, simply took advantage of carelessness and could claim no more merit than a man who picks up a purse in the road, whereas a forger matched his wits against the best minds that the banks and the government could hire. The detectives were paid professionals, trained for the purpose and well supplied with resources. The forger was alone. When he won, he had beaten them fairly at their own game. When he lost, he went to prison, and he considered that the rules had been properly enforced.

I confess I found the argument difficult to answer on its own ground. It was the reasoning of a sportsman, not a criminal. He did not think of the men whose names he had signed, or of the money that had passed through his hands. He thought of the contest. Remove the victims, and what remained was a very honourable game.

## Draft C

I once spent an afternoon with a man who had been a forger for many years and had since given it up. He was entirely frank about his former trade and seemed to take a certain pleasure in explaining it, much as a retired sea captain will explain the finer points of navigation. What interested me most was not his methods but his morals, for it turned out that he had a very definite code and was prepared to defend it.

The first thing I learned was that he despised thieves. I do not mean great thieves, of whom he said little, but the petty kind, the pickpocket and the sneak who lifts a coat from a hallway. He spoke of them with real contempt. They were, he said, a low sort of people, without skill or courage, who took what was not theirs simply because it was there. He would not have sat at a table with one.

I made the mistake of suggesting that forgery was, after all, a kind of theft. He was genuinely offended. He put down his cup and looked at me as a gentleman might look at someone who had confused him with his coachman. There was no comparison, he told me. A thief took; a forger earned.

His view, as he went on to explain it, was that forgery was a contest. On one side stood the forger, with his skill, his patience and his nerve. On the other stood the detectives, men paid by the banks for the express purpose of catching him. It was a match of wits between professionals, fairly entered on both sides. If he won, he had beaten trained men at their own game; if he lost, he went to prison and bore no grudge. The thief entered no contest at all.

I came away uncertain whether I had been talking to a scoundrel or a sportsman. I suspect he would have said there was no difference.
