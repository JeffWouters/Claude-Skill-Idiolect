# Judge packet synthetic-noor-04

## The passage (by the author)

Every October the heating comes back on. And every October, the radiators knock.

It starts in the night. A tick, then a knock, then a long gurgle like a drain. The one in the back bedroom is the worst. It sounds like someone walking in the wall.

So on the first cold Saturday I bleed them. All nine. It takes about an hour.

The tool is a small brass key. It costs a few euros and it is the one tool I always lose. I keep three now. One on a hook in the boiler cupboard. One in the kitchen drawer. One in the toolbox. I can usually find one of the three.

First I turn the heating on and let it run for 20 minutes. The radiators get hot. Then I feel each one with my hand. Flat palm, top to bottom. A radiator with air in it is hot at the bottom and cold at the top. You can feel the line where the heat stops. On the back bedroom one, the line is halfway down.

Then I turn the heating off. This is the step people skip. Bleed a radiator with the pump running and you pull in more air. Or you get a jet of hot black water in the face. I have had both.

I wait ten minutes for things to settle. I make a coffee.

So then I start at the bottom of the house and work up. Downstairs first, then the landing, then the bedrooms. Air rises. If you start at the top, the air from below comes up after you.

At each radiator, the same steps.

Put an old cloth under the valve. The valve is a small square nut at one top corner. Fit the key. Hold a second cloth round the key. Then turn it a quarter turn anticlockwise. Slowly.

## Draft A

Every year, when the heating comes back on, there is one small job that has to be done before the house feels right again: bleeding the radiators. Air collects in the system over time, and trapped air means radiators that stay partly cold no matter how high the thermostat is set. Letting it out takes very little skill, but it does reward doing things in the right order.

The first challenge is always finding a bleed key. They are small, easy to lose, and needed only once a year, which is the worst possible combination. Over time I have solved this by keeping spares in several places around the house: [locations needed]. It means that whichever room I start in, there is usually a key within reach, and I no longer spend the first half hour of the job searching drawers.

The process itself begins with warming the system. I turn the heating on and let it run until the radiators have had time to heat up properly. Then I go round and feel each one. A radiator with trapped air is usually warm at the bottom and noticeably cool at the top, because the air rises and sits in the upper part. Those cool upper sections tell me which radiators need attention.

Once I know which ones they are, I switch the heating off again before bleeding anything. Bleeding a system while the pump is running and the water is hot is both less effective and less pleasant, so I give it a little time to settle first.

Then I work my way upward, starting on the ground floor and moving to the higher floors afterwards. At each radiator I put a cloth under the bleed valve, fit the key and turn it gently. There is no need for force here. A slow quarter turn is usually enough to hear the hiss of air escaping, and the gentler the turn, the more control I keep over what happens next.

## Draft B

I bleed the radiators once a year. I never know where the key is.

That is not true. I know where the keys are. There are several, stashed in different places. [example needed: where they are kept]. The spares were meant to solve the problem. They did not. So now there are keys all over the house, and I still go looking for one. Every year.

Here is the job. Warm the system. Find the cold patches. Switch off. Bleed. Start low and work up.

First the heat. Turn the heating on, and let it run until the radiators are warm. Trapped air does not show on a cold radiator. You need the water moving.

Then the hunt. I go round the house with a flat hand on the top of each radiator. Along the whole length. A good radiator is warm from top to bottom. A bad one is warm at the bottom and cool along the top. That cool strip is the air. If the top is cool and the bottom is hot, it needs bleeding. Simple as that.

Then switch the heating off. Let the pump stop. Do not bleed a system while it runs. And the water in there is hot, so give it time.

So to the valves. Start on the ground floor. Work upward. Air rises, so the top floor goes last. A folded cloth under the valve. The key in the square nut. Then turn it gently. A small turn is enough. More than you think will come out. Turn it too far, and the whole valve can come loose. Then you have water on the floor.

You hear it first. A soft hiss. That is the air.

This morning the cloth is on the landing, folded. Waiting for the next radiator.

## Draft C

Once a year I bleed the radiators. [month needed]. It is not hard. The hard part is finding the key.

A bleed key is a small thing. It lives in a drawer and then it does not. So I keep spares. Several of them, in several places. [locations needed]. They are cheap. Cheaper than a morning spent looking for one.

First, heat. Put the heating on and let the whole system warm up. Give it time. Then walk round the house and put a hand on each radiator. Top and bottom. The one you want is warm at the bottom and cool at the top. That cool band is air. It sits up there and the hot water cannot get in. The radiator is doing half a job. [example needed].

Note which ones. I just remember. In a bigger house you might write it down.

Then switch the heating off. This matters. Do not bleed a radiator with the system running. And the water is hot. Let it settle for a bit before you start.

Start on the ground floor. Work upward. Upstairs goes last. Take a cloth. Hold it under the valve. It is at the top, at one end.

Put the key on. Turn it gently. Not far. You will hear a hiss. That is the air coming out. Do not open it wide. The valve is small, and old ones are fragile. You want a slow hiss, not a rush. If you have to lean on the key, stop. [detail needed: what to do with a stuck valve].

Then wait. Listen. The hiss goes on for a while. It is a thin, steady sound, like a tyre going down. Keep the cloth ready. Keep the key on. Do not walk off to do the next one. This is the part that needs you to stand there.

It is a dull job. Standing next to a radiator, holding a rag. But a cold top half all winter is worse.
