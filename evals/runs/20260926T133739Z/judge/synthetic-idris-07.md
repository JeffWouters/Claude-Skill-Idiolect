## The passage (by the author)

Why is it that we continue to ask for estimates when so few of us believe in them? The question is not entirely rhetorical, since the practice of estimation survives in almost every software organisation despite a long and well-documented history of disappointment, and the persistence of a practice that consistently fails usually suggests that it is doing something other than what it claims to do. Arguably the estimate is not a prediction at all; it is a negotiation conducted in the vocabulary of prediction, a way of reconciling what the business wants with what the engineers fear, in which both parties agree to pretend that the resulting figure describes the future. I suspect most experienced engineers understand this intuitively, even if they would not articulate it in quite those terms. The difficulty arises when the pretence is forgotten — when the negotiated figure hardens into a commitment, and the commitment becomes a measure by which people are judged.

It may be helpful to consider what we are actually doing when we produce an estimate for a piece of software work. We are imagining a sequence of activities that has not yet happened, in a system we only partially understand, subject to interruptions we cannot foresee, and we are compressing all of that uncertainty into a single figure that must fit neatly into a planning document. The act is, in some sense, heroic; it asks the imagination to perform a task for which it is poorly equipped, and it rewards confidence over accuracy. Is it any wonder that the results are unreliable? What is more surprising, perhaps, is how rarely the unreliability is acknowledged in the planning process itself, as though each new estimate were an entirely fresh attempt rather than the latest entry in a long series of similar misjudgements.

## Draft A

Few practices in software development are as persistent, or as widely distrusted, as the estimate. Teams produce them week after week, managers ask for them and write them down, and yet there is a quiet understanding on all sides that the figures should not be taken too literally. It is worth asking why a practice that commands so little confidence continues to occupy so much of our attention.

Part of the answer, I think, lies in what an estimate actually is. We tend to speak of it as a prediction, a technical judgement about how long something will take. In practice it is closer to a negotiation. On one side stand the wishes of the business, which would like the work to be done soon and cheaply; on the other stand the worries of the engineers, who know how many ways the work might turn out to be harder than it looks. The number that emerges is a kind of settlement between these two pressures, and like most settlements it satisfies neither party entirely.

This would be a reasonable enough arrangement if the number were understood as a settlement. The trouble begins when it is not — when the figure agreed in a planning conversation is lifted out of that conversation and treated elsewhere as a binding promise. At that point the provisional becomes fixed, and every subsequent departure from it is read not as new information but as a failure.

What makes this more difficult to correct is that planning rarely looks back. It is unusual for a team to set its new estimates alongside a candid record of how often its previous ones proved wrong, even though such a record would be the most useful guide available. The misses are acknowledged one at a time, if at all, and then forgotten. Perhaps the estimate would earn more trust if we were willing to remember, honestly and together, how little trust it has so far deserved.

## Draft B

Why do software teams go on producing estimates that so few people, including the teams themselves, genuinely trust? It may be that we misunderstand what an estimate is and what it is for, and that the distrust follows naturally from asking it to be something it cannot be. I suspect the number was never really a measurement in the first place.

Consider how an estimate actually comes into being, in the room where it is first spoken aloud. On one side there is what the business would like, a date or a scope that fits a plan already half-made; on the other there are the worries of the engineers, who can see the unknowns that the plan does not mention. The figure that emerges is, in some sense, a bargain struck between these two positions, a point at which both sides agree to stop arguing for the time being, and there is nothing wrong with that so long as everyone remembers that a bargain is what it is.

The trouble starts when that figure turns into a binding promise, which tends to happen without anyone deciding that it should, as a number that was offered as a compromise is written into a plan, repeated in meetings, and gradually treated as though it had been derived from evidence; the engineers then feel held to something they never quite promised, and the business feels misled when it slips. Is it any wonder that neither side comes to trust the next one?

There is also a curious silence in most planning, in that teams seldom look back at how often their past guesses missed, and by how much, although that record is arguably the most useful information they possess. It seems to me that admitting this history openly would do more for planning than any new technique. Perhaps an estimate earns trust not by being right but by being honest about how rarely it has been.

## Draft C

Software teams produce estimates constantly, and few people trust them. Not the engineers who give them, not the managers who pass them on, and not the business that plans around them. Yet the estimates keep coming. It is worth asking why.

Part of the answer is that an estimate is rarely what it claims to be. It presents itself as a measurement: this will take so long. In practice it is a bargain. On one side are the business's wishes, a date that would be convenient, a scope that would be ideal. On the other side are engineering's worries, the unknowns, the dependencies, the parts of the system nobody wants to touch. The number that comes out is where those two sides settled, for now. That is useful information, as long as everyone understands it as a negotiated position rather than a fact.

Trouble starts once that figure turns into a binding promise. Somewhere between the planning meeting and the roadmap, the bargain loses its context. The worries that shaped it are no longer attached. What remains is a number, and a number looks like a commitment. From then on, any change to it reads as a failure rather than as the ordinary result of learning more.

What makes this worse is that planning seldom admits how often past guesses missed. Most teams have a long history of estimates, and most of those estimates were off, sometimes by a lot. That history is rarely brought into the room when the next estimate is made. Each new plan is built as if this time the numbers will hold, even though there is little reason to think so.

The result is a cycle: estimates are asked for, given, hardened into promises, missed, and then asked for again with the same confidence. Breaking it does not require better estimators. It requires being honest about what an estimate is, and about how well our previous ones did.
