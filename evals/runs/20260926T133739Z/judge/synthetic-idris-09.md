# Judge packet synthetic-idris-09

## The passage (by the author)

It may be that the most valuable thing a team can do is to make learning visible — to treat it as a legitimate activity that people can talk about openly rather than as a private deficiency to be remedied in secret. This means, among other things, allowing people to say that they do not understand something without that admission being counted against them; it means asking questions in shared channels rather than in private messages; it means senior engineers occasionally demonstrating their own uncertainty, so that others feel permitted to acknowledge theirs. The effect of such practices is considerable, though difficult to measure. A team in which learning happens in public tends to learn faster, since each person's confusion becomes an occasion for collective clarification, and the questions asked by one person often turn out to be the questions that several others were too embarrassed to raise.

Why, then, is this kind of openness so rare? Part of the answer, I suspect, lies in the way expertise functions as a form of status within technical organisations. To know something that others do not is to possess a kind of currency, and admitting ignorance can feel like spending that currency carelessly; the more senior one becomes, the higher the perceived cost of saying "I don't know". There is also, perhaps, a cultural inheritance from the way many of us were educated, in which not knowing the answer was associated with failure rather than with the beginning of inquiry. These attitudes are deeply ingrained and rarely examined, even by people who would disavow them if asked. They persist not because anyone defends them explicitly but because they are woven into countless small interactions, each of which reinforces the sense that competence must be performed even when it is not felt.

Perhaps what we need is a more generous understanding of what professional competence actually consists of. It is not the possession of a fixed body of knowledge, which will in any case become obsolete; it is, arguably, the capacity to keep learning, to tolerate the discomfort of not yet understanding, and to help others through that discomfort as well. Seen in this light, the moment of becoming a beginner again is not a threat to our expertise but an expression of it. The engineers I have most admired have rarely been those who knew the most; they have been those who remained curious, who asked the obvious questions without embarrassment, and who seemed to regard their own ignorance as an interesting place to begin rather than as something to be hidden.

## Draft A

Engineering teams talk a lot about learning. They run lunch sessions, share links, and put growth goals in everyone's review. Yet in many teams, the actual experience of learning, the part where you do not understand something yet, stays mostly hidden. People learn in private and show up in public only once they have figured it out.

That is a missed opportunity. When someone admits confusion publicly, everyone learns quicker. A question asked in a shared channel gets answered once, for everyone who had the same question and did not ask it. A half-formed understanding, said out loud, gets corrected before it spreads. A colleague who says they are stuck on something often turns out to be stuck on the same thing as three other people, and the team solves it together instead of four times over. Openness about not knowing is not a weakness in a team's learning. It is the mechanism.

So why is it so rare? Mostly because knowledge works as status. In engineering, being the person who knows is a source of standing. People come to you, your opinion carries weight, your name comes up when something difficult needs doing. That standing is earned, and it is also fragile. Every visible gap in your knowledge feels like a small withdrawal from it.

This weighs heaviest on senior engineers. The more your role is defined by knowing things, the more exposed you feel when you do not. Juniors are expected to ask questions. Seniors are expected to answer them. So seniors often fear showing ignorance more than anyone, and the result is that the people whose example matters most are the ones least likely to show the team what learning looks like.

I think this rests on a mistaken idea of what competence is. Real competence in a field that keeps changing does not mean having finished learning. It means continuing to learn, steadily and without embarrassment. The engineers people most admire are rarely the ones who seem to know everything. They are the ones who stay curious. They ask the naive question in the meeting, say they have not used a tool before, and treat not knowing as the ordinary starting point for knowing.

Making learning open and normal, then, starts at the top. When senior people say "I do not know, let me find out" in front of others, they lower the cost of saying it for everyone else. They show that status and curiosity are not in conflict. And over time, the team stops treating confusion as something to hide and starts treating it as something to share.

That shift does not need a programme or a budget. [example needed] It needs a few people willing to go first.

## Draft B

There is a particular hesitation that settles over a room when someone is about to ask a question and suspects, perhaps rightly, that everyone else already knows the answer. You may have felt it yourself, the brief calculation of whether the question is worth the small loss of standing it might cost, and the quiet decision, more often than not, to look it up later instead. It may be that this hesitation, repeated across a team day after day, does more to slow its learning than any shortage of documentation or training. I suspect it is also one of the easier things to change.

Consider what happens when someone does admit their confusion in public. The question, once asked, turns out to be shared by several others who had been waiting for someone else to ask it; the answer, once given, is heard by everyone at once rather than rediscovered separately by each of them; and the person who asked has shown, in some sense, that asking is permitted. A team in which this happens routinely learns considerably faster than one in which each member struggles privately, not because its people are cleverer but because its knowledge moves more freely.

There is also a reason this is harder than it sounds. In most engineering organisations knowledge functions as a kind of status, arguably the most important kind, and seniority is understood to mean knowing things that others do not. For a senior engineer to say openly that they do not understand something therefore feels like spending a currency they have taken years to accumulate. Is it any wonder that those with the most to lose are often the most reluctant to be seen learning, even when their example would do the most good?

It seems to me that the way out lies in a different understanding of competence, one that treats it not as a store of settled knowledge but as a continuing capacity to learn. This means leaders asking basic questions where others can hear them; it means treating an admission of ignorance as a contribution rather than a confession; it means, perhaps, noticing and saying aloud when someone has learned something new, so that learning becomes something a team recognises rather than something each person hides. None of this requires a programme or a budget.

When you think of the engineers you have most admired in your working life, I suspect what you remember is not how much they knew but how curious they remained. Perhaps that is what seniority ought to mean in the end: not having stopped needing to learn, but never having wanted to.

## Draft C

There is a small moment, familiar to anyone who has worked in an engineering team, when someone in a meeting uses a term or refers to a system that you do not understand. The choice that follows is usually made in an instant and almost without thought: to ask, and so reveal the gap in one's knowledge, or to nod along and hope to work it out later. Most of us, most of the time, choose the second. It is worth considering what that habit costs, and what it might take to change it.

The case for asking openly is, on the face of it, rather strong. A question spoken aloud helps not only the person who asks it but also the others in the room who were quietly wondering the same thing, and it often brings to light assumptions that the more knowledgeable members of the team had never realised they were making. A team in which confusion is admitted freely tends, I suspect, to learn considerably faster than one in which it is concealed, simply because its misunderstandings are corrected sooner and more often.

Why, then, is this so difficult? Part of the answer lies in the role that knowledge plays within engineering culture. To know things — to be the person others consult, the one who understands how the old system really works — is a source of standing, and sometimes the principal one. Knowledge functions as a kind of status, and status is not easily put at risk. This is felt most acutely by those who have the most of it. A junior engineer who admits to not understanding something is merely behaving as juniors are expected to behave; a senior engineer who does the same may feel, rightly or wrongly, that something of their authority has been surrendered.

It seems to me that this rests on a misunderstanding of what competence actually is. In a field that changes as quickly as ours, competence cannot be a fixed stock of knowledge acquired once and then defended. It is better understood as a capacity for continuing to learn — for meeting the unfamiliar without anxiety and working through it with others. If we think about the engineers we most admire, it is striking how rarely their distinction lies in knowing everything. More often it lies in a persistent curiosity, and in a readiness to say, without apparent discomfort, that they do not yet understand something and would like to.

Making learning open and normal, then, is less a matter of new practices than of changed example. When the most experienced people in a team are seen to ask their questions aloud, everyone else acquires permission to do the same.
