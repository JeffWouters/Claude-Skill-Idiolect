# Judge packet synthetic-idris-08

## The passage (by the author)

Where do deadlines actually come from? The question may seem odd, since most deadlines arrive with an air of inevitability, as though they had been imposed by the nature of things rather than decided by particular people for particular reasons. Some are genuinely external — a regulatory requirement, a contractual obligation, an event that will happen whether or not the software is ready — but a considerable proportion are, in some sense, invented; they emerge from planning conversations, from the desire to have something to announce, from a general feeling that work without a date attached is work that will never be finished. I do not say this to dismiss them, since invention is not the same thing as arbitrariness. It may be that invented deadlines serve a real purpose, concentrating attention and forcing decisions that would otherwise be postponed indefinitely; the difficulty is that we so rarely distinguish between the invented and the unavoidable, and we therefore tend to treat all of them with the same anxious reverence.

Consider what happens to a team as a deadline approaches. There is often a gradual narrowing of attention, in which everything not directly connected to the immediate goal begins to seem like a distraction; conversations become shorter, decisions become quicker, and the careful deliberation that characterised the early stages of the project gives way to a more pragmatic, sometimes more ruthless, style of working. Some of this is healthy — the pressure clarifies priorities and dissolves the kind of endless refinement that can paralyse a project. Some of it, arguably, is not; corners are cut that will have to be repaired later, people work hours that they would never accept in calmer periods, and the quality of judgement declines precisely when the stakes are highest. Who decides whether the trade-off is worth making, and on what basis? In my experience the decision is almost never made explicitly; it simply happens, as the logic of the deadline asserts itself.

## Draft A

Every project seems to have a due date, but it is worth asking where those dates come from, and what they do once they exist.

Some are imposed from outside. A contract specifies it, a regulation takes effect, an event is scheduled that will not move for anyone. These dates are real in a straightforward sense: missing them has consequences that are not ours to negotiate.

Many others are made up. Someone needed a date for a plan, a quarter was ending, a round number felt right. That is not necessarily a bad thing. An invented date can still help focus. It gives a team something to aim at, a reason to decide instead of deliberating, a shared sense that the work should come to an end at some point. Without any date at all, work has a way of expanding.

As a date approaches, whatever its origin, it changes how people behave. The good effect is that priorities sharpen. Things that seemed important turn out to be optional. Decisions that were postponed get made. The team becomes clearer about what actually matters.

The less welcome effect comes at the same time. Approaching dates invite shortcuts: tests that get skipped, reviews that get rushed, known problems that get noted for later. They also invite overwork, the late evenings and weekend sessions that feel heroic in the moment and leave people depleted afterward. Those costs do not disappear when the date passes. They show up later as defects, as fatigue, sometimes as people leaving.

What strikes me is that nobody consciously weighs whether that trade is worthwhile. The focus and the shortcuts arrive together, and we accept both as part of how deadlines work. Rarely does anyone stop and ask whether this particular date, especially if it was one we made up, is worth the shortcuts and the overwork it is producing.

That question deserves to be asked out loud. Sometimes the answer will be yes. Often, I suspect, it will not.

## Draft B

Where do project due dates actually come from, and what do they do to the work once they exist? It may be that we treat all dates as though they had the same origin, when in fact they arrive by quite different routes and deserve quite different kinds of respect. I suspect most of them were invented by somebody, at some point, for reasons nobody now recalls.

Consider the two broad kinds, which are rarely distinguished in practice. Some dates are imposed from outside, by circumstances that will arrive whether we are ready or not; others are simply made up, chosen because a plan needed a date and this one seemed reasonable at the time. The invented kind is not necessarily useless, since a date, even an arbitrary one, can give a team something to focus on and a reason to make decisions it might otherwise postpone indefinitely.

There is also the matter of what a date does as it approaches. Priorities sharpen considerably; questions that were open for weeks are suddenly settled in an afternoon; the team discovers which features it can live without. At the same time, the approaching date invites shortcuts that will have to be repaid later, and it draws people into longer hours that leave them less capable of good judgement than they were before. Both effects are real, and they arrive together.

It seems to me that what is missing is any conscious weighing of the one against the other. Nobody sits down and asks whether the focus a date provides is worth the shortcuts and the overwork it will also produce; the trade is simply made, in some sense by default, because the date is there. Perhaps the question to ask of any deadline is not whether we can meet it but what we are prepared to spend in doing so.

## Draft C

Where do project due dates come from? The question sounds simple, and in some cases the answer is. A date may be imposed from outside the team altogether — by a regulation that takes effect on a particular day, by a contract, by an event that will happen whether or not the work is ready for it. Such dates are facts of the environment, and there is little to be gained by pretending otherwise.

Many dates, however, have no such origin. They are, if we are honest about it, made up: chosen because a quarter was ending, or because a round number seemed appropriate, or because someone needed to put something on a slide. It would be easy to dismiss these as arbitrary, but I am not sure that would be fair. An invented date can still do useful work. It gives a diffuse effort a point of focus, and it lends a certain urgency to decisions that might otherwise be deferred indefinitely.

The effect of an approaching date is, in fact, rather double-edged. On the one hand, it tends to sharpen priorities wonderfully; questions that had been debated for weeks are suddenly settled, and work that was never quite essential is quietly set aside. On the other hand, the same pressure invites shortcuts that will have to be paid for later, and it draws people into long evenings and weekends whose cost is borne less visibly but no less really.

What strikes me about this trade is not that it is made, since there are circumstances in which it is entirely worth making. It is that it is so seldom made deliberately. No one sits down and asks whether the clarity gained is worth the corners cut and the energy spent; the trade simply happens, as a by-product of the date's existence, and its terms are accepted without ever having been examined. We might do rather better if, before setting a date, we were to ask what we are prepared to give up in order to meet it.
