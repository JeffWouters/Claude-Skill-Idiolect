# Judge packet alice-meynell-04

## The passage (by the author)

He has not passed in vain through the long ten years, the ten years that are the treasury of preceptions--the first. The great disillusion shall never shorten those years, nor set nearer together the days that made them. "Far apart," I have said, and that "far apart" is wonderful. The past of childhood is not single, is not motionless, nor fixed in one point; it has summits a world away one from the other. Year from year differs as the antiquity of Mexico from the antiquity of Chaldea. And the man of thirty-five knows for ever afterwards what is flight, even though he finds no great historic distances to prove his wings by.

There is a long and mysterious moment in long and mysterious childhood, which is the extremest distance known to any human fancy. Many other moments, many other hours, are long in the first ten years. Hours of weariness are long--not with a mysterious length, but with a mere length of protraction, so that the things called minutes and half-hours by the elderly may be something else to their apparent contemporaries, the children. The ancient moment is not merely one of these--it is a space not of long, but of immeasurable, time. It is the moment of going to sleep. The man knows that borderland, and has a contempt for it: he has long ceased to find antiquity there. It has become a common enough margin of dreams to him; and he does not attend to its phantasies. He knows that he has a frolic spirit in his head which has its way at those hours, but he is not interested in it. It is the inexperienced child who passes with simplicity through the marginal country; and the thing he meets there is principally the yet further conception of illimitable time.

## Draft A

In memory, the earliest years of my life stand far apart from one another. Each one is distinct, a separate room with its own light and furniture, and between them lies a great distance. Later years run together; I could not easily tell you what separated one from the next. But the years of early childhood still keep their spacing, as if each had been long enough to be a small lifetime of its own.

It is tempting to explain this by boredom. Children are often bored, and tedious hours do feel long. Anyone who has waited through a dull afternoon knows how time can drag. But the vastness I remember is not the same thing. A tedious hour is long in a narrow, uncomfortable way; it presses on you and you want it to end. The time of early childhood was long in a different way altogether. It had no pressure in it. It simply seemed to have no edge, to go on in every direction without any sense that it would run out.

I think I felt this most clearly when drifting off to sleep. Lying in bed at the end of the day, a child can feel time open up around him, endless and calm, with the night stretching away and the morning unimaginably far off. There is no hurry and no count. Adults rarely notice anything like this. We fall asleep thinking of tomorrow's tasks, or we count the hours we have left before the alarm. The same passage from waking to sleep that once felt like entering boundless time is now just a gap to be got through.

Perhaps that sense of endlessness was an illusion. But it was a real experience, and I suspect it is where most of us first learned what the word forever might mean.

## Draft B

To the grown man the years of his early childhood remain, in memory, strangely distant from one another. Each has preserved its own character and its own particular light; the year of [example needed] is not the year of [example needed], and between them lie intervals that seem wider than entire decades of later existence. Time was not then a stream but a country, and a country of immeasurable extent.

It will be objected that time is long enough for the adult also, whenever he is bored. The tedious hour at a desk, the waiting-room, the sermon that will not conclude--these are long, no doubt. But they are long in a different manner. Their length is a burden, a counting of minutes against the clock, and the sufferer is continually conscious of the ending he is waiting for. The child's time had no such ending in view. It was not tedious but boundless, and there is a difference between a thing that is too long and a thing that has no discoverable length at all.

The child knows this boundlessness most intimately at the edge of sleep. He lies in the darkness, the household grows quiet, and as he drifts away he has a sense of time extending on every side--not forward only, but around him, without walls or limits, as spacious as the night itself. Who, among adults, pays the slightest attention to this? We put the child to bed, we close the door, and we consider the day finished and accounted for. For him, it may be, the day has only opened into something larger than any day. We have forgotten it, and we do not ask.

## Draft C

The first years of life lie in the memory not close together, as later years do, but far apart, each one isolated in its own wide country. Between the summer when one was four and the summer when one was five there is a distance that no after-decade can match. They do not run into one another. Each is a separate age, with its own light and its own weather, and one remembers them as one remembers different lands.

It will be said that children find the hours long because they are bored, and that is true in its degree. A wet afternoon, a lesson, a sermon--these were long indeed. But their length was of another kind. It was the length of a road that has an end one cannot yet see; tedious, but finite; a thing to be got through. The child knew, even in his weariness, that the clock would strike. That is not the vastness I mean. The true immensity of early time had no weariness in it at all. It was not a delay; it was an openness.

It came most plainly at the edge of sleep. The child in his bed, with the voices of the house growing faint below, lets go, and in the letting go there opens before him a time without shore. He is not waiting for anything. He is not counting. The night is not a night of so many hours but a boundless tract into which he drifts, and it seems to him that it will never be crossed. What grown man attends to the moment of his falling asleep? He has made it a habit, and habit has abolished it. But the child knows it, if he cannot name it, as the largest thing he knows.
