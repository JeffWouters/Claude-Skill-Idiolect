# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-03

## The passage (by the author)

Many attempts have been made to depict the character of an accomplished scamp. But Gil Blas and Roderick Random and Jonathan Wild the Great are after all seen from the outside. The author may attempt to do them justice, but there is a vein of irony that reveals a judgment of his own. They lack the essential element of incorrigibility, which is that the scamp does not suspect himself, has not found himself out.

No novelist has ever been able to give such a portraiture of a complacent criminal as was given a century ago in the autobiography of Stephen Burroughs.

Burroughs was the son of a worthy clergyman of Hanover, New Hampshire, and from the outset was looked upon as a black sheep. As a mere boy he ran away from home and joined the army, and then with equal irresponsibility deserted. He became a ship’s surgeon, a privateersman, then a self-ordained minister, a counterfeiter, a teacher of youth, a founder of libraries, and a miscellaneous philanthropist. He was a patriot and an optimist and an enthusiastic worker in the cause of general education. He was chock-full of fine sentiment and had a gift for its expression. He enjoyed doing good, though in his own way, and never neglected any opportunity to rebuke those who he felt were in the wrong. He had a desire to reform the world, and had no doubt of the plans which he elaborated. He was capable on occasions of acts of magnanimity, which, while not appreciated by the public, gave him great pleasure in the retrospect. The intervals between his various enterprises were spent in New England jails. These experiences only deepened his love of liberty, which was one of the passions of his life.

Burroughs had a happy disposition that enabled him to get a measure of satisfaction out of all the vicissitudes of his life. He had learned neither to worry nor to repine. He was not troubled by the harsh judgments of his fellow men, for he had learned to find his happiness in the approbation of his own conscience.

## Draft A

The rascals of fiction are never quite free. However lively they may be, there is always the author standing behind them, and the author has his opinion. He lets the rogue have his way for a chapter or two, but in the end he sits in judgment. The reader knows all the time that the scales are being kept, and that the account will be settled before the last page.

It is quite otherwise with the real rogue who writes his own memoir. There is no one behind him. He is his own author and his own judge, and as judge he is singularly indulgent. The swindler whose book I have been reading never once brings himself to trial. It does not occur to him that there is any case to answer. He is not defending himself, for he is not aware of being accused. He is simply relating the adventures of a virtuous man in a world that did not always appreciate him.

His career would have furnished material for half a dozen ordinary lives. He drifted into soldiering, and out of it. He drifted into preaching, and found the pulpit agreeable for a season. He drifted into forgery, which he seems to have regarded as another branch of the literary profession. He tried teaching, and doubtless taught what he knew. And from time to time he drifted into prison, which he accepted as one of the inconveniences of travel. There is no sign that he ever planned any of these changes. He went where the current took him, and found each landing-place as good as the last.

What is remarkable is that through all this he remained perfectly cheerful. Misfortune did not sour him, and disgrace did not humble him. He had one guide whom he never doubted, and that was his own conscience. Other men's opinions he treated as matters of local custom, interesting but not binding. His conscience, on the other hand, was a faithful companion that never found fault with him.

I confess that I envy him a little. Most of us have consciences of a more critical turn. His was the kind that makes a man happy, whatever it does for his neighbors.

## Draft B

The rascals of fiction are, after all, very carefully supervised. However freely the novelist allows his rogue to wander, he keeps an eye on him, and when the last chapter comes the author steps forward to pronounce judgment. The reader is never left in doubt about the moral bookkeeping. But the real rogue has no such guardian, and when he writes his own memoirs there is nobody to pass sentence on him except himself. That is precisely what makes the memoir of [name needed: the rogue] such an instructive document. It is the autobiography of a swindler who never once suspected that he was anything but a virtuous man.

His career, if it deserves so dignified a name, was remarkably various. He drifted into soldiering and out of it again, took up preaching for a season and forgery for a longer one, kept a school when the other professions had grown inconvenient, and spent a considerable portion of his life in prison, which he regarded as an unfortunate interruption of his usefulness and never as its natural consequence. Any one of these occupations might have given a thoughtful man some occasion for reflection. He passed through them all without reflecting at all.

"Surely," says the reader, "a man so often in trouble must have had some misgivings." Not at all. He was the most cheerful of companions to himself. Whenever the world condemned him he retired to consult his conscience, and his conscience, which had been educated in a very indulgent school and had never once been asked to examine the evidence, invariably acquitted him. He trusted it as other men trust a banker, and it never failed to honour his drafts.

There is something wholesome in such serenity, and I would not be too hard on it. Most of us spend a good deal of energy in condemning ourselves for small offences which nobody else has noticed. This man spared himself that labour entirely. He committed large offences, which everybody noticed, and then slept soundly. I cannot recommend his example, yet I confess I read his account of it with more envy than I care to admit. The fictional rogue is judged by his creator. The real one may appoint his own judge.

## Draft C

The rogues of fiction are always found out, if not by the constable then by the author. The novelist who invents a rascal stands a little behind him the whole time, and however much he enjoys the rascal's tricks he lets us see, by a word here and a turn of phrase there, that he knows what the fellow is. We laugh at the rogue, but we laugh at him from the author's side of the fence.

The memoir I have been reading offers no such fence. Its author is a real rogue, and he has written his own story, and there is nobody standing behind him to pass judgment, least of all himself. That is what makes the book remarkable. It is the record of a swindler who seems never once to have suspected that he was one.

His life, as he tells it, was a long drift from one occupation to another. He was a soldier for a time. He was a preacher. He took up forgery, and practised it with some care. He taught school. He spent a good many seasons in prison. He passes from each of these to the next without any sense that the transition calls for explanation, as a man might describe moving from one boarding house to another down the same street. What holds the whole thing together is his temper, which is unfailingly cheerful. Misfortune does not sour him and exposure does not shame him. When he is caught he is surprised, perhaps a little aggrieved, but never humbled, and he is soon looking about for the next opening. He has an answer for every charge, and the answer always is that he acted according to his conscience.

That conscience is the one authority he trusts. He does not consult the law, which has so often misunderstood him, nor the church, which he served for a while from the pulpit, nor the opinion of his neighbors. He consults himself, and he always finds himself in the right. There is something almost enviable in such serenity, and something very alarming. A villain who knows he is a villain can be reasoned with. A villain who is sure he is a good man has closed every door but his own.
