# Judge packet alexander-smith-09

## The passage (by the author)

Besides the itinerant lecturer, and the permanent library, we have the Sunday sermon. These sum up the intellectual aids and furtherances of the whole place. We have a church and a chapel, and I attend both. The Dreamthorp people are Dissenters, for the most part; why, I never could understand; because dissent implies a certain intellectual effort. But Dissenters they are, and Dissenters they are likely to remain. In an ungainly building, filled with hard gaunt pews, without an organ, without a touch of colour in the windows, with nothing to stir the imagination or the devotional sense, the simple people worship. On Sunday, they are put upon a diet of spiritual bread and water. Personally, I should desire more generous food. But the labouring people listen attentively, till once they fall asleep, and they wake up to receive the benediction with a feeling of having done their duty. They know they ought to go to chapel, and they go. I go likewise, from habit, although I have long ago lost the power of following a discourse. In my pew, and whilst the clergyman is going on, I think of the strangest things--of the tree at the window, of the congregation of the dead outside, of the wheat-fields and the corn-fields beyond and all around. And the odd thing is, that it is during sermon only that my mind flies off at a tangent and busies itself with things removed from the place and the circumstances. Whenever it is finished fancy returns from her wanderings, and I am alive to the objects around me. The clergyman knows my humour, and is good Christian enough to forgive me; and he smiles good-humouredly when I ask him to let me have the chapel keys, that I may enter, when in the mood, and preach a sermon to myself. To my mind, an empty chapel is impressive; a crowded one, comparatively a commonplace affair. Alone, I could choose my own text, and my silent discourse would not be without its practical applications.

## Draft A

Most of the village goes to chapel rather than church. The chapel is plain nonconformist, a square building with plain windows and plainer benches, and the worship inside it matches. There is no altar worth the name, no candles, no painted glass, no ceremony to speak of. There are hymns, a long prayer and a longer sermon, and then everyone goes home. The people who attend are proud of this bareness. To them it is not poverty but honesty, a way of meeting God without anything getting in the way.

I go too, most Sundays, though I would be hard pressed to give a good reason. It is habit more than conviction. I went as a boy, I have gone ever since, and not going would take more effort, and cause more comment, than going does. So I put on my good coat, take my usual seat near the back, and do as the others do.

I should confess that I do not listen very well. The sermons are long, and the minister's voice is steady and even, and somewhere around the second point my attention slips its moorings and drifts away. I find myself thinking about the week, or about nothing at all, watching the light move across the whitewashed wall or counting the panes in the window opposite. I stand when the others stand and sing when they sing, and I come out at the end with very little idea of what has been said. I am not proud of this, but I am not sure I am alone in it either.

What I like best about the chapel, oddly, is the chapel itself when no one is in it. I sometimes go in on a weekday, when the door is open for cleaning or airing, and sit for a while on my own. Empty, the building is completely quiet. There is no voice to follow and no reason to feel guilty for not following it. The bare walls and plain benches, which seem merely dull on a crowded Sunday, become restful. I suspect I get more out of those empty half-hours than out of all the sermons I have sat through. ## Draft B

The religious life of the village runs, for the most part, in a narrow and a plain channel. Most of the people follow the chapel--a bare, square, whitewashed building, as innocent of ornament as a barn, where they meet to worship in the manner their fathers taught them. There is no organ, no painted window, no vestment, nothing to please the eye or beguile the ear. The worship is as bare as the walls. They sing their hymns lustily, and a little out of tune; they pray at length, and with a fervour that would put many a cathedral to shame. Whatever else may be said of them, they are in earnest.

I go myself, now and then--more, I fear, from habit than from any warmth of conviction. I sit in a corner pew and listen, or seem to listen; but I confess that the sermon has seldom the whole of me. Before the preacher is well into his second head my thoughts have slipped away through the open window, and are wandering in the lanes, or lying on the hillside, or following a bee across the meadow. I come back with a start at the amen, and hope that no one has observed my absence. It is not that the preaching is bad; it is only that my mind, like a restless dog, will not stay where it is put.

But the chapel I like best is the chapel empty. Of a weekday afternoon, when the door stands open and there is no one within, I sometimes step inside and sit down. The bareness that seemed so chill on the Sunday is restful then. The sunlight lies in long squares upon the floor; a sparrow chirps in the porch; the silence is of the kind that one can hear. A full chapel speaks to me in the preacher's voice, and I do not always follow him. An empty chapel speaks in a voice of its own, and I have never yet failed to understand it.

## Draft C

It is generally believed that a man goes to church to be made better, and I would not dispute it; but I suspect that a good many of us go chiefly because we went last Sunday, and the Sunday before that. In our small village the greater part of the people follow the nonconformist chapel, a plain, square, unornamented building, as bare within as a barn swept for the harvest. There is no painted window there, no carved screen, no music but the voices of the congregation; the worship is as plain as the walls, and the worshippers are proud of its plainness, as a poor man is occasionally proud of his poverty. I attend with the rest, from habit and not from conviction, and I sit in my accustomed place and look attentive. Yet my attention, I confess, is a deceiver. The preacher begins his discourse, and before he has fairly reached his second head I am far away--in the fields, on the lake, among my books, anywhere but in the theological argument. The one part of me sits upright in the pew; the other has slipped out at the door and gone wandering. [example needed: a particular daydream during a sermon] Is it wicked to dream in chapel? I hope not. The mind that is too tired to follow a sermon may yet be quiet enough to receive a blessing. And, to tell the truth, I like the chapel best when there is nobody in it. On a week-day, when the door stands open and the sun lies across the empty benches, the place has a stillness which no congregation, however devout, can communicate to it; the dust floats in the light, a bird chirps in the eaves, and Silence, like a patient minister, preaches the only sermon I have never slept through. A full chapel speaks to the ear; an empty one speaks to the soul. Of the two, I verily believe, the empty one has done me the more good!
