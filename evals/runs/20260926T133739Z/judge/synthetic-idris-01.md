# Judge packet synthetic-idris-01

## The passage (by the author)

It would be easy to conclude from this that recurring meetings should simply be abolished, and there are teams that have tried precisely that experiment with a certain evangelical enthusiasm. I am not convinced that the conclusion follows. What the meeting provides, even in its degraded form, is a guaranteed occasion for contact — a moment when you can raise something half-formed without having to justify interrupting anyone, when the quieter members of a team can be seen and heard, when small frictions surface before they harden into grievances. Perhaps the ritual persists precisely because it performs this function, however inefficiently, and because nobody has designed a better substitute. In some sense the meeting is less a tool for making decisions than a piece of social infrastructure; its value is not visible in any particular session but in the accumulated familiarity it produces over months. Remove it without replacing that function, and we may discover that the team has become efficient in a way that is also, subtly, lonelier.

What, then, might we do differently? One possibility is to treat each recurring meeting as something that must periodically earn its renewal, rather than as a permanent fixture whose continuation requires no argument. A team might pause every so often and ask, with genuine openness, whether the original purpose still holds, whether a different purpose has replaced it, and whether that new purpose is worth the collective attention it consumes. The conversation is often uncomfortable, since it touches on questions of status and inclusion that are rarely discussed openly; the person who organised the meeting may feel that their judgement is being questioned, and the people who value it most may be the least inclined to say so. It is nevertheless the kind of discomfort that tends to be productive, because it forces the team to articulate what it actually wants from its time together. I suspect that many meetings would survive such scrutiny in a modified form — shorter, less frequent, or reframed around a clearer question — and that a few would be quietly and gratefully retired.

## Draft A

What is a standing team meeting actually for? The question is asked more frequently now, usually by someone looking at a crowded calendar and wondering which of its fixtures could be quietly removed without anyone noticing, and the standing meeting is the obvious candidate. It may be that the case against such meetings is weaker than it looks, because what they do is rarely written down in any agenda and is therefore easy to overlook. I suspect they matter more than we admit.

Consider what happens in a meeting whose ostensible business could have been settled by a short message. People arrive a few minutes early and talk about something unrelated; someone mentions, almost in passing, that a piece of work is taking longer than expected; someone else notices a slight edge in a colleague's voice and makes a point of asking about it afterwards. None of this appears in the minutes, and none of it would survive being moved into a written update that each person reads alone at a different hour. It is, in some sense, the connective tissue of a team, and the standing meeting is one of the few places where it is regularly exercised, so that minor tensions show themselves early, while they are still minor, rather than surfacing months later as something considerably harder to address.

There is also a quieter risk in scrapping such meetings outright, which is that a team meeting only when there is a stated reason to meet may become more efficient on paper while arguably becoming more isolated in practice, with each person left to infer the mood of the group from fragments. Is it any wonder that people in such teams report feeling less connected, even when the work itself is going well?

It seems to me that the answer is not abolition but periodic scrutiny, in which each standing meeting is asked, at intervals, to justify itself; its purpose restated, its length reconsidered, its attendance reviewed. Many will shrink under this kind of attention, and some will end, which is as it should be. Perhaps the trouble is not that we have too many meetings but that we have stopped asking what each of them is for.

## Draft B

There is a familiar complaint, voiced most often on a crowded Tuesday afternoon, that the standing team meeting has outlived whatever purpose it once served and ought simply to be abolished. The complaint is understandable, and in many cases it is at least partly justified. Most of us have sat through a weekly gathering in which the agenda was read aloud, a few updates were offered that could as easily have been written down, and everyone returned to their desks with the faint sense that an hour had been spent on nothing in particular. It is tempting, from that vantage point, to conclude that the meeting itself is the problem.

I am not sure that conclusion survives closer inspection. What the standing meeting provides is rarely visible on its agenda. It keeps people in one another's company on a regular basis; it gives colleagues a reason to hear each other's voices even in weeks when nothing much is happening; and, perhaps most usefully, it allows the small frictions of working together to surface while they are still small. A slightly sharp remark, a hesitation before agreeing, a silence where there might have been enthusiasm — these are the early signs of tension, and they are far easier to notice in a room than in a status document.

Removing the meeting outright, then, carries a risk that is easy to underestimate. The time is recovered at once, and the benefit is felt at once; the cost arrives later and more quietly, as a team whose members see one another less and understand one another less, and who may not realise how isolated they have become until something goes wrong between them.

A more careful approach would be to ask each recurring meeting to justify itself at regular intervals, rather than assuming either that it is necessary or that it is not. What is this gathering for? Would anyone miss it if it stopped? Could it be shorter, or less frequent? Many meetings, I suspect, would shrink under that questioning, and some would end altogether. But the ones that remained would be there because someone had chosen them, which is a rather different thing from being there because no one had thought to stop them.

## Draft C

Every few months someone suggests scrapping the standing team meeting. The argument is familiar: the meeting runs long, half the updates could have been a message, and people leave with less time than they arrived with. It is a fair complaint. Most of us have sat through a weekly meeting that existed mainly because it was on the calendar.

Still, I think scrapping them outright is the wrong answer, because the value of a standing meeting is rarely in its agenda. It is in the fact that the same people are in the same room, or on the same call, at a predictable time. That regular contact keeps a team connected in ways that are hard to see until they are gone. It is also where minor tensions show early. A clipped reply, a pause before someone agrees, a colleague who has gone quiet for two weeks running: these are small signals, and they tend to surface when people talk to each other, not when they post status updates into a channel. Catch them early and they stay small. Miss them and they grow.

Cut every standing meeting and you may win back some hours, but you also risk a team that feels more isolated. People stop bumping into each other's work. Questions that would have taken thirty seconds in the room turn into threads nobody quite owns. The quiet ones get quieter. None of that shows up in a calendar audit, which is exactly why it is easy to underestimate.

The better move is to make each standing meeting justify itself at intervals. Every so often, ask plainly: what is this meeting for, is it still doing that, and does it need this much time and this many people? Be willing to act on the answer. In my experience, many meetings shrink when you do this. An hour becomes thirty minutes, a large group becomes the few who actually need to be there. Some end altogether, and that is fine too, because they had stopped earning their place.

What you keep after that review is a set of meetings that exist on purpose. That is a far better outcome than keeping everything out of habit, or deleting everything out of frustration.
