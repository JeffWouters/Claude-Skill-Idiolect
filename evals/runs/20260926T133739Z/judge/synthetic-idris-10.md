## The passage (by the author)

There is also a subtler distortion at work. Status updates tend to describe activity rather than understanding; they report what has been done, which is easy to enumerate, rather than what has been learned, which is often more important but harder to articulate. A team may complete a great many tasks while its grasp of the underlying problem remains as uncertain as it was at the outset, and the update will faithfully record the tasks while saying almost nothing about the uncertainty. Conversely, a team may spend a period apparently producing little, while in fact resolving a fundamental question that will make everything subsequent far easier; the update, which has no category for such progress, will make that period look like a failure. You might say that the format rewards visible motion over invisible insight, and in doing so it shapes, perhaps more than we realise, what teams choose to work on.

What might a more truthful kind of update look like? It seems to me that it would make room for confidence as well as progress — for the reporter to say, in effect, how sure they are about what they are reporting, and what would change their mind. It would distinguish between the parts of the work that are well understood and the parts that remain obscure; it would treat an emerging concern as legitimate information rather than as an alarm that must be justified before it can be raised. Such updates are harder to write and harder to read, since they resist the comforting simplicity of a single colour. They also, I suspect, give the people who receive them a far more accurate sense of where things stand, which is presumably what the update was meant to provide in the first place.

Perhaps the difficulty of reporting status honestly is a symptom of something larger: the discomfort that institutions feel with uncertainty, and the corresponding pressure on individuals to present their work as more settled than it is. The update becomes a small performance of control, repeated at regular intervals, in which everyone understands that the performance is partly fictional but nobody wishes to be the first to abandon it. To change this is not a matter of redesigning a template but of cultivating a culture in which saying "I am not yet sure" is regarded as a sign of honesty rather than of weakness. We might discover, if we tried, that the most useful thing a status update can communicate is not how far we have come, but how clearly we can see the road ahead.

## Draft A

Progress reports occupy a strange position in the life of a team. They are written with some care, read with varying degrees of attention, and relied upon to convey the state of a piece of work to people who are not close enough to see it for themselves. Yet it is worth asking what, exactly, they convey, and whether it corresponds to what the team actually knows.

Most reports, in my experience, are organised around tasks. They list what has been completed since the last report, what is currently under way, and what remains to be done. This has the considerable advantage of being concrete and easily checked. Its disadvantage is less obvious but perhaps more important. A list of completed tasks tells us about activity; it tells us very little about understanding. A team may finish a dozen tasks in a week and learn almost nothing about whether its approach will work, or finish very few while discovering something that changes the whole shape of the project. The report, by its structure, rewards the first and renders the second nearly invisible. Over time this has a subtle effect on priorities, since the work that shows up well in reports is the work that tends to get done, while the slower business of reducing uncertainty is quietly deferred.

What might a better report look like? One possibility would be to include, alongside the familiar list of tasks, some statement of how confident the team is in its current plan, and on what grounds. Another would be to name explicitly the areas that remain unclear — the questions not yet answered, the assumptions not yet tested, the parts of the problem that no one yet fully understands. Such a report would be less tidy than the usual kind. It would also, I think, be considerably more truthful, and far more useful to anyone trying to decide what to do next.

It would be a mistake, however, to imagine that this is chiefly a matter of changing the template. A new format can be adopted in an afternoon; the difficulty lies in what happens when someone uses it honestly. In many organisations, to say that one is uncertain is to invite the suspicion that one is unprepared, and to name an unclear area is to seem to have failed to clarify it. As long as that remains true, people will continue to report what they have done rather than what they know, and no redesign will persuade them otherwise.

The real change, then, is cultural. It depends on coming to regard the admission of uncertainty not as a weakness to be managed but as a form of honesty to be valued — perhaps even as a sign that the person reporting has understood the work well enough to see where its difficulties lie.

## Draft B

Most progress reports answer one question: what got done? They list the tickets closed, the features shipped, the milestones passed. That is useful as far as it goes. But it misrepresents what teams actually know, because a list of completed tasks says nothing about the insight gained along the way.

Consider how much of a team's real progress is not a task at all. Discovering that an approach will not work. Learning that a dependency is shakier than it looked. Realising that the problem is different from the one originally described. These are often the most valuable things a team learns in a given period, and none of them fit neatly into a list of completed items. So they get left out, or buried in a note at the bottom that nobody reads.

That omission skews priorities. What gets reported gets rewarded, and what gets rewarded gets done. If reports only count finished tasks, teams learn to favour work that finishes cleanly and visibly. Exploratory work, the kind that reduces risk without producing anything you can tick off, starts to look like a poor use of time. The team ends up optimising for the appearance of progress rather than for understanding.

Better reports would look different. Alongside what was done, they would state confidence levels. How sure are we about the plan from here? Which parts do we understand well, and which are still guesses? They would flag unclear areas explicitly, not as an admission of failure but as useful information for anyone making decisions. A report that says "we are confident about these three things and unsure about this one" is far more useful than one that says everything is on track.

The format is not the hard part, though. You could introduce a confidence field tomorrow and nothing would change if people felt it was unsafe to use it honestly. The fix is cultural. It depends on treating an admission of uncertainty as honest rather than as weak. If a team lead writes "we do not know yet" and the response is concern about their competence, the next report will not say it. If the response is a useful conversation about how to find out, the next report will say it again.

That is the real test of a reporting culture: not how neat the reports look, but whether people can say what they do not know without paying for it. Get that right, and the reports will start to reflect what the team actually knows.

## Draft C

What does a progress report actually tell us about a team's work? The obvious answer is that it tells us what has been done, and that is precisely the difficulty, since what has been done is only a small part of what a team knows at any given moment. It may be that our reports, however diligently prepared, give a systematically misleading picture of where a project stands. I suspect most people who write them know this.

Consider what a typical report contains, and what it leaves out, because it lists completed tasks, closed tickets and delivered features, each of which can be counted and each of which looks like progress; it says very little about what the team has learned, what it now understands that it did not understand a month ago, or which of its original assumptions have quietly collapsed. That insight is nonetheless, arguably, the most valuable thing a team produces in the early stages of any difficult piece of work. A report that records only finished tasks rewards finishing tasks, and so, over time, it skews priorities towards whatever can be completed and shown, and away from the slower, less visible work of reducing uncertainty.

There is also the question of what a report leaves unsaid. Most formats have no place for doubt; there is nowhere to record that one part of the plan rests on a guess, that another depends on something nobody has yet tested, or that the team is simply unsure whether its approach will hold. The absence of such information is easily mistaken for its absence in reality.

It seems to me that better reports would state, alongside what has been done, how confident the team is in the remainder, and would flag plainly those areas where the picture is still unclear. This means giving uncertainty a named place in the format; it means treating a low confidence level as useful information rather than as a warning sign; it means, perhaps, asking what has been learned as routinely as we ask what has been finished. None of this is technically difficult, and none of it requires a new tool. [example needed: what such a report might look like in practice]

The harder part, in the end, is cultural. A team will only write honestly about uncertainty if it believes that doing so will be received as honesty rather than as weakness, and that belief is built slowly, by the way readers respond to the first few reports that admit they do not yet know. Perhaps the most accurate report is not the one that shows the most progress but the one that is least afraid of showing what is still unknown.
