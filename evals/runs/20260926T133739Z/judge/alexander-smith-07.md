# Judge packet alexander-smith-07

## The passage (by the author)

The ruined chapel adjoins the ruined castle on which I am now sitting, and is evidently a building of much older date. It is a mere shell now. It is quite roofless, ivy covers it in part; the stone tracery of the great western window is yet intact, but the coloured glass is gone with the splendid vestments of the abbot, the fuming incense, the chanting choirs, and the patient, sad-eyed monks, who muttered Aves, shrived guilt, and illuminated missals. Time was when this place breathed actual benedictions, and was a home of active peace. At present it is visited only by the stranger, and delights but the antiquary. The village people have so little respect for it, that they do not even consider it haunted. There are several tombs in the interior bearing knights' escutcheons, which time has sadly defaced. The dust you stand upon is noble. Earls have been brought here in dinted mail from battle, and earls' wives from the pangs of child-bearing. The last trumpet will break the slumber of a right honourable company. One of the tombs--the most perfect of all in point of preservation--I look at often, and try to conjecture what it commemorates. With all my fancies, I can get no further than the old story of love and death. There, on the slab, the white figures sleep; marble hands, folded in prayer, on marble breasts. And I like to think that he was brave, she beautiful; that although the monument is worn by time, and sullied by the stains of the weather, the qualities which it commemorates--husbandly and wifely affection, courtesy, courage, knightly scorn of wrong and falsehood, meekness, penitence, charity--are existing yet somewhere, recognisable by each other. The man who in this world can keep the whiteness of his soul, is not likely to lose it in any other.

## Draft A

Beside the castle ruin stands a small medieval chapel, in rather worse repair than the castle itself. The roof is patched where it has not fallen, the windows have lost most of their glass, and the stone is softening in the damp like old bread. It is easy to walk past without noticing it at all, and most people do.

Whatever religious life once went on here has long since gone. No services are held, and there is no sign that anyone remembers when the last one was. The ornament has gone too: the painted walls are bare plaster, the carving is broken or worn smooth, and whatever furnishings the chapel once had were taken away long ago. The villagers ignore it entirely. It is not a place they visit, or mention, or seem to think about. It is simply there, like a boulder in a field.

Yet inside, if you push open the door, you find that the chapel is not empty. Aristocratic tombs lie along its floor and walls, the resting places of the family who once held the castle next door. Most of the inscriptions are too worn to read. One tomb, though, has kept its figures. It shows a lord and his lady lying side by side, their hands joined, carved as a devoted couple who meant to be seen together for as long as the stone would last. Whoever made it wanted that devotion remembered more than any title or deed.

I found it more moving than I expected. The chapel's purpose has failed, its beauty has gone, its builders are forgotten. But the virtue the tomb honours, that simple faithfulness between two people, does not seem to have faded with the rest. Perhaps such things endure beyond death in a way that ornament and ceremony do not. The carving is crumbling. What it records, I suspect, is not.

## Draft B

Hard by the castle ruin, and sharing its decay, stands the old chapel. It is a small place, and a sad one. Whatever life of worship it once held has long since departed; the altar is gone, the images are gone, the painted glass is gone, and the carved work that once made it fair has crumbled away, or been carried off to mend a pigsty. The wind comes in where the windows were, and the ivy follows the wind. The villagers pass it every day and never look at it. To them it is a heap of old stones, a little less useful than a barn; and I do not know that I can blame them, for a thing that has ceased to serve is soon ceased to be seen.

And yet the chapel is not empty. Beneath its broken floor, and along its mouldering walls, lie the great folk of the castle--lords and ladies who in their day could have bought the whole village and never missed the price. Their tombs remain, though the hands that tended them are dust. On one of these a lord and his lady lie side by side in stone, their hands joined, their faces turned a little toward each other, as if even in death they were loth to part. The sculptor, whoever he was, has made them a devoted couple; and I like to believe he did not lie.

Standing there of an evening, I have sometimes wondered what, of all that was honoured in this place, is left. The pomp is gone, the ritual is gone, the very names are half effaced. But the virtues that the stone was set up to commemorate--faithfulness, and constancy, and love that holds on to the end--these are not of the stuff that crumbles. The chapel may fall, and the tomb with it; but it may be that what those two folded hands were meant to say outlasts them both, and is still saying it somewhere beyond the reach of the weather.

## Draft C

We are apt to suppose that a building dies when its use departs from it; but a building, like a man, may outlive its occupation by a long while, and grow more interesting in its decay. Beside the castle ruin stands a little chapel of the same age, crumbling quietly into the grass. Its religious life has long since gone out of it. No bell calls to it; no priest serves in it; the carving and colour that once adorned it have fallen or faded, and the walls stand bare to the weather. The villagers pass it every day without a glance--it is part of the commonplace to them, like the hedge and the milestone--and I suspect that hardly one of them could tell you what lies within. Yet within lie the old lords of the castle, sleeping in their tombs while their towers tumble overhead. On one of these the sculptor has pictured a husband and wife lying side by side, hand clasped in hand, as though Death himself, who parts most couples, had been persuaded for once to let this pair go on together. [example needed: a detail of the carving] The stone has worn; the faces are blurred; but the devotion is plain. Is it all over with them? I cannot think so. Their titles are forgotten, their castle is a heap, their chapel is a shell. But the virtues honoured on that tomb--faithfulness, tenderness, constancy--are not things that crumble with mortar. Just as the scent of a crushed herb clings to the hand long after the herb is thrown away, so these qualities cling about the place; and it may be that they endure, beyond the chapel and the tomb, wherever such qualities go. Love was the one possession of theirs that the centuries could not steal!
