## The passage (by the author)

And then the weft. This is the part I like. I go across, side to side, over one line and under the next. Over, under, over, under. At the end of each row I turn and come back. Under where I went over. Over where I went under.

Slowly a small square of cloth grows over the hole. A woven patch. Firm and flat.

The rule is to keep it loose. Not slack. Loose. If you pull tight, the patch puckers. Then it rubs a blister on your heel. I learned that on darn number two.

A patch the size of a coin takes me 30 minutes. A big heel hole takes an hour. I do it in the evening, in the chair by the lamp. The radio on. A cup of tea going cold on the arm.

Some people buy new socks. I know. Socks are cheap. A new pair costs less than an hour of my time, if you count it that way.

But I do not count it that way. The hour is not work. It is a quiet hour with my hands busy. And the sock is better after. A darned heel is thicker than the old heel. It lasts longer than the wool around it. So the next hole is always somewhere new.

My grandfather darned his own socks. He was a fisherman. He could darn in the dark by feel. He showed me once, when I was small. I did not pay much attention. Years later I found his mushroom in a box. Dark wood, worn smooth. I tried to use it. I could not remember how. So I taught myself from a book. The bulb works better than the mushroom for me. I kept the mushroom anyway.

These grey socks will not last for ever. The wool at the ankle is getting thin now, all the way round. That is hard to darn. When that goes, they go.

## Draft A

Now the crosswise threads. The lengthwise ones are already in, a row of parallel lines over the hole. Now you go the other way. Over one, under one. Then back again, under where you went over. That is the weave. It is slow. It is meant to be.

The one thing to watch is the tension. Do not pull the threads tight. It is tempting. A tight thread looks neat. But a tight patch puckers. The sock goes on, the heel stretches, and the patch drags on the good wool round it. Then that gives instead. Leave each thread a little slack. It should lie flat and give when you press it.

When the gap is filled, take the ends back into the wool on the inside. Trim them. Turn the sock the right way out. Done.

## Draft B

The last stage of darning a sock is the part I enjoy most. The lengthwise threads are already in place across the hole, and now the crosswise threads go in, weaving over and under, back and forth, until the gap closes into a small patch of fabric.

The one thing to watch at this stage is tension. It is tempting to pull each thread snug so that the weave looks neat and tight, but a patch pulled taut will pucker, and a puckered patch is uncomfortable to wear and pulls on the surrounding fabric. I let each crosswise thread lie easily, with just enough tension to sit flat. The weave should look relaxed rather than stretched.

It is slow work, and that is part of its appeal. I have come to find the time spent darning genuinely restful. There is nothing to decide once the pattern is set, only the steady rhythm of the needle going over and under. It asks for enough attention to keep the mind from wandering off into worry, but not so much that it becomes tiring.

It is also worth doing for practical reasons. A well-darned heel often outlasts the original fabric around it. The patch is denser than the knitting it replaces, and it tends to hold up long after the rest of the sock has started to thin.

I did not learn this from a class. I first saw a darning tool in the hands of a grandparent, [detail needed], and that memory stayed with me long enough that I eventually decided to teach myself. Much of what I know came from trial and error, a few [sources needed], and a fair number of lumpy early patches.

Darning does not make a sock last forever. Sooner or later a pair wears through at the ankle, where the fabric is harder to support and repair, and that is usually the end of it. But until then, each mended heel buys another season of wear, and a little quiet time as well.

## Draft C

I finished the heel last night. It took longer than I meant it to.

The long threads were already in. Straight lines across the hole, fixed in the good wool on each side. So what was left was the weaving. This is the part that turns a hole into cloth.

Here is how it goes. Take the needle across the long threads. Over one, under the next. Then back the other way. Under where you went over, over where you went under. Row after row. Each cross thread sits close to the last.

The mistake is to pull. You want a firm patch, so you pull the thread snug. Do not. A taut thread puckers the patch, and a puckered patch rubs. You will feel it. So leave each row a little slack. Slacker than you think. The wool will settle when you wear it.

I pulled too hard on my first darns. Every one of them. I still catch myself doing it.

And it is slow. There is no way round that. [number needed] hours a heel. But the time does not feel wasted. It feels restful. The needle goes over and under. Your hands know the pattern after a few rows. Your head goes quiet. I do not get that from much else.

So why bother? Socks are cheap. But a darned heel lasts longer than the one it mends. That is the test. If the darn outlasts the sock around it, the darn was worth doing.
