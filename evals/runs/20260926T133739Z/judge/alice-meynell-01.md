# Judge packet alice-meynell-01

## The passage (by the author)

Even more wanton than the disclosure of a death is that of a mortal illness. If the man had recovered, his illness would have been rightly his own secret. But because he did not recover, it is assumed to be news for the first comer. Which of us would suffer the details of any physical suffering, over and done in our own lives, to be displayed and described? This is not a confidence we have a mind to make; and no one is authorised to ask for attention or pity on our behalf. The story of pain ought not to be told of us, seeing that by us it would assuredly not be told.

There is only one other thing that concerns a man still more exclusively, and that is his own mental illness, or the dreams and illusions of a long delirium. When he is in common language not himself, amends should be made for so bitter a paradox; he should be allowed such solitude as is possible to the alienated spirit; he should be left to the "not himself," and spared the intrusion against which he can so ill guard that he could hardly have even resented it.

The double helplessness of delusion and death should keep the door of Rossetti's house, for example, and refuse him to the reader. His mortal illness had nothing to do with his poetry. Some rather affected objection is taken every now and then to the publication of some facts (others being already well known) in the life of Shelley. Nevertheless, these are all, properly speaking, biography. What is not biography is the detail of the accident of the manner of his death, the detail of his cremation. Or if it was to be told--told briefly--it was certainly not for marble. Shelley's death had no significance, except inasmuch as he died young. It was a detachable and disconnected incident. Ah, that was a frost of fancy and of the heart that used it so, dealing with an insignificant fact, and conferring a futile immortality. Those are ill- named biographers who seem to think that a betrayal of the ways of death is a part of their ordinary duty, and that if material enough for a last chapter does not lie to their hand they are to search it out. They, of all survivors, are called upon, in honour and reason, to look upon a death with more composure. To those who loved the dead closely, this is, for a time, impossible. To them death becomes, for a year, disproportionate. Their dreams are fixed upon it night by night. They have, in those dreams, to find the dead in some labyrinth; they have to mourn his dying and to welcome his recovery in such a mingling of distress and of always incredulous happiness as is not known even to dreams save in that first year of separation. But they are not biographers.

## Draft A

It is the custom of biographers to follow a famous man to his bed, to sit there through the last weeks, and to report to the world how he coughed, how he wandered, and how at length he failed. They do it without apology, and with a certain pride in their thoroughness. The reader is told the name of the malady and the progress of the fever, what the physicians said and what they could not say, and at last the day, the hour, the final breath; and all this accumulation is called completeness. Yet no one would think of publishing, in the same particularity, the history of an illness from which the man recovered, for that illness would be considered his own affair. Why, then, should the one that killed him be ours? A sickness does not become public property merely because it happened to be the last. It was private while he suffered it, and it is private still.

Of all such privacies, the privacy of a disordered mind should be guarded the most scrupulously. If the body in its weakness deserves a veil, how much more the intelligence in its wandering! Madness is not the man, and delirium is not the man; what he said in fever he did not say--or would not have said, had he been himself. To set down those broken words, to weigh them, to discover in them some revelation of the secret character, is to take the one moment when he could not defend himself and make it the moment by which he is remembered. I fear this is done more often than we suppose, and with a multitudinous industry that mistakes the gathering of particulars for the understanding of a person. It is not knowledge but trespass.

Nor are deathbed details biography at all, in any true sense of the word. A life is the thing a man accomplished with his health and his wits--his work, his friendships, his decisions, his long habitualness of thought and conduct. The dying is not the life but the conclusion of the life, and very frequently the least characteristic portion of it. The strong man grows weak, the eloquent man grows silent, and the gentle man, it may be, grows fretful and suspicious. Which of these is the portrait? Surely none of them. A biographer who lingers in that chamber has confused the event that closed a career with the career itself, and has devoted his final chapter to the one season in which his subject was least himself.

There are those, indeed, who may be forgiven for dwelling upon these hours. The wife, the child, the friend who watched--they must needs remember, and they remember because they loved. Grief has its own right to the sickroom, since it was present there and kept the vigil, and its lingering is only the lingering of an affection that does not know how to depart. But the biographer was not present, and he kept no vigil. His lingering is not grief but curiosity, and curiosity has no such excuse. Let him leave the room to those who mourn, for it was never his.

## Draft B

It is a curious thing that the biographer, who would not dream of publishing the particulars of a fever from which his subject rose up and went about his business, should think himself bound to publish the particulars of the fever from which he did not rise. The one sickness was private; so, surely, was the other. That the second ended in death makes it no more the world's affair than the first. A man's body, in its weakness, belonged to him while he lived, and it is not by dying that he gives it up to the reading public. Nay, the illness that was recovered from was at least survived and, as it were, put by; the survivor might have told of it himself, had he cared to tell. The fatal illness he never had the opportunity to tell or to withhold. He is the more defenceless, and his reticence should be the more respected.

If this is true of the body, how much more is it true of the mind in its disorder. Madness is a privacy within a privacy. Delirium is the confession of a man who is not there to confess. What he said in his wandering hours he did not say; it was said through him, by a stranger borrowing his voice and his bed. To print those sayings is to attribute to the man the words of his disease. And there is in it, besides, a kind of trespass that has no name, for it enters not merely a house but a house whose master is away. Of all silences, this is the one a decent writer should keep most jealously.

Nor let it be pretended that the deathbed is biography. Biography is the history of a life--of what was done, and thought, and made, and loved. The last days are, for the most part, the days in which nothing of the kind was possible. They show us not the man but the common end of men, the end that he shares with everyone and that tells us nothing of what was his alone. Who, for instance, but learns more of a poet from one line of his verse than from a whole chapter of his breathing and his sleeping, his lucid intervals and his relapses? The chapter is long, doubtless, and it is exact; it is also, in the strict sense, irrelevant. It would be irrelevant were it written of any man, and it is written of this one only because he happened to be famous.

There are those, indeed, who may be forgiven for lingering there. The wife, the child, the friend who watched--these may go back and back to the last room and the last hours. Their grief has its own rights, and one of them is the right to repeat. They are not writing a life; they are keeping a vigil after the vigil is over. But the biographer keeps no vigil. He has no loss to excuse him, and what is pardonable, even tender, in the mourner becomes in him merely an intrusion. [example needed]

## Draft C

There is a habit among biographers that deserves more resistance than it gets: the habit of telling us, in close and lingering detail, how a famous person fell ill and how they died. We have come to treat the final illness as a natural chapter of any life story, as though the reader were owed it along with the birth, the schooling, the marriage and the work. I do not think the reader is owed it at all.

Consider how we would regard the same sickness had the person recovered. If a writer caught a fever at forty, lay in bed for a month and then got up again, no sensible biographer would reconstruct the temperature charts, the sleepless nights and the indignities of the sickroom. We would call that intrusive, and we would be right. A body in distress is a private matter. It does not become less private because the distress happened to be the last one. A fatal sickness is simply a sickness that did not end well, and it belongs to the person who suffered it just as much as a recovered one would.

The case is stronger still when the illness touched the mind. Madness and delirium strip a person of the very thing a biography is meant to honour: their own voice, their judgement, their considered self. To record what someone said while raving, or how they behaved when they no longer knew where they were, is to put on display a figure they would not have recognised and could not have defended. If ordinary illness deserves discretion, the wandering of a failing mind deserves something closer to silence. Whatever was said in that state was not said by the person whose life we are supposedly examining.

Some will answer that deathbed scenes reveal character, that a person's last days show who they really were. I doubt it. A life is revealed in its choices, its work and its long habits, not in the narrow, exhausted hours when choice has mostly been taken away. The deathbed tells us about the disease far more than about the man or woman in the bed. Details of that kind are not real biography; they are medical record dressed up as insight, and they tend to crowd out the parts of a life that were actually lived.

There is one exception, and it is a human one. Those who loved the dying person may be forgiven for dwelling on the end. Grief goes over the last conversations again and again, remembers the final look, the last word, the hour of the change. That is how mourning works, and nobody should begrudge it. But the grieving have a claim that the public does not. What is natural in a widow's memoir or a son's letter becomes something else when a stranger, years later, assembles the same material for general readers.

So I would ask biographers to stop where the life stops being lived and starts merely being endured. A sentence to say that the subject fell ill and died is enough. The rest belongs to the dead, and to those who mourned them.
