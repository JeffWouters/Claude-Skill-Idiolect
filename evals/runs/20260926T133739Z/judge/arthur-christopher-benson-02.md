# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-02

## The passage (by the author)

Perhaps one ought not to use the word dulness, because it may be misunderstood. The kind of dulness of which I speak is not inconsistent with a high degree, not only of practical, but even of mental, ability. I know several people of very great intellectual power who are models of dulness. Their memories are loaded with what is no doubt very valuable information, and their conclusions are of the weightiest character; but they have no vivid perception, no alertness, they are not open to new ideas, they never say an interesting or a suggestive thing; their presence is a load on the spirits of a lively party, their very facial expression is a rebuke to all light-mindedness and triviality. Sometimes these people are silent, and then to be in their presence is like being in a thick mist; there is no outlook, no enlivening prospect. Sometimes they are talkers; and I am not sure that that is not even worse, because they generally discourse on their own subjects with profound and serious conviction. They have no power of conversation, because they are not interested in any one else's point of view; they care no more who their companions are, than a pump cares what sort of a vessel is put under it--they only demand that people should listen in silence. I remember not long ago meeting one of the species, in this case an antiquarian. He discoursed continuously, with a hard eye, fixed as a rule upon the table, about the antiquities of the neighbourhood. I was on one side of him, and was far too much crushed to attempt resistance. I ate and drank mechanically; I said "Yes" and "Very interesting" at intervals; and the only ray of hope upon the horizon was that the hands of the clock upon the mantelpiece did undoubtedly move, though they moved with leaden slowness. On the other side of the savant was a lively talker, Matthews by name, who grew very restive under the process. The great man had selected Dorchester as his theme, because he had unhappily discovered that I had recently visited it. My friend Matthews, who had been included in the audience, made desperate attempts to escape; and once, seeing that I was fairly grappled, began a conversation with his next neighbour. But the antiquary was not to be put off. He stopped, and looked at Matthews with a relentless eye. "Matthews," he said, "MATTHEWS!" raising his voice. Matthews looked round. "I was saying that Dorchester was a very interesting place." Matthews made no further attempt to escape, and resigned himself to his fate.

## Draft A

It is a curious and rather melancholy fact that cleverness and knowledge are no sort of guarantee that a man will be good company. One would suppose that a person who had read widely, and thought a good deal, and had a quick and capable mind, would be the most delightful of companions; and yet I have known many such people who could reduce any gathering, in a quarter of an hour, to a condition of dull and hopeless endurance. They are not stupid; that is the worst of it. If they were stupid, one could bear with them, or escape them; but they are formidable, and they know a great deal, and they are quite determined that one shall know it too.

The root of the matter is, I think, that such people have no curiosity about the minds of others. They do not care in the least what their hearers think; they do not ask, and if an opinion is offered they wait, with a patient and absent air, until it is finished, and then resume exactly where they left off. Talk, to them, is not an exchange at all; it is a lecture, and the company are simply the audience, who are expected to sit still and be instructed. The subject may be interesting enough in itself; but there is nothing so wearisome as interesting information poured out upon people who have not asked for it, and who are given no chance of turning it to any account of their own.

I remember a dinner, not long ago, which illustrated this very vividly. Among the guests was a man who was an enthusiast for the history of the neighbourhood; he knew, I believe, everything that could be known about it, and he was resolved that the knowledge should not be wasted. He fastened early in the evening upon two of the guests, and held them, as it were, in a corner, while he unfolded the whole matter before them, point by point. One of them, after a long while, made a gallant attempt to slip away; he half rose, murmured something, and edged towards the rest of the company. But the historian was not to be baulked; he put out a hand, drew the fugitive back, and went on with his narrative as though nothing had happened; and the poor man sat down again with a face of such resignation as I have seldom seen.

I do not say that the historian was a bad man, or even an unkind one. He simply did not perceive that his hearers were suffering; and it is this blindness, far more than any want of wit or learning, that makes a bore. A very ordinary person, who is willing to listen and to be interested, is worth a dozen such scholars at a dinner-table.

## Draft B

It is commonly supposed, and I confess that I find the supposition hard to abandon, that cleverness and learning are a sure passport to agreeable society, and that one has only to gather a few well-stored minds about a table to be certain of a lively evening. Yet brains and knowledge are no guarantee of good company; they no more ensure it than a well-filled cellar ensures a pleasant host. Some of the dullest hours that one can spend are spent in the society of people who know a great deal, and who are determined, at whatever cost to their hearers, that the fact shall not be concealed.

What is the mark of such a bore? It is, I think, that he has no curiosity about other minds. He does not converse, he lectures; he does not wait for a question, or care for an objection, or pause to discover whether his audience is interested; he simply pours out, like water from a pump, whatever happens to be uppermost in his mind, and he would be rather astonished to learn that anybody else had an opinion upon the subject. The worst of it is that he is often a perfectly amiable and honourable person, whom one would not willingly wound; and so one sits, and nods, and endures.

There was once a dinner which illustrates the whole matter admirably. Among the guests was an enthusiast for local history, a man, so to speak, of one idea, and that idea the antiquities of his own neighbourhood. He fastened upon two of the company and held them captive; and when one of the two, in a moment of desperation, attempted to slip quietly away, the enthusiast put out a hand, drew him back to his place, and continued the story exactly where he had left it. There was no malice in it; there was simply no perception that the victim wished to escape. It is, perhaps, as perfect a picture of the whole tribe as one could desire!

Of course one must not be too severe, for the man who knows a subject thoroughly has something to give, and there are occasions when one is grateful for a lecture. But talk is not an exchange of information; it is an exchange of minds, a game in which the pleasure lies in the give and take, in the sudden agreement, the unexpected difference, the thought half-formed and completed by another. The learned bore has never discovered this; he mistakes the audience for the conversation, and he goes home, I have no doubt, perfectly satisfied, leaving behind him a little group of exhausted listeners, staring silently at the empty chairs.

## Draft C

It is a curious thing, and I have never quite got used to it, that some of the cleverest people I know are among the dullest to be with. One assumes that brains and knowledge must make for lively company. A person who has read a great deal and thought hard about it, surely, will have something to offer at the table. Sometimes that is true. But often enough it is the very reverse, and the more a person knows, the more thoroughly they can drain the life out of a room.

The trouble is not the knowledge itself. It is what they do with it. The clever bore does not converse; he lectures. He has no real interest in what anyone else thinks, and if you venture a view of your own he waits, politely or otherwise, for you to finish so that he can carry on where he left off. A question is not an opening to him but an interruption. He mistakes an audience for a companion, and he never seems to notice that the audience has stopped listening some time ago.

I remember a dinner where this was shown to me with unusual clarity. Among the guests was an enthusiast for local history, who evidently knew a great deal about the district and had decided that the rest of us should know it too. The enthusiast fastened on two of the guests and did not let them go. Every attempt to steer the talk elsewhere was met with another story and another detail from the district's past. At one point one of the two saw a chance and began, very quietly, to edge away towards another part of the room. I watched it with some sympathy. But the enthusiast was having none of it. He reached out, drew the poor fellow back by the arm, and resumed exactly where he had stopped, as though nothing had happened. The escape had failed, and both captives spent the rest of the evening where he had put them.

What struck me afterwards was that the man was not stupid, nor, I think, unkind. He simply had no sense that conversation is a shared thing. Everything he said might have been interesting in the right setting, in a book or in answer to a question someone had actually asked. Pressed on unwilling listeners, it became a burden.

I have come to think that good company depends far less on how much a person knows than on how much attention they are willing to give. A person of quite ordinary learning who listens, who is curious about you, who lets the talk go where it wants, will do more for a gathering than any number of experts. Cleverness without that attention is not a gift to others at all. It is only a way of keeping them captive.
