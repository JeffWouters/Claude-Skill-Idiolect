# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-07

## The passage (by the author)

My own belief is that every one has a perfect right to choose his own circle, and to make it large or small as he desires. It is a monstrous thing to hold that, if an agreeable or desirable person comes to a place, one has but to leave a piece of pasteboard at his door to entail upon him the duty of coming round till he finds one at home, and of disporting himself gingerly, like a dancing bear among the teacups. A card ought to be a species of charity, left on solitary strangers, to give them the chance of coming, if they like, to see the leaver of it, or as a preliminary to a real invitation. It ought to be a ticket of admission, which a man may use or not as he likes, not a legal summons. That any one should return a call should be a compliment and an honour, not regarded as the mere discharging of a compulsory duty.

I have heard fair ladies complain of the boredom they endured at tea-parties; they speak of themselves as the martyrs and victims of a sense of duty. If such people talked of the duty of visiting the sick and afflicted as a thing which their conception of Christian love entailed upon them, which they performed, reluctantly and unwillingly, from a sense of obligation, I should respect them deeply and profoundly. But I have not often found that the people who complain most of their social duties, and who discharge them most sedulously, complain because such duties interrupt a course of Christian beneficence. It is, indeed, rather the other way; it is generally true that those who see a good deal of society (from a sense of duty) and find it dull, are the people who have no particular interests or pursuits of their own.

## Draft A

I hold, as a first principle of social life, that every one has a perfect right to choose his own friends, and to make his own circle as wide or as narrow as he pleases. It seems a simple enough proposition; and yet the whole machinery of visiting and card-leaving appears to be designed, so to speak, to deny it, and to bind us to a round of acquaintance which we did not choose and cannot easily escape.

Take the humble visiting card. What ought it to be? I think it ought to be an invitation, and nothing more; a little signal that one would be glad of further acquaintance, which the receiver may take up or let fall exactly as he likes. But it has become, in too many households, a kind of summons, a debt which must be repaid within a fixed time, on pain of giving offence; and so the cards go to and fro, like shuttles in a loom, weaving a web of obligation in which nobody is particularly happy and from which nobody dares to break loose.

Of course one would not wish to be ungracious, and there is a certain small courtesy in these forms which it would be churlish to despise. But the remedy is not a revolution; it is simply a little honesty, a willingness to let the card mean what it says, and to leave people free to respond or not.

It is a curious thing, and I have perhaps been slow to notice it, that the people who grumble most loudly about the tedium of social duties are very often those who have no hobbies or interests of their own. They complain of their visits; but they would be at a loss, I suspect, if the visits ceased, for they have nothing with which to fill the empty afternoons. The man with a garden, a book, a craft or a pursuit is seldom oppressed by his acquaintances; he simply finds that he has no time for them, and they, like a tide on a quiet shore, slowly withdraw.

## Draft B

It seems to me a plain principle, though it is often forgotten, that everyone has the right to choose their own friends. We do not expect to be told which books to read or which walks to take, and I see no reason why we should be told whose drawing room we must sit in. Society has built up a whole apparatus of calls and cards and return visits which rather assumes the opposite: that once you are introduced to someone, or once they have moved into your street, you are bound to them by a set of obligations you never agreed to.

The calling card is the clearest example. In itself it is a pleasant enough invention. Left at a door, it could simply say: I am here, I should be glad to know you, come and see me if you like. That is how it ought to work, as an invitation that the other person is entirely free to take up or let drop. The trouble begins when it becomes a debt, when leaving one card means another must be returned within a certain time, and a visit paid, and a visit repaid, whether or not either party has the slightest wish to see the other. At that point the card stops being a courtesy and becomes a small tax on everyone's time.

I have noticed something else, too. The people who grumble most about dull social duties are not, as a rule, the busiest people. More often they are the ones with no hobbies or interests of their own. Having nothing in particular to do, they drift into the round of calls because it fills the hours, and then complain that the hours are empty. Those who have something they care about seldom have that problem. They choose their company as they choose everything else, and they are the better company for it.

## Draft C

It seems to me a very simple principle, and yet one that is constantly forgotten, that every one has a right to choose his own friends. We do not permit any one to dictate to us what books we shall read, or what food we shall eat, or how we shall spend our holidays; and yet in the matter of our acquaintance, which touches our happiness far more nearly, we submit to a whole code of rules which were made by nobody in particular and suit nobody at all.

Take the matter of cards. A card left at a house ought, I think, to mean no more than this: that the person who leaves it would be glad to know the household better, if the household is so inclined. It should be an invitation, and a perfectly optional one, which may be taken up or left alone without offence on either side. But what it has come to mean is something quite different; it has become a debt, which must be paid in the same coin within a certain time, and the failure to pay it is regarded as a kind of social dishonesty. So people go on exchanging cards and calls with others for whom they care nothing, simply because the machinery, once started, cannot be stopped.

And yet I have observed one thing which makes me a little less sorry for the sufferers than I might be. It is, as a rule, those who grumble most loudly about the tedium of social duties who have the fewest interests of their own. The man who has a pursuit that he loves, a hobby, a study, a craft, is seldom much troubled by these things; he finds time for what he cares about, and lets the rest go. It is the person with an empty day who finds the calls so wearisome, and who yet cannot bring himself to give them up.
