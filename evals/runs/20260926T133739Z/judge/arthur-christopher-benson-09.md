## The passage (by the author)

And from that moment the deterioration began. At first the great secret ran silently through the world from soul to soul, till the world was leavened. But even so the process of capturing and transforming the faith in accordance with human weakness began. The intellectual spirit laid hold on it first. Metaphysicians scrutinized the humble and sweet mystery, overlaid it with definitions, harmonized it with ancient systems, dogmatized it, made it hard, and subtle, and uninspiring. Vivid metaphors and illustrations were seized upon and converted into precise statements of principles. The very misapprehensions of the original hearers were invested with the same sanctity that belonged to the Master Himself. But even so the bright and beautiful spirit made its way, like a stream of clear water, refreshing thirsty places and making the desert bloom like the rose, till at last the world itself, in the middle of its luxuries and pomp, became aware that here was a mighty force abroad which must be reckoned with; and then the world itself determined upon the capture of Christianity; and how sadly it succeeded can be read in the pages of history; until at last the pure creature, like a barbarian captive, bright with youth and beauty, was bound with golden chains, and bidden, bewildered and amazed, to grace the triumph and ride in the very chariot of its conqueror.

Let me take one salient instance. Could there, to any impartial observer, be anything in the world more incredible than that the Pope, surrounded by ritual and pomp, and hierarchies, and policies, should be held to be the representative on earth of the peasant-teacher of Galilee? And yet the melancholy process of development is plain enough. As the world became Christianized, it could not be expected to give up its social order, its ambitions, its love of power and influence. Christianity uncurbed is an inconvenient, a dangerous, a subversive force; it must be tamed and muzzled; it must be robed and crowned; it must be given a high and honoured place among institutions. And so it has fallen a victim to bribery and intrigue and worldly power.

## Draft A

It is one of the saddest things in history, I think, to watch what befell the teaching of Christ in the centuries that followed Him. The message itself was of the simplest kind; it was addressed to plain people, it dealt with plain things, love and forgiveness and trust and humility; and it could be understood by a child. But it had hardly begun to spread before it fell into the hands of the philosophers; and the philosophers, who could not bear to leave anything simple, set to work upon it. They defined and distinguished and argued; they built up a great structure of doctrine, each part of which had to be held exactly, on pain of exclusion; and the living word became a system, rigid and elaborate, which only the learned could fully understand.

And then came a worse thing. The faith, which had begun among the poor and the despised, attracted at last the notice of the powerful; and the powerful, finding that they could not crush it, did what the powerful always do: they took it over. They gave it wealth and rank and privilege; they made it part of the machinery of the state; and in so doing they tamed it. What had been a spirit became an institution, with offices and revenues and a hierarchy of its own, and with all the interests that institutions have in keeping things as they are.

One has only to think of the papacy in its grandeur to see how far the drift has carried us. [example needed: a particular scene or period of papal splendour] The palaces, the ceremonies, the jewelled robes, the claims to temporal sovereignty; and then, set beside them, the figure of the humble Galilean teacher, who had not where to lay His head, and who walked the lanes with a few fishermen. I do not say this in any spirit of bitterness; the men who built the system were, many of them, sincere. But it is impossible not to feel that something precious was lost on the way.

## Draft B

The message that came out of Galilee was, at the start, a very simple one. It was addressed to ordinary people, in ordinary words, and it asked of them something that anyone could understand even if few could manage it: to love God, to love one another, to turn from selfishness and live differently. What happened to it afterwards is one of the saddest stories I know, and it happened in two ways at once.

The first was the work of the intellect. As the faith spread, it came into the hands of philosophers, men trained to define and argue and systematise. They could not leave a simple thing alone. Every saying had to be analysed, every image turned into a proposition, every mystery settled by a formula. Out of this came doctrine, and the doctrine hardened, and before long belief meant assent to a set of carefully worded statements rather than a way of living. People were divided and condemned over points that the first hearers would not even have understood. [example needed]

The second was the work of worldly power. So long as the faith belonged to the poor and the powerless, it kept something of its edge. But the powerful are very good at taking in what threatens them and making it harmless. They did not fight the new religion for ever; they adopted it. They gave it buildings and revenues and a place at court, and in return it learned to be respectable. What had been a movement became an institution, with ranks and offices and interests of its own to protect, and the institution was naturally inclined to support the order that supported it.

Nowhere is the distance travelled plainer than in the papacy at its height. Think of the grandeur of it. [example needed] Then set beside it the figure at the beginning, a humble teacher in Galilee with none of it. It is hard to believe the one grew out of the other. It did, of course, step by step, and each step probably seemed reasonable at the time. But the sum of them is a drift so great that the teacher himself would scarcely recognise what was built in his name.

## Draft C

I have sometimes tried to picture to myself what the earliest Christian communities must have been, before the world laid its heavy hand upon them. The message they received was a very simple one, a message of love, forgiveness and trust, delivered in homely words to homely people; it asked for no learning and no rank, only for a changed heart. And then, it seems to me, two forces set to work upon it, each in its own way, and between them they transformed it almost past recognition.

The first of these forces was the intellect; the philosophers came, with their subtle minds and their love of definition, and they could not leave the simple thing alone; they must analyse it, systematise it, fence it about with propositions, until what had been a way of living became a body of rigid doctrine, to be accepted on pain of exclusion. I do not doubt that many of them were sincere and devout men. But the effect of their labours was like that of a botanist who presses a wild flower between the leaves of a heavy volume; the form is preserved with admirable exactness, and the life is gone.

The second force was worldly power, and it worked more quietly; the great ones of the earth, finding that they could not suppress the faith, did the wiser thing and absorbed it; they took it into their palaces, endowed it, organised it, and tamed it into an institution which could sit comfortably beside the throne. Of course an institution was inevitable, and it has preserved much that would otherwise have perished; yet one cannot contemplate the result without a certain dismay.

Is there any more striking witness to the distance travelled than the grandeur of the papacy? One thinks of the splendid courts, the jewelled vestments, the titles and processions and the whole magnificent apparatus of dominion; and then one thinks of the humble teacher in Galilee, walking the dusty roads among plain people, and speaking to them quietly, so to speak, as one neighbour speaks to another over a garden wall.
