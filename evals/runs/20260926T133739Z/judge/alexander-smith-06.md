# Judge packet alexander-smith-06

## The passage (by the author)

Commend me to Shakspeare's vagabonds, the most delightful in the world! His sweet-blooded and liberal nature blossomed into all fine generosities as naturally as an apple-bough into pink blossoms and odours. Listen to Gonsalvo talking to the shipwrecked Milan nobles camped for the night in Prospero's isle, full of sweet voices, with Ariel shooting through the enchanted air like a falling star;--

"Had I the plantation of this isle, my lord, I' the commonwealth I would by contraries Execute all things; for no kind of traffic Would I admit; no name of magistrate; Letters should not be known; riches, poverty, And use of service none; contract, succession, Bourne, bound of land, tilth, title, vineyard none; No use of metal coin, or wine, or oil; No occupation--all men idle--all! And women too, but innocent and pure; No sovereignty; All things in common nature should produce, Without sweat or endurance; treason, felony, Sword, pike, knife, gun, or need of any engine Would I not have; but nature would bring forth Of its own kind all foison, all abundance, To feed my innocent people. I would with such perfection govern, sir, To excel the golden age."

What think you of a world after that pattern? "As You Like it" is a vagabond play, and, verily, if there waved in any wind that blows a forest peopled like Arden's, with an exiled king drawing the sweetest, humanest lessons from misfortune; a melancholy Jacques, stretched by the river bank, moralising on the bleeding deer; a fair Rosalind, chanting her saucy cuckoo-song; fools like Touchstone--not like those of our acquaintance, my friends; and the whole place, from centre to circumference, filled with mighty oak bolls, all carven with lovers' names,--if such a forest waved in wind, I say, I would, be my worldly prospects what they might, pack up at once, and cast in my lot with that vagabond company. For there I should find more gallant courtesies, finer sentiments, completer innocence and happiness, more wit and wisdom, than I am like to do here even, though I search for them from shepherd's cot to king's palace. Just to think how those people lived! Carelessly as the blossoming trees, happily as the singing birds, time measured only by the patter of the acorn on the fruitful soil! A world without debtor or creditor, passing rich, yet with never a doit in its purse, with no sordid care, no regard for appearances; nothing to occupy the young but love-making, nothing to occupy the old but perusing the "sermons in stones" and the musical wisdom which dwells in "running brooks"! But Arden forest draws its sustenance from a poet's brain: the light that sleeps on its leafy pillows is "the light that never was on sea or shore." We but please and tantalise ourselves with beautiful dreams.

## Draft A

Shakespeare's plays are full of drifters: people who have slipped, by accident or choice, out of the ordinary machinery of getting and spending, and who seem, for a while at least, none the worse for it. They wander in forests, wash up on islands, and talk a great deal about how much better life would be if only the world were arranged differently. They are some of the most charming people he wrote, and it is worth asking what he meant by them.

In The Tempest a courtier, shipwrecked on a strange island, amuses himself by imagining how he would govern it. His ideal commonwealth would have no trade, no money, no magistrates, no letters, no labour. Nature would bring forth everything of its own accord, and the people would be innocent and idle together. It is an absurd scheme, and the play does not pretend otherwise. But the dream is lovely all the same, and he speaks it with such evident pleasure that it is hard not to share it for a moment.

The Arden forest community goes further, because there we are not merely told about the dream but shown it. A little society of exiles lives among the trees, having fled or been driven from a court full of envy and intrigue. They hunt, they sing, they sit about and moralise. They have no money worth speaking of and no work beyond what the day requires. And they seem, by any measure the play offers, wiser and happier than the people they left behind. Hardship, in the forest, seems to lose its sting. Against the real society of the court, with its jealousy and its plotting, the forest looks like the better way to live.

It is very tempting to take these idylls at their word, to believe that Shakespeare is telling us that the carefree life is the true one, and that we have simply lost our way by building towns and counting-houses. I do not think he is. Such idylls are only a poet's fantasy, and he seems to have known it perfectly well. The courtier's commonwealth is a way of passing time on a strange shore, not a plan anyone intends to carry out. The forest life, for all its charm, is a refuge rather than a destination. [example needed] The dream is offered as a dream: a pleasant place to rest the mind before returning to a world where money and work are not optional.

That is not a criticism. We need such places. The drifters give us a holiday from our own responsibilities, and we come back from them a little refreshed. But we should not mistake the holiday for a home.

## Draft B

Shakespeare, who knew every kind of man, knew the drifter well, and loved him. His plays are full of people who have slipped out of the harness of the world--banished men, idle courtiers, fools in motley, lovers who have run away into the woods--and who seem, for the space of a few scenes, to have found there something that the harness never gave them. They are the carefree drifters of literature, and one cannot read of them without a little pang of envy.

In The Tempest an old courtier, cast away upon the enchanted island, falls to dreaming aloud of how he would govern it if it were his. There should be no traffic in his commonwealth, no money, no riches and no poverty; no labour, no magistrates, no letters; nature should bring forth of her own accord all that men required, and the people should live innocent and idle. His companions laugh at him, and not without reason, for the scheme has holes in it through which a coach might be driven. And yet the dream is a pleasant one, and there is hardly a man in the audience who has not, on some wet Monday morning, dreamed it too.

The forest of Arden is the same dream made flesh. There a little community lives under the greenwood tree, reads its lessons in the running brooks and the changing leaves, and appears, one and all, a great deal wiser and happier than the court they have left behind. Nobody in Arden is in a hurry. Nobody is ambitious, or if he is, the forest cures him of it. They idle a little, and talk a great deal, and moralise at leisure upon the follies of the world they have escaped; and the world, seen from under the oaks, looks very foolish indeed. Real society, with its envies and its intrigues, seems a poor thing beside this company of cheerful exiles.

But we must not be deceived. These idylls are the fantasy of a poet, and the poet knew it better than anyone. The island where no man labours is an island in the sea of dreams; Arden has no winter that cannot be sung away, and no rent-day at all. Were we to transport ourselves there in the body, we should find the greenwood damp, the dinner uncertain, and the lessons of the brooks remarkably tedious after the first week. The drifters are delightful because they drift across a stage, and the play ends before the bill comes in. Shakespeare lets us wander with them for an afternoon, and then sends us home--a little refreshed, a little rebuked, and not at all inclined to throw up our situations. That is the proper use of an idyll. It is a holiday for the imagination, and a holiday is only sweet because it ends.

## Draft C

Every age complains that it works too hard, and every age consoles itself with a dream of people who do not work at all. Shakespeare, who knew most things about us, knew this too, and his plays are full of pleasant drifters--men and women who have slipped out of the harness of society and seem none the worse for it. In The Tempest, a courtier, finding himself upon an island, begins idly to legislate for it. Were he king there, he says, he would have no traffic, no riches, no poverty, no labour; money should be unknown, and nobody should toil, since Nature herself would bring forth all things in abundance for the people. His companions mock him, and he takes their mockery with a good grace. Yet one feels that the poet did not wholly mock him. The dream is too sweetly spoken for that.

Go next into the forest of Arden. There a small community has set up house among the trees, and appears, to the spectator's considerable surprise, happier than any assembly in palaces. Nobody there flatters; nobody there schemes; the weather is inclement, but it is honestly inclement. The foresters wander, and sing, and philosophise, and fall in love, and out of love, and in again, with nothing to hinder them but the weather. Compared with ordinary society, this little community appears not merely happier but wiser; it has shaken off the foolishest parts of civilisation and kept, one would say, only the gracefulest. What should they want with offices and lawsuits? Why should they return? And the reader, sitting by his own fire with his own cares about him, half wishes that he might join them to-morrow.

But he knows, and Shakespeare knew, that he cannot. Such idylls are the pure invention of a poet; they exist nowhere but on the stage, and they would not survive a week of real rain. The island has no neighbours and the forest no tax-gatherer; the one is imaginary, the other is rather conveniently free of everything that makes life hard. Care, the old tyrant, has simply been left out of the cast. Just as a painter may leave the smoke out of his picture of a city, and give you roofs and spires shining in a clear air that no city ever breathed, so the dramatist leaves out of his greenwood the want and weariness that would have followed any real community into it. Is he to be blamed for that? Surely not. He does not deceive us. He lets us walk for an hour in a world that is better than our own, and then, very kindly, sends us home. The dream is a fantasy; verily, it is a fantasy we could ill spare!
