# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-02

## The passage (by the author)

Great was the joy in the community when one day Four-Eyed Nick announced that he had struck pay ore and that he was about to celebrate his good fortune by getting married. Every one was intensely interested. The newspapers made an especial feature of the approaching marriage in high life. Nick was dazed by the sudden glare of publicity. Who should be invited? His generous heart rebelled against any discrimination, and he solved his problem by saying, “Come one! Come all!” He engaged every vehicle in the town to be at the disposal of such of his fellow citizens as would honor him with their presence at his nuptials.

It would have delighted the heart of Chaucer to have seen the procession of wedding guests wending their way over the ten miles of abominable mountain road to Nick’s cabin. Not on the road to Canterbury was there more variety or more hearty good fellowship. Nick had invited the town, and the town was bent on showing its appreciation of the compliment. The mayor and members of the city council, the lawyers, editors, doctors, clergymen, gamblers, mining experts, saloon-keepers, and honest miners all joined heartily in doing honor to one whom they, for the moment, agreed to consider their most distinguished fellow citizen.

No one could remain long in assured obscurity. It pleased the community to turn its search-light now upon one member and now upon another, and give him a brief experience of living in the public eye. Greatness of one sort or another was sure to be thrust upon one in the course of the year. The choicest spirits of the town were always collaborating in some work of high-grade fiction, and were on the lookout for interesting material. It would have been churlish for any one when his turn came to have refused to be a notability.

## Draft A

When fortune comes to a mining town it does not come privately. A prospector who had searched the hills for [number needed: how many] years without reward struck at last upon a vein of remarkably rich ore. He then did what any sensible man in his position ought to do, and decided to marry. But he was not content to marry in a corner. He invited the entire population to the wedding, and since the ceremony was to be held at some distance, he paid for the transport of every guest who cared to come.

I should like to have seen that company on the road. The officials of the county sat beside the gamblers whose tables they were supposed to regulate, and the clergy, who perhaps entertained some private misgivings about their neighbours, travelled with the laborers from the mines, who entertained no misgivings about anybody. It was a pilgrimage in the ancient manner. I could not help thinking of Chaucer's pilgrims, that miscellaneous fellowship which shared one dusty road and told its tales without much regard for rank or reputation. The mining camp, I suspect, could have furnished the poet with most of his characters and one or two he had overlooked.

"But what," you ask, "was the purpose of it all?" The purpose, I think, was that everybody should have a turn. A mining town is a democracy of celebrity. It does not keep one great man on a pedestal for a generation; it lifts each of its residents into prominence for a day or a week, admires him thoroughly, and then sets him down again to make room for the next candidate. To-day it was the prospector and his bride. To-morrow it would be somebody else, and nobody considered the arrangement unjust.

## Draft B

In a mining town the fortunes of one man are the property of all. When a prospector who had been poking about the hills with nothing but a mule and a hopeful disposition struck a vein of rich ore, the news did not belong to him alone. It belonged to the camp, and the camp took possession of it at once.

What he did next showed that he understood the obligations of his new position. He decided to marry, and, not content with a private ceremony, he invited everybody. More than that, he paid for the transport of all who would come. It was a stroke of genius. A man may be forgiven his wealth if he will only share the fun of it.

The company that set out for the wedding was such as could be gathered nowhere but on the frontier. There were officials, who for the day forgot to be official; gamblers, who for once had nothing at stake; clergy, who found themselves in unaccustomed company and bore it cheerfully; and laborers, who were the most at ease of all. They travelled together, sharing the road and the jokes of the road. I could not help thinking of Chaucer's pilgrims, who set out for Canterbury with no more in common than the way they were going. Here also the Knight and the Pardoner rode side by side, and nobody thought it strange.

There is a kind of democracy in such a place that is not written in any constitution. It is the democracy of opportunity for fame. In older communities celebrity is a scarce article and is reserved for a few. In the mining camp it is distributed in turn. To-day it is the lucky prospector; to-morrow it will be the man who lost his claim in a spectacular manner; the day after, it may be the cook. The town takes turns at making each resident famous, and nobody is kept waiting very long. It is a generous arrangement, and I have sometimes wished that older societies would adopt it.

## Draft C

In a mining town, luck is a public affair. When a prospector who has scratched at the hills for years finally breaks into rich ore, the news runs down the gulch faster than he can, and by the time he reaches the saloon the whole camp feels it has a share in his good fortune. So it was when our lucky man struck it and, in the same breath, announced that he meant to marry.

He was not the sort to marry quietly. He sent word that everyone was invited, every soul in the district, and that he would pay for all the transport himself. Wagons, stages, horses and whatever else could be pressed into service were hired on his account, and the town emptied itself onto the road toward the wedding.

It was a procession worth seeing. Officials rode beside gamblers, and the clergy found themselves sharing a seat with laborers who had not seen the inside of a church in years. Nobody minded. A reader of Chaucer would have recognized the company at once: here were his pilgrims again, the respectable and the disreputable jolted along together, each with a tale to tell and each rather enjoying the others. The talk on that road would have filled a book, and very little of it would have passed the censor.

What struck me most was the good nature of the whole affair. A mining town has a curious habit of taking turns with its citizens. One week it is the man who found the lead; the next it is the fellow who lost his claim at cards, or the preacher who gave a rousing sermon, or the woman who kept the boarding house through the hard winter. Each is famous for a little while, talked about in every cabin, and then gently set down so that someone else may have the platform. On this occasion it was the prospector's turn, and the town gave it to him with both hands.
