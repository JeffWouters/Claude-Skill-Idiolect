# Judge packet alexander-smith-01

## The passage (by the author)

In summer I spend a good deal of time floating about the lake. The landing-place to which my boat is tethered is ruinous, like the chapel and palace, and my embarkation causes quite a stir in the sleepy little village. Small boys leave their games and mud-pies, and gather round in silence; they have seen me get off a hundred times, but their interest in the matter seems always new. Not unfrequently an idle cobbler, in red night-cap and leathern apron, leans on a broken stile, and honours my proceedings with his attention. I shoot off, and the human knot dissolves. The lake contains three islands, each with a solitary tree, and on these islands the swans breed. I feed the birds daily with bits of bread. See, one comes gliding towards me, with superbly arched neck, to receive its customary alms! How wildly beautiful its motions! How haughtily it begs! The green pasture lands run down to the edge of the water, and into it in the afternoons the red kine wade and stand knee-deep in their shadows, surrounded by troops of flies. Patiently the honest creatures abide the attacks of their tormentors. Now one swishes itself with its tail,--now its neighbour flaps a huge ear. I draw my oars alongside, and let my boat float at its own will. The soft blue heavenly abysses, the wandering streams of vapour, the long beaches of rippled clouds, are glassed and repeated in the lake. Dreamthorp is silent as a picture, the voices of the children are mute; and the smoke from the houses, the blue pillars all sloping in one angle, float upward as if in sleep. Grave and stern the old castle rises from its emerald banks, which long ago came down to the lake in terrace on terrace, gay with fruits and flowers, and with stone nymph and satyrs hid in every nook. Silent and empty enough to-day! A flock of daws suddenly bursts out from a turret, and round and round they wheel, as if in panic. Has some great scandal exploded? Has a conspiracy been discovered? Has a revolution broken out? The excitement has subsided, and one of them, perched on the old banner-staff, chatters confidentially to himself as he, sideways, eyes the world beneath him. Floating about thus, time passes swiftly, for, before I know where I am, the kine have withdrawn from the lake to couch on the herbage, while one on a little height is lowing for the milkmaid and her pails. Along the road I see the labourers coming home for supper, while the sun setting behind me makes the village windows blaze; and so I take out my oars, and pull leisurely through waters faintly flushed with evening colours.

## Draft A

The afternoon was too warm for anything useful, so I untied the rowing boat from its post and pushed off onto the lake. I did not get away unobserved. A handful of the village children had gathered on the bank, as they always seem to when anything is happening, and they watched me fumble with the oars with the frank interest of people who expect a disaster and would not mind one. Behind them an older onlooker leaned on the fence, saying nothing, which was worse. I managed a few straight strokes, the children lost interest, and the bank slid away behind me.

Once out past the reeds I stopped rowing altogether. There is no point in hurrying on a lake; it goes nowhere, and neither need you. I shipped the oars, lay back against the stern, and let the boat turn slowly on whatever faint current the water had to offer.

The swans came first. They had been watching from the far side and now made their way over in that unhurried, faintly offended manner swans have, as though the lake were theirs and I had come without an invitation. Which, to be fair, I had. They circled once, decided I had no bread, and drifted off again, keeping one eye on me as they went.

On the meadow that runs down to the water the cattle stood knee-deep in the shallows, too hot to graze and too comfortable to move. Now and then one of them lifted its head, looked across the lake at nothing in particular, and lowered it again. I found I envied them. They had solved the problem of a summer afternoon long before I had.

Above it all stood the old castle, or what remains of it. From the water the ruin looks larger and more complete than it does from the road, its broken walls catching the light, its empty windows framing patches of sky. The jackdaws own it now. They wheeled and quarrelled around the top of the tower the whole afternoon, a noisy, restless crowd that never seemed to settle on anything, and their chatter carried across the water long after the rest of the world had gone quiet.

I meant to row back after an hour. I did not. The hours went the way they go on water, without announcing themselves. I watched the shadows of the castle walls stretch out across the meadow, watched the cattle finally wander back up the field, watched the light go from white to gold to something softer than either. I thought about a great many things and settled none of them, which seemed exactly right.

It was the cool that told me the day was over. A small wind came off the water, the swans had gone in among the reeds, and the jackdaws had at last fallen silent on their tower. Dusk was coming up from the far bank. I took up the oars again, stiff and reluctant, and rowed slowly back to shore, where the children, and the onlooker by the fence, had long since gone home.

## Draft B

Men say that idleness is the root of all evil; and I suppose it is true, for the saying is very old, and old sayings have a way of being right in the end. Yet there are idlenesses and idlenesses. On a summer afternoon, with the lake lying below the village as still as a sheet of glass, I untie the boat and step down into it, and I cannot persuade myself that I am doing anything wicked. The children of the place come running to the edge of the water to see me go; they stand in a row upon the bank, silent, watchful, grave as a bench of magistrates; and an onlooker, leaning on the rail above them, takes his hands from his pockets, as if the setting out of a rowing boat were an event worth an evening's talk. Perhaps, in so quiet a place, it is. I pull a few leisurely strokes, and then I let the oars trail. What should I hurry for, and whither should I go? The lake has no business with me, nor I with it; and so, with a conscience entirely undisturbed, I drift.

The swans come sailing out from the reeds to inspect me, proud as duchesses, and, finding that I have brought them nothing, turn their backs with a magnificent contempt--it is the foolishest thing in the world to be despised by a swan, and yet one feels it. On the far shore the cattle stand knee-deep in the shallows, switching at the flies, and regard me with that large, mild, unenterprising stupidity which is, I think, the peculiar wisdom of cattle. Above them, on its green rise, stands the old castle; and round its broken towers the jackdaws wheel and clamour all the afternoon, as though they were the last of the garrison, and had not yet heard that the siege was over. Time, which pulled down the walls, has spared the jackdaws; the one he could conquer, the other he did not think worth the trouble. I lie back in the boat and contemplate the clouds--those idlest and gracefulest of travellers. The hours slip from me as the water slips from the blade of an oar, quietly, without a sound, and leaving no mark behind. I do not count them; nay, I do not know that they are going, until the light changes on the castle walls, and the swans have gone in among the reeds, and the cattle have waded out into the meadow, and the whole lake has turned the colour of a dove's breast. Then Dusk comes over the hill, lays a cool hand upon my shoulder, and bids me home. I take up the oars reluctantly. The children are gone; the onlooker is gone; the bank is empty. I row back through the grey water, make fast the boat, and walk up to the village with nothing done. To-morrow, if the weather hold, I shall do nothing again!

## Draft C

On the afternoon of which I speak, the heat lay upon the village like a hand, and I went down to the lake, and loosed the boat, and took to the water. I did not go unobserved. A knot of village children had gathered on the bank, as children will gather wherever anything is being done, and watched me with that grave and unwinking curiosity which is the only perfectly honest criticism in the world. They would have been delighted had I overturned; they were a little disappointed, I think, that I did not. Behind them, leaning on the rail, stood an onlooker of riper years, who regarded the whole proceeding with the air of a man who had seen boats before, and had formed no high opinion of them. I pushed off under the fire of their eyes, and in a few strokes was free of them all.

It is a pleasant thing to row, but it is pleasanter to cease rowing. When I had reached the middle of the lake I shipped the oars, and let the boat go where it would--and it would go nowhere in particular, which suited me exactly. The swans came sailing out from the reeds to inspect me, proud as Spanish grandees, and as little disposed to be familiar; finding I had nothing for them, they turned their white backs upon me and went off in a kind of stately disgust. On the farther shore the cattle had come down to drink, and stood knee-deep in the shallows, motionless, as if they had been painted there by some patient Dutchman and left to dry. Above, round the broken towers of the old castle, the jackdaws wheeled and quarrelled and settled and rose again, keeping up that endless clamour which seems to be the only speech a ruin has left. The castle itself looked down upon the water as an old man looks upon a child's game--with tolerance, and with a certain weariness, having seen the game played so often.

What I thought about in those hours I cannot now tell; I am not sure that I thought at all. There are seasons when the mind, like the boat, is content to drift, and is the better for it. Thought is a tax we pay upon living; and now and again, on a summer afternoon, one may be permitted to fall into arrears. The shadows of the trees crept out across the water; the swans went home; the cattle climbed the bank one by one and were gone. The light yellowed, and reddened, and then began to fail.

It was the dusk that recalled me. A chill came off the water, the castle darkened into a mere shape against the sky, and the jackdaws fell silent at last. I took up the oars, and pulled slowly in. The children had long since departed, and the onlooker too; there was no one to see me land. I had spent an afternoon and had nothing to show for it--and I have seldom spent one better.
