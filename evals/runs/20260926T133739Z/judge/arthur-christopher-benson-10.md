# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-10

## The passage (by the author)

Well, for the purposes of my argument, I will call my friend Perry, though it is not his name; and having finished my introduction I will go on to my main story.

I took in to dinner the other night a beautiful and accomplished lady, with whom it is always a pleasure to talk. The conversation turned upon Mr. Perry. She said with a graceful air of judgment that she had but one fault to find with him, and that was that he hated women. I hazarded a belief that he was shy, to which she replied with a dignified assurance that he was not shy; he was lazy.

Prudence and discretion forbade me to appeal against this decision; but I endeavoured to arrive at the principles that supported such a verdict. I gathered that Egeria considered that every one owed a certain duty to society; that people had no business to pick and choose, to cultivate the society of those who happened to please and interest them, and to eschew the society of those who bored and wearied them; that such a course was not fair to the uninteresting people, and so forth. But the point was that there was a duty involved, and that some sacrifice was required of virtuous people in the matter.

Egeria herself is certainly blameless in the matter: she diffuses sweetness and light in many tedious assemblies; she is true to her principles; but for all that I cannot agree with her on this point.

In the first place I cannot agree that sociability is a duty at all, and to conceive of it as such seems to me to misunderstand the whole situation. I think that a man loses a great deal by being unsociable, and that for his own happiness he had better make an effort to see something of his fellows. All kinds of grumpinesses and morbidities arise from solitude; and a shy man ought to take occasional dips into society from a medicinal point of view, as a man should take a cold bath; even if he confers no pleasure on others by so doing, the mere sense, to a timid man, of having steered a moderately straight course through a social entertainment is in itself enlivening and invigorating, and gives the pleasing feeling of having escaped from a great peril. But the accusation of unsociability does not apply to Perry, whose doors are open day and night, and whose welcome is always perfectly sincere. Moreover, the frame of mind in which a man goes to a party, determined to confer pleasure and exercise influence, is a dangerously self-satisfied one. Society is, after all, a recreation and a delight, and ought to be sought for with pleasurable motives, not with a consciousness of rectitude and justice.

## Draft A

I was dining not long ago in a house where the talk turned, as talk will, upon an absent friend; and the lady who sat next to me took occasion to find fault with him. He was, it seemed, a man who avoided the society of women; he did not go to parties, he did not pay calls, he was not to be seen at the places where people meet. She spoke of it quite seriously, as a moral failing. He was lazy, she said; he was shirking; every one owed it to society to make an effort, and a man who would not take his share of the trouble was simply selfish. I did not argue the point at the time, for it was not a moment for argument; but I have thought about it since, and I find that I disagree with her almost entirely.

I do not doubt for a moment that it is good for a man to mix with his fellows. It keeps him from growing narrow and crusty; it rubs off his angles; it shows him that there are other ways of looking at things than his own, and other lives than the one he happens to lead. A man who shuts himself up altogether is apt to grow strange, and to lose his sense of proportion. But all this is an argument for his own sake, not for the sake of society; it is a matter of his own health and happiness, as exercise is, or fresh air. It is prudent for him to go out, perhaps; but I cannot see that it is his duty. Nobody is injured by his staying at home, except possibly himself; and that is his own affair.

And indeed I think that the idea of going into society as a duty, as a thing that one owes, is a rather unlovely one. There is something about the person who goes out to do good, who enters a drawing-room with the sense that he is conferring a benefit, which is not at all agreeable. One feels the complacency of it; the little glow of virtue, the consciousness of having made an effort for the sake of others. I would a great deal rather be visited by some one who came because he liked it than by some one who came because he thought he ought.

For the truth is that company, if it is to be worth anything, must be enjoyed. It is a pleasure, or it is nothing. The best talk, the best friendship, the best hours spent with others, come not from a sense of obligation but from a real desire for one another's society; and if a man does not feel that desire, or feels it only for a very few, it is no use to lecture him. He had better be left to find his own way.

## Draft B

I was at a dinner not long ago when the talk turned, as it sometimes will, to an absent friend. My neighbour at the table had a complaint against him. This friend, it seemed, avoided the company of women; he did not go to the parties and gatherings where he might meet them, and he made no particular effort to be agreeable when he did. My neighbour called this laziness, pure and simple. Everyone, he said, owes society a certain amount of effort. We cannot simply please ourselves. We have a duty to go out, to mix, to make ourselves pleasant, and a man who shirks it is failing in something he owes to others.

I did not agree, though I am not sure I put my case very well at the time. I have thought about it since, and I think I can say more clearly now where the difference between us lies.

I do believe that mixing with other people is a good thing. I believe it is good for most of us to get out of our own heads, to meet people unlike ourselves, to be surprised and challenged and amused. Someone who never does so is likely to grow narrow and a little stale. But the reason it is good is that it is good for us. It belongs with fresh air and exercise and a varied diet, things we do because they keep us well, not things we owe to anyone else. If my friend chooses to go without them, he may be the poorer for it, but he has not wronged anybody. It is his own wellbeing he is neglecting, and that is his affair.

I also distrust the idea of going into company as a duty for another reason. There is a kind of person who goes out in order to do good: to bring the shy one out, to cheer the dull evening, to lend his presence to the occasion. I have met a few of them, and they are not, on the whole, restful to be with. There is something smug about it. They are conscious all the time of their own virtue in being there, and the rest of us are conscious of it too. Company offered in that spirit is a kind of charity, and nobody much enjoys being the object of charity at a dinner party.

The best company, it seems to me, comes from people who are there because they want to be. They enjoy the talk, they enjoy the people, and their enjoyment spreads. That cannot be produced by a sense of obligation. It can only be encouraged by letting people find the company that suits them and go to it gladly. If my friend has not yet found his, I would rather he were left in peace to look than lectured for not trying harder.

## Draft C

I was dining with a companion who fell, in the course of the evening, to discussing a certain friend, and discussing him with some severity. The charge was that he avoided the society of women; that he would not take the trouble to go where they were, or to make himself agreeable when he found himself among them; and the verdict was that this was mere laziness, a kind of moral slackness, since every one, as my companion put it, owes society a certain effort, and has no right to withhold it for the sake of his own comfort. It was said with conviction, and I could see that it was meant as a serious judgement upon character.

I did not agree with it then, and I do not agree with it now. Is sociability, then, a duty at all? I am inclined myself to believe that it is not, though I know that the opposite view is perhaps the more respectable. Of course it is good for a man to mix with his fellows, and good for him to mix with women as well as men; it widens his sympathies, it rubs off his angles, it saves him from the narrowness and oddity which grow so easily upon the solitary. But these are benefits to himself; they are reasons why he may be wise to seek company, not reasons why he is wicked to avoid it; and if he chooses to forgo them, the loss, so far as I can see, is chiefly his own.

The worst of it is that the doctrine of social duty produces a very unlovely frame of mind. There is a type of person who goes out into company, so to speak, with a moral purpose, in order to do good, to cheer the dull, to draw out the shy, to discharge an obligation to the community; and I confess that I find something rather smug in the attitude. One feels oneself, perhaps, being improved, or patronised, or made the object of a benevolent exercise. The talk loses its freedom; the laughter becomes a little conscious; and the whole occasion takes on the air of a charitable bazaar, where everybody is virtuous and nobody is amused.

Surely the true reason for seeking company is simply that one enjoys it. We go to our friends as we go to a fire on a cold evening, because it warms us, not because we owe the fire a visit. If this friend does not find that warmth in the society of women, I think one may regret it for his sake, and hope that he will find it in time; but it is no part of anybody else to scold him into the drawing-room. He would sit there, I fear, like a cat carried into a strange kitchen, stiff, watchful, and longing only for the door.
