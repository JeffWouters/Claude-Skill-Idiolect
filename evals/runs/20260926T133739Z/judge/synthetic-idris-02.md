## The passage (by the author)

There is a considerable literature on techniques that promise to improve the situation — relative sizing, probabilistic forecasting, the breaking down of work into ever smaller pieces — and I do not wish to dismiss any of them, since each captures something true about the problem. Yet I am struck by how often these techniques are adopted as a kind of procedural reassurance rather than as a genuine change in how uncertainty is discussed. A team may replace its hours with points, or its points with ranges, while the conversation surrounding the estimate remains exactly as it was: somebody asks when the work will be finished, somebody else provides an answer they suspect is optimistic, and everyone proceeds as though the answer were reliable. The technique changes; the institutional relationship to uncertainty does not. It may be that no method of estimation can compensate for an organisation that is unwilling to hear the word "unknown".

What would it look like to take uncertainty seriously? You might begin by treating every estimate as a statement about the present state of our knowledge rather than about the future, and by expecting it to change as that knowledge changes — which is to say, by treating revision as a sign of learning rather than as a failure of discipline. You might also distinguish, more carefully than we usually do, between the parts of a project that resemble work we have done before and the parts that are genuinely novel, since the former can often be estimated with reasonable confidence while the latter resist prediction almost entirely. None of this is especially original; it is, arguably, what thoughtful engineers have been saying for a very long time. The obstacle has never been a shortage of ideas but a reluctance, on all sides, to relinquish the comfort that a precise-sounding figure provides.

## Draft A

Every few years a new estimation method arrives with the promise that this time planning will work. Hours give way to story points, story points give way to t-shirt sizes, and those in turn give way to something else. The teams change their units. The planning rarely gets better.

The reason, I think, is that the unit was never the problem. Teams swap one scale for another but keep pretending the resulting numbers are dependable. A point is treated with the same false precision an hour used to be. The spreadsheet still adds them up, the roadmap still hangs off the total, and everyone still acts surprised when the total turns out to be wrong.

An estimate is only ever a statement of what we know right now. That is all it can be. As work proceeds we learn things, and the estimate should move with that learning. Revising an estimate is not a failure of planning; it is planning doing its job. The trouble starts when a revision is treated as an admission of error rather than as new information, because then people stop revising and start defending.

It also helps to separate two kinds of work that usually get estimated the same way. Familiar work, the kind a team has done many times before, can be estimated reasonably well, whatever unit you use. Truly new work cannot, and no method changes that. Lumping the two together lets the confidence we rightly have in the first leak into the second, where it does not belong.

So when a new method disappoints, it is worth looking past the method. The real barrier is not the scale on the estimate but our attachment to exact figures. As long as we want a number we can hold people to, any method will end up producing one, and it will be wrong in the same old ways. Let go of that attachment and almost any method will do.

## Draft B

Every few years a team discovers a new way of estimating its work and adopts it with a certain hopefulness. One unit of measure gives way to another, and that in turn to some further refinement, and each change is accompanied by the expectation that planning will at last become reliable. It rarely does. The reason, I think, is that the units change while the underlying pretence remains exactly where it was: the belief, shared quietly by everyone involved, that the numbers can be depended upon in a way that they plainly cannot.

What an estimate actually represents is a statement of what we know at a particular moment, and what we know at the beginning of a piece of work is usually rather little. It would seem to follow that estimates ought to be revised freely as understanding improves, and that such revision should be treated not as a failure of the original figure but as its natural continuation. In practice the opposite tends to happen. The first number acquires an authority it never earned, and every subsequent adjustment is experienced as a small embarrassment to be explained away.

It may help to distinguish more deliberately between two kinds of work. Some tasks resemble things the team has done many times before, and for these a reasonably confident estimate is possible. Others are genuinely new, involving problems no one on the team has yet solved, and for these any figure is closer to a guess dressed in the clothing of a forecast. Treating both kinds identically, with the same precision and the same expectations, is a recipe for disappointment.

None of this requires a new method. What it requires is something harder: a willingness to let go of our attachment to exact figures, and to accept that a range honestly held is worth more than a number confidently wrong. That attachment, rather than any shortcoming of technique, is what stands in the way.

## Draft C

Why do newer estimation methods so rarely improve the way a team plans? It may be that the method was never the problem, and that each new scheme simply gives an old habit a fresh vocabulary. I suspect the habit is the thing worth examining.

Consider what usually happens when a team abandons hours for points, or points for sizes, or sizes for some other unit that promises to be more honest. The unit changes, the ceremony around it changes a little, and yet the figures produced are still treated, within a few weeks, as though they were dependable measurements rather than informed guesses. The team has swapped its units; it has kept its pretence; it has, arguably, gained nothing except a new set of words in which to be disappointed.

It seems to me that an estimate should be understood as a statement of what we currently know, no more and no less, and that it ought to be revised freely as that knowledge changes, without anyone treating the revision as a small admission of failure. There is also a useful distinction to be drawn between work that is familiar, where past experience genuinely offers some guidance, and work that is truly new, where any figure is in some sense a hope wearing the clothes of a calculation. Treating the two alike tends to make the first look less reliable than it is and the second considerably more reliable than it could ever be.

The real barrier, in the end, is not technical. It is an attachment to exact figures, and to the comfort they offer those who must plan around them. Perhaps what teams need is not a better unit but a greater willingness to say how little they yet know.
