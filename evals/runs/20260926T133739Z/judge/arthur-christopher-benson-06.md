# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-06

## The passage (by the author)

The theory of religion would then be that the crude idea of propitiatory and conciliatory sacrifice would fall to the ground; that to use the inspired words of the old Roman poet--

"Aptissima quaeque dabunt Di. Carior est illis homo quam sibi;"

and that the only sacrifices required of man would be, on the one hand, the sacrifice of selfish desires, evil tendencies, sinful appetites; and, on the other hand, the voluntary abnegation of comfortable and desirable things, in the presence of a noble aim, a great idea, a generous purpose.

Religion would then become a purely subjective thing; an intense desire to put the human will in harmony with the Divine will, a hopeful, generous, and trustful attitude of soul, a determination to receive suffering and pain as a gift from the Father, as bravely and sincerely as the gifts of happiness and joy, with a fervent faith that God did indeed, by implanting in men so ardent a longing for strength and joy, and so deeply rooted a terror of pain and weakness, imply that He intended joy, of a purified and elevated kind, to be the ultimate inheritance of His creatures; and the sacrifice of man would then be the willing resignation of everything which could in any degree thwart the ultimate purpose of God.

That I believe from the depths of my heart to be the meaning of the Christian revelation; and I should look upon the thought of objective sacrifice as being an unworthy survival from a time when men had little true knowledge of the Fatherly Heart of God.

And thus, to my mind, the only possible theory of worship is that it is a deliberate act, an opening of the door that leads to the Heavenly presence. Any influence is religious which fills the mind with gratitude and peace, which makes a man humble and patient and wise, which teaches him that the only happiness possible is to attune and harmonize his mind with the gracious purpose of God.

## Draft A

I have never been able to believe in a God who needs to be appeased. The old idea of sacrifice, of offering something up so that an angry power will be soothed and turn its anger elsewhere, seems to me to have very little to do with what faith really is. It treats God as a sort of powerful and touchy neighbour who must be kept sweet, and it treats religion as a bargain. I cannot find either of those things at the heart of the matter.

Yet I do not think sacrifice should be thrown out altogether. It only needs to be understood rightly. True sacrifice, as I see it, is not the giving of gifts to God but the giving up of ourselves, or at least of the parts of ourselves that stand in the way. It means letting go of selfishness, of ease, of the comforts we cling to, for the sake of something worth more. That is a real cost, and often a hard one, but it is paid for a worthy end, not to buy off displeasure.

Faith, then, becomes something inward. It is not a set of transactions but a slow aligning of one's own will with God's. That is easy to say and difficult to do, because our wills are stubborn and we like our own way. It also means accepting pain when it comes, not with resentment and not with a sense that we are being punished, but trustingly, in the belief that it is not meaningless and that we are not abandoned in it. I do not pretend this is simple. But it seems to me the only kind of faith that could hold up in a real life.

And if that is faith, then worship need not be confined to any particular form. Anything that brings us to thankfulness, to calm and to humility is worship in the truest sense. It might be a service in a church, but it need not be. [example needed] What matters is the state it leaves us in, not the setting in which it happens.

## Draft B

I find it more and more difficult, the longer I consider the matter, to believe in a God who needs to be appeased, who must be bought off, so to speak, with offerings and observances, as a surly neighbour is quieted with a present at the new year. The whole idea of propitiation seems to me to belong to an older and darker world, where the divine was a thing to be feared and managed rather than trusted; and I am inclined myself to believe that it lingers among us chiefly because we have inherited the words without examining what they mean.

What, then, is sacrifice, if it is not an offering laid upon an altar? I think it is something at once simpler and harder, the giving up of our own selfishness, our comforts and our small indulgences, for the sake of ends which are worth the cost. It is not a transaction with heaven; it is a discipline of the heart. The mother who spends herself for her children, the worker who sets aside his ease for a cause he believes in, the friend who surrenders a pleasure that another may be spared a pain, these, perhaps, make the only offerings which have any real value.

And faith, as I conceive it, is not the acceptance of a set of propositions, but an inward aligning of one's own will with the will of God; a slow, patient, often painful effort to desire what He desires, and to accept what He sends, even when it is sorrow, with a kind of trustful quietness. One does not understand the pain; one does not pretend to; but one believes that it is not wasted, and one goes on.

Of course this leaves little room for ceremony as such. But I would not say that worship is abolished; I would rather say that it is enlarged. Whatever makes us thankful, whatever calms and steadies us, whatever humbles us and makes us conscious of our smallness and our dependence, is worship, whether it comes to us in a cathedral or on a lonely hillside at evening, when the light fades slowly from the fields and the first star appears.

## Draft C

I have never been able to believe that God desires to be appeased by offerings. The old notion that He must be placated, that His anger must be turned aside by some gift or some suffering laid upon an altar, seems to me to belong to a very dark and fearful stage of human thought; and I do not think that we mend it much by refining the offerings, and putting forms and ceremonies where once there were bullocks and incense. God is not, I am sure, a being who is pleased by our going without things merely for the sake of going without them.

And yet I do not wish to banish the idea of sacrifice altogether; for there is a true sacrifice, and it is a very noble thing. It is simply the giving up of our own selfishness, our ease and our comforts, for the sake of some end that is worth the cost; the laying aside of what we should like for the sake of what is good. That is a sacrifice which has a meaning, because something real is gained by it; and it is the only kind, I think, that can be acceptable.

Faith, as I understand it, is not the holding of certain opinions, but an inward thing; a quiet and patient bringing of one's own will into line with the will of God. It means accepting what comes, even when what comes is pain, not with a sullen submission, but with a kind of trust; believing that there is a purpose in it, though one cannot see the purpose, and that one is not forgotten.

And worship, if this be so, is a far wider thing than we commonly suppose. Whatever makes us thankful, whatever calms and quiets the restless heart, whatever makes us humble and simple and content to be small, is worship; it may come in a church, but it may come just as truly in a garden, or over a book, or in the company of a friend.
