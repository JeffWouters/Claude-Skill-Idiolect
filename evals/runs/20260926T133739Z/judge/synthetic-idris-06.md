# Judge packet synthetic-idris-06

## The passage (by the author)

What is a meeting, really, once it has been placed on the calendar and allowed to recur indefinitely? It may be that the recurring meeting is the closest thing most organisations have to a ritual, in the anthropological sense of the word — a gathering whose original purpose has quietly dissolved while its form persists, maintained by a vague institutional reluctance to be the person who suggests cancelling it. We arrive, we exchange the customary pleasantries, and we wait for someone to articulate why we are there; perhaps someone does, and perhaps the explanation is convincing, but more often the conversation drifts toward whatever happens to be most visible that week. I suspect that very few of us would defend the meeting if asked to justify it from first principles, and yet we continue to attend with considerable loyalty. There is something in this that deserves more attention than the usual complaints about wasted time, because the persistence of the ritual tells us something about what teams actually need from one another.

Consider the way a meeting begins its life. Usually it is created in response to a genuine difficulty — a project that has lost its sense of direction, a dependency between teams that nobody quite owns, a decision that keeps being deferred — and in those first weeks it is arguably the most valuable hour anyone spends. The people in the room have a shared problem; they bring information that cannot easily be written down; they leave with a clearer sense of who is doing what. The difficulty is that the problem eventually resolves, or changes shape, while the invitation remains exactly as it was, reproducing itself week after week with the indifference of any automated process. Does anyone notice the moment at which the meeting stops serving its purpose? In my experience the transition is almost always invisible, since the attendees are still talking, the notes are still being taken, and the calendar still insists that this is where we ought to be.

## Draft A

Look at any calendar and you will find them: repeating meetings, set up long ago, still showing up every week. It can help to see them less as meetings and more as rituals.

Rituals have a particular quality. Their form outlasts the reason they were created. The same slot, the same invite list, the same order of speakers, long after anyone can say exactly why. And yet attendance stays loyal. People keep turning up, partly out of habit, partly because skipping a ritual feels like a small breach of something, even when nobody could say what.

It is worth remembering how these meetings usually begin. Almost always, they start by solving a real, shared problem. A project needs coordination, two teams keep stepping on each other, a decision keeps getting delayed because the right people are never in the same room. Someone sets up a recurring slot to fix that, and for a while it works. The meeting earns its place because everyone in it can see what it is for.

Then the problem changes, or gets solved, or moves elsewhere. The meeting does not. It keeps its slot and its invite list, and it fills its time with whatever is at hand. Updates that could be read. Discussions that belong to smaller groups. Agenda items that exist because an agenda is expected.

What makes this hard is that nobody notices when a meeting stops being useful. There is no single moment when it tips over. It declines slowly, one slightly less valuable session at a time, and each individual session seems fine enough. By the time someone does notice, the meeting has become part of how the team understands itself, and questioning it feels like questioning the team.

That is exactly why it is worth looking at repeating meetings with some deliberate distance. Ask what problem this ritual first solved, and whether that problem is still here. If it is, keep it. If it is not, the loyalty people show it deserves a better object.

## Draft B

It is worth looking at the recurring entries in a team's calendar with something of an anthropologist's eye. Seen from that angle, many of them are less like meetings than like rituals: gatherings that take place at a fixed time, in a fixed form, attended faithfully by people who could not always say with any precision what the gathering is for. The form persists, and so does the loyalty of those who attend, long after the reason for the whole arrangement has faded from memory.

This is not because such meetings were foolish to begin with. On the contrary, most of them began as sensible responses to a genuine difficulty. Something kept going wrong that no one could fix alone, and a regular gathering was the obvious remedy [example needed]. In each case the meeting solved a real problem, and it solved a problem that was shared, which is precisely why everyone was willing to come.

What tends to happen next is rather quieter. The problem is solved, or it changes shape, or the people who felt it most keenly move on to other work. The meeting, however, remains in the calendar, because calendars are good at remembering and poor at forgetting. It continues to recur, and people continue to attend, partly out of habit and partly out of a vague sense that absence might be noticed and misread.

I suspect the most striking thing about this process is that no one really sees it happen. There is no moment at which a meeting announces that it has stopped being useful; its usefulness simply drains away, gradually and without ceremony, while its form stays intact. Perhaps the most we can do is to keep asking, now and again, what problem a given ritual was meant to solve — and whether that problem is still with us.

## Draft C

There is a familiar kind of calendar entry that repeats every week at the same hour, carries a title nobody quite remembers choosing, and draws the same faces into the same room long after anyone can say precisely why. It may be that such meetings are best understood not as meetings at all but as rituals, practices whose form has outlived the reason they were created. I suspect most organisations have more of them than they would care to count.

Consider how they usually begin, because almost always there was a real problem, shared by several people, that could not be solved without regular conversation; a system that kept failing, a handover that kept going wrong, a group of teams whose work kept colliding. The meeting was a sensible response, and for a time it did exactly what it was meant to do, which is arguably why it earned its place in the calendar in the first place.

What happens next is quieter and harder to see, since the problem is solved, or fades, or moves elsewhere, and yet the meeting continues, because nothing in its structure asks whether it should. The agenda becomes a matter of habit; the updates become a matter of form; the attendance, remarkably, remains loyal, since to stop attending would feel like a small act of disloyalty to colleagues who are still turning up. Who among us has not sat through such a meeting and wondered, briefly, whether anyone else was wondering the same thing?

It seems to me that the difficulty is not that these meetings exist but that nobody notices when they stop being useful. There is no moment of failure to mark the change, only a gradual thinning of purpose that is, in some sense, invisible from the inside. Perhaps the most useful thing a team can do with its rituals is not to abolish them but to ask, from time to time, what they were ever for.
