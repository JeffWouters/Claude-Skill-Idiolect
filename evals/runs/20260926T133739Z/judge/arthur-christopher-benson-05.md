# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-05

## The passage (by the author)

We have a perfect right--nay, we do well--to condemn in others faults which we frankly condemn in ourselves. It does not help on the world if we go about everywhere slobbering with forgiveness and affection; it is the most mawkish sentimentality to love people in such a way that we condone grave faults in them; and to condone a fault because a man is great, when we condemn it if he is not great, is only a species of snobbishness. It is right to compassionate sinners, to find excuse for the faults of every one but ourselves; but we ought not to love so foolishly and irrationally, that we cannot even bring ourselves to wish our hero's faults away.

I confess to feeling the most minute and detailed interest in the smallest matters connected with other people's lives and idiosyncrasies. I cannot bear biographies of the dignified order, which do not condescend to give what are called personal details, but confine themselves to matters of undoubted importance. When I have finished reading such books I feel as if I had been reading The Statesman's Year-book, or The Annual Register. I have no mental picture of the hero; he is merely like one of those bronze statues, in frockcoat and trousers, that decorate our London squares.

I was reading, the other day, an ecclesiastical biography. The subject of it, a high dignitary of the Church, had attended the funeral of one of his episcopal colleagues, with whom he had had several technical controversies. On the evening of the day he wrote a very tender and beautiful account of the funeral in his diary, which is quoted at length: "How little," he wrote, "the sense of difference, and how strong my feeling of his power and solid sense; how little I care that he was wrong about the Discipline Bill, how much that he was so happy with us in the summer; how much that he was, as all the family told me, so 'devoted' to my Nellie!"

## Draft A

I have often noticed in myself, and in others, a disposition to judge the faults of eminent people very differently from the faults of obscure ones. If a plain man is mean or unkind or false, we call him so, and think no more about it; but if a great man is guilty of the same things, we find a hundred ingenious reasons for excusing him. We say that genius has its own laws, that great minds must not be measured by little rules, that the fault was a mere spot upon the sun. I cannot help thinking that this is nothing but a kind of snobbery, and a rather mean kind. A fault is a fault, whoever commits it; and the honest thing is to look at it plainly, neither magnifying it nor explaining it away.

It is the same impulse, I think, that makes so many biographies such dreary reading. The writer, out of reverence or discretion, leaves out everything that is personal and intimate; the little habits, the tempers, the weaknesses, the tendernesses; and gives us instead a long and dignified record of offices held and speeches made and works accomplished. The result is not a man at all; it is a kind of almanac, a book of reference, which one may consult for a date but can never read for pleasure. What one wants from a life is the sense of a living person; and that comes, as a rule, from precisely the small things which the careful biographer has struck out.

I remember reading in the diary of an old ecclesiastic of high position an entry written on the day of the burial of a brother bishop, with whom he had been in sharp and long dispute. It is a small thing, but it tells one more of the writer than many solemn chapters.

## Draft B

There is a habit, common enough and rarely noticed, of excusing in famous people what we would plainly condemn in anyone else. A man of no importance who behaves badly is called what he is. A great man who does the same thing is said to have been under pressure, or to have lived in different times, or to have been too occupied with larger matters to attend to small ones. I have come to think this is a kind of snobbery. It assumes that fame or rank puts a person on another footing, where the ordinary rules are softened. But a wrong is a wrong whoever does it, and the honest course is to judge the great by the same measure as the rest of us, neither harsher nor kinder.

The same honesty, I think, is what makes a life story worth reading. Too many formal biographies are written in a spirit of respect so careful that nothing personal survives it. We are given dates, offices, speeches, appointments, the whole public record in good order, and at the end we know no more of the person than we did at the start. Such books feel less like the story of a life than like a reference almanac: useful for looking things up, but not for meeting anyone. What I want is the intimate detail, the small habits and private feelings, the moments when the public figure is simply a person.

Those moments are often the most revealing of all. I remember reading an entry in the diary of a senior cleric, written after he had attended the burial of a rival bishop. [name needed] The two had disputed with each other for years, and one might have expected the entry to be cool, or dutiful, or even faintly relieved. It was none of these. What the diarist recorded was the warmth he remembered in the man, and how little, standing at the grave, the old disputes seemed to matter. [exact wording needed]

A single entry like that tells us more about both men than a whole shelf of official accounts. It shows a person judging honestly, including himself, and finding that the quarrel was smaller than the man. That is exactly the kind of truth a good life story ought to keep.

## Draft C

I have always felt that there is a certain kind of charity which is really a disguised form of snobbery, and it is the charity which we extend so readily to the faults of the great. If an obscure man is hasty, vain or grasping, we call him so without hesitation; but if a famous man displays the same qualities, we are apt to speak of the defects of genius, or the privileges of eminence, or the pressure of great affairs, and so to excuse in the one what we condemn in the other. I do not think this is either just or wise, though it may pass for rather a generous habit. The faults of a distinguished person are faults still, and one ought to judge them honestly, neither with malice nor with a courtier's softness; for it is only by seeing a man as he was that we can understand him at all.

The same instinct, I believe, has spoilt a great many biographies. The formal life, in its solemn volumes, with its dates and offices and public utterances, carefully purged of everything personal, is perhaps little better than a reference almanac; one consults it, as one consults a directory, and closes it without having met anybody. What one desires in a life is the intimate detail, the small habits, the private moods, the words spoken off guard; these are the things which make a figure live, and without them the most eminent career is a mere procession of events.

Of course such details must be used with discretion; but how much, perhaps, they can reveal! There is a diary entry of a senior cleric, written after the burial of a rival bishop with whom he had long been in dispute; and instead of recalling their quarrels, he set down his remembrance of the man's warmth, as if the old controversies had simply faded, so to speak, in the presence of death. That single entry, I am inclined myself to think, tells one more about both men than a whole chapter of official narrative; it is a little lamp held up in a dim room, and for a moment one sees two faces clearly.
