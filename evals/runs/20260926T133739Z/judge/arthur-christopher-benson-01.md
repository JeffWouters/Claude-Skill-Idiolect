# Judge packet arthur-christopher-benson-01

## The passage (by the author)

It might be inferred from the above considerations that I am an inveterately unsociable person; but such is not the case. I am extremely gregarious at the right time and place. I love to spend a large part of the day alone; I think that a perfect day consists in a solitary breakfast and a solitary morning; a single companion for luncheon and exercise; again some solitary hours; but then I love to dine in company and, if possible, to spend the rest of the evening with two or three congenial persons. But more and more, as life goes on, do I find the mixed company tiresome, and the tete-a-tete delightful. The only amusement of society is the getting to know what other people really think and feel: what amuses them, what pleases them, what shocks them; what they like and what they loathe; what they tolerate and what they condemn. A dinner-party is agreeable, principally because one is absolutely tied down to make the best of two people. Very few English people have the art of conversing unaffectedly and sincerely before a circle; when one does come across it, it is a rare and beautiful art, like singing, or oratory. But the presence of such an improvisatore is the only thing that makes a circle tolerable. On the other hand, a great many English people have the art of tete-a-tete talking; and I can honestly say that I have very seldom been brought into close relations with an individual without finding an unsuspected depth and width of interest in the companionship.

But in any case the whole thing is a mere question of pleasure; and I return to my thesis, which is that the only possible theory is for every one to find and create the kind of society that he or she may like. Depend upon it, congenial society is the only kind of society to, and in which, any one will give his best. If people like the society of the restaurant, the club, the drawing-room, the dining-room, the open air, the cricket-field, the moor, the golf-course, in the name of pleasure and common sense let them have it; but to condemn people, by brandishing the fiery sword of duty over their heads, to attend uncongenial gatherings seems to me to be both absurd and unjust.

## Draft A

I have come, over the years, to know fairly well what sort of company suits me, and I have stopped apologising for it. My mornings I like to keep to myself. That is when I think most clearly and work most gladly, and the presence of another person, however pleasant, seems to thin the air a little. By the evening the case is quite different. Then I want talk and laughter, but only with a few friends, and preferably ones I know well enough that nothing needs to be performed.

What I value most is conversation between two people. When there are only two of you, each can follow the other's thought, turn it over, disagree with it, return to it later. Something is actually built. Add a third and a fourth and a sixth, and talk tends to break into scraps: a remark here, a joke there, and nobody quite finishing a sentence. There are a few people who can hold a whole table, who can draw everyone into a single thread and keep it going, and I admire them greatly. But they are rare, and I am certainly not one of them. For most of us a large gathering produces noise rather than conversation, and we come away tired without having said or heard anything that mattered.

None of this, I think, makes me unsociable. It simply means my taste runs one way and another person's runs another. Some people come alive in a crowded room; they like the stir of it, the chance meetings, the feeling of being in the middle of things. I do not grudge them that pleasure in the least. What I object to is the idea that there is one correct way to be with other people and that everyone ought to be bent towards it.

And that is exactly what happens when people are pressed into gatherings they dislike in the name of duty. We are told we owe it to our hosts, or to our neighbours, or to some vague notion of society, to turn up and be agreeable. The result is that the reluctant guest is miserable and, quite often, makes the evening a little worse for everyone else. It seems to me unfair to ask that of anyone. Company is meant to be a pleasure, and pleasures differ. The kindest arrangement is to let each person find the kind of company that suits them, and to trust that they will be better friends for it.

## Draft B

I will confess at once, for it is better to be plain about such things, that my own taste in company is a very definite one, and that I have long ceased to be ashamed of it. I like my mornings to myself. There is a certain freshness in the early hours, when the mind is clear and the day has not yet begun to make its demands, which I would not willingly surrender to any one; and I think that the man who is compelled to spend his mornings in talk, however agreeable, is robbed of something that he can never quite recover. But when the evening comes, and the work of the day is done, I find that I desire company, and of a particular kind; not a crowd, not a gathering of acquaintances, but a few friends, with whom one can sit at ease and say what comes into one's head.

And even among friends I find that the talk I care for most is the talk of two. When two people sit together, there is a real exchange; each can follow the other, can question and differ and return; the talk has a shape and a direction. But when a whole table is engaged, the thing becomes a kind of scramble; one catches a phrase here and a jest there, and nothing is carried through. It is true that there are a few people who can hold a whole table, who can gather up the scattered threads and make a single conversation of them; but they are very rare, and the gift is not one that can be learned. For most of us, a large party means a good deal of small, broken chatter, which leaves one tired and a little empty.

Now I do not in the least wish to lay down that my own preference is the right one. There are people, I know, who delight in a crowd, who are stimulated by noise and movement, and who find a quiet evening with a single friend intolerably dull. Let them have their crowds, by all means! What I protest against is the notion that there is one right way of being sociable, and that those who do not fall in with it are somehow failing in their duty. It seems to me a real unfairness to drag people, in the name of duty, into gatherings which they dislike, and where they give and get no pleasure. Society ought to fit itself to the temper of each; and a man who knows what company suits him, and seeks it, is not selfish, but simply sensible.

## Draft C

For myself, I have long known that the only kind of day which really suits me is one spent, in the morning, entirely alone and in the evening with a few friends. The morning hours are the hours of work and reflection; I like to have them to myself, to read and write and walk without the necessity of making conversation or of being agreeable to anybody; and when the light begins to fail, I like, so to speak, to come out of my shell, and to sit with two or three people whom I know and trust. I do not think this is an uncommon taste; indeed I am inclined myself to believe that it is more common than the world allows, and that many people who pass for sociable are perhaps only enduring, with a patient face, what they would gladly escape. Of course there are those who delight in a crowd,. But for my own part the best talk is the talk of two, a tete-a-tete in which one can follow a thought to its end, and examine it deliberately.

What is it that spoils the talk of a larger party? It is, I think, that very few people possess the gift of holding a whole table; the conversation breaks up into little eddies and backwaters, or else one person seizes it and the rest sit silent, like hens on a perch, waiting for the carriages. One who can gather up the whole company, encourage the shy and restrain the talkative, is a rare and precious creature.

The worst of it is that we are apt to treat sociability as a duty, and to drive people into gatherings which they dislike, in the name of what they owe to their neighbours. I believe this to be unfair, and even a little cruel. One would not compel a man who detested music to sit through a concert and call it a moral discipline; yet we do something very like it when we insist that everybody should dine out and pay calls and attend parties, whatever his temperament may be. Society should fit the person, not the person the society. Let those who love a crowd have their crowds; let those who love a quiet evening by the fire have that; and let us not pretend that the one is virtuous and the other selfish. For the solitary who is dragged unwillingly from his corner contributes nothing to the entertainment; he sits there, dutiful and dumb, and goes home at last through the dark streets, tired, unrefreshed, and a little ashamed of himself.
