# Judge packet alexander-smith-08

## The passage (by the author)

"News had come to Rome of the cruelty of the Sultan's mother to Constance, and an army was sent to waste her country. After the land had been burned and desolated, the commander was crossing the seas in triumph, when he met the ship sailing in which sat Constance and her little boy. They were both brought to Rome, and although the commander's wife and Constance were cousins, the one did not know the other. By this time, remorse for the slaying of his mother had seized Alla's mind, and he could find no rest. He resolved to make a pilgrimage to Rome in search of peace. He crossed the Alps with his train, and entered the city with great glory and magnificence. One day he feasted at the commander's house, at which Constance dwelt; and at her request her little son was admitted, and during the progress of the feast the child went and stood looking in the king's face. 'What fair child is that standing yonder?' said the king. 'By St. John; I know not!' quoth the commander; 'he has a mother, but no father that I know of.' And then he told the king--who seemed all the while like a man stunned--how he had found the mother and child floating about on the sea. The king rose from the table and sent for Constance; and when he saw her, and thought on all her wrongs, he could not refrain from tears. 'This is your little son, Maurice,' she said, as she led him in by the hand. Next day she met the emperor her father in the street, and, falling down on her knees before him, said, 'Father, has the remembrance of your young child Constance gone out of your mind? I am that Constance whom you sent to Syria, and who was thought to be lost in the sea.' That day there was great joy in Rome; and soon afterwards Alla, with his wife and child, returned to England, where they lived in great prosperity till he died."

## Draft A

The queen had been wronged twice over. Falsely accused and cast out, she was set adrift on the open sea with her young son, in a boat with no oars and no one to steer it, and left to whatever the wind and the waves might do with them. For a long time they drifted. She kept the boy close, prayed, and waited for a death that did not come.

What came instead was a ship. A Roman commander, sailing home, sighted the little boat and brought it alongside. He found in it a woman worn thin by hardship, still holding her child, and he took them both aboard. He did not know who she was, and she did not tell him. She was content to be a stranger, grateful for rescue and asking nothing more. He carried them to Rome, where she lived quietly with her son, unknown and unremarked, while the boy grew.

Far away, the king who had been her husband was carrying a heavy remorse. He had learned, too late, how she had been wronged, and the knowledge gave him no peace. In time he came to Rome himself, seeking to make amends for what had been done. A banquet was given in his honour, and among those who attended was the boy, sent there by his mother.

The king looked at the child and could not look away. Something in the face, in the bearing, in the way the boy stood, called up the wife he had lost. He asked whose son this was, and when he heard the answer, he understood. He wept at the table, in front of everyone, for the wife he believed dead and the son he had never known.

The rest followed quickly. Husband and wife were brought together, and the wrong between them was set right. But the queen had one secret still to tell. Her father was the emperor himself, and in all her years of exile she had never made herself known to him. Now she went before him and revealed who she was. He knew her at last, and the daughter he had long given up was restored to him.

So the family that had been broken apart by cruelty and chance was made whole again, and all ended happily.

## Draft B

The queen, wronged and cast upon the waters through no fault of her own, was carried many days upon the sea with her little son in her arms. She had no helm and no oar; she had only her patience, and her trust in a hand stronger than her own. Nor was that trust misplaced. It chanced that a Roman commander, returning homeward with his ships, came upon the frail vessel tossing in the waves, and drew near, and found in it a woman and a child. He did not know who she was, and she did not tell him. He saw only that she was fair and sorrowful and alone, and, being a man of good heart, he took them both aboard and carried them home. There the queen lived quietly, unknown and unasking, and the boy grew.

Meanwhile the king her husband had repented of the wrong done her, and his remorse was heavy upon him. It fell out in time that the commander made a banquet for him, and that the boy was sent in among the guests. The king looked at the child, and looked again. There was something in the face that he could not put from him--the face of the wife he had lost, looking out of the eyes of a stranger. He asked whose the boy was. And when he had heard what little the commander could tell, he could not keep back his tears, and he wept at the table before them all.

The rest is soon told. The king sought out the mother, and knew her, and fell at her feet; and she forgave him, as the good forgive, without making a merchandise of it. But one thing remained. Her father was the emperor himself, and all this while he had not known that his daughter lived. She went before him and made herself known; and the old man, who had mourned her as dead, took her once more into his arms. So the wronged were righted and the lost were found, and all ended happily--as, in the old tales, things were permitted to end.

## Draft C

The old story-tellers had no fear of misfortune; they heaped sorrow upon their heroines as a mason heaps stone upon stone, knowing that the higher the pile, the finer the view from the top. It is even so with this wronged queen. Cast adrift upon the sea with her little son, betrayed, exiled, and all but forgotten, she floats upon the waters as helpless as a leaf upon a mill-stream; and Providence, which has apparently deserted her, is only biding its time. At length a Roman commander comes upon the boat, takes the mother and child aboard, not knowing who they are, and gives them the shelter of his protection. Meanwhile the king, her husband, has learned the truth of the wrong that was done her. Remorse has laid its heavy hand upon him, and he goes about his affairs like a man who carries a stone in his breast; and at a banquet, among strangers, the boy is set before him. The king looks at the child, and looks again. Something in that young face--the eyes, the brow, the grave sweetness of the mouth--brings back to him the wife he thinks he has lost; and the strong man, in the midst of the feast, bows his head and weeps. It is perhaps the finest moment in the tale--verily, the old poet knew where the heart is tenderest! Recognition follows, and forgiveness follows recognition. Then comes the last and happiest turn, when the queen goes before the emperor, who is her own father, and reveals herself to him; and the old man, who had mourned her as dead, receives her as one risen from the grave. Husband, wife, son and father are gathered together at last. Nothing is wanting, and everything ends happily. We smile at so complete a reconciliation, and say that life is never so obliging; and it is not. The story, however, does not pretend to be life. It pretends to be better than life, and it succeeds!
