# Judge packet alice-meynell-07

## The passage (by the author)

He who has survived his childhood intelligently must become conscious of something more than a change in his sense of the present and in his apprehension of the future. He must be aware of no less a thing than the destruction of the past. Its events and empires stand where they did, and the mere relation of time is as it was. But that which has fallen together, has fallen in, has fallen close, and lies in a little heap, is the past itself--time--the fact of antiquity.

He has grown into a smaller world as he has grown older. There are no more extremities. Recorded time has no more terrors. The unit of measure which he holds in his hand has become in his eyes a thing of paltry length. The discovery draws in the annals of mankind. He had thought them to be wide.

For a man has nothing whereby to order and place the floods, the states, the conquests, and the temples of the past, except only the measure which he holds. Call that measure a space of ten years. His first ten years had given him the illusion of a most august scale and measure. It was then that he conceived Antiquity. But now! Is it to a decade of ten such little years as these now in his hand--ten of his mature years--that men give the dignity of a century? They call it an age; but what if life shows now so small that the word age has lost its gravity?

In fact, when a child begins to know that there is a past, he has a most noble rod to measure it by--he has his own ten years. He attributes an overwhelming majesty to all recorded time. He confers distance. He, and he alone, bestows mystery. Remoteness is his. He creates more than mortal centuries. He sends armies fighting into the extremities of the past. He assigns the Parthenon to a hill of ages, and the temples of Upper Egypt to sidereal time.

## Draft A

We have no instrument for measuring history except ourselves. When we say that something happened long ago, we are really comparing it with the span of our own years. A century means a certain number of lifetimes, and a lifetime means what our own life has felt like so far. That comparison is so automatic that we rarely notice we are making it.

For a child, the comparison works in history's favour. A child's first decade feels huge. Each year is long, crowded and distinct, and ten of them together make up an entire world. Measured against that, a hundred years is an almost unthinkable stretch, and a thousand years is simply beyond reach. The past ages that a child hears about take on a kind of majesty. They are not just earlier; they are immensely, gloriously far away, and the people in them seem to belong to another order of existence.

Adulthood changes the measure. The decades begin to feel brief. Ten years pass and seem to have taken very little time; much of them cannot even be told apart. Once that shorter decade becomes the yardstick, history shrinks with it. A century is now only a few of these quick decades laid end to end. The great ages of the past, which once seemed to lie at the bottom of a deep well, now seem close enough to touch. The centuries have not changed, but they have lost their weight.

I do not think the adult view is simply the correct one and the child's a mistake. Both are measurements taken with a personal ruler, and neither ruler is neutral. The child overestimates the distance because his own years are so full; the adult underestimates it because his have begun to slip by. Perhaps the truth about how far away the past really is lies somewhere between the two.

What is lost in the adult view is not accuracy but a certain feeling: the sense that the past is grand simply because it is far. Once the felt length of our own years collapses, that grandeur collapses too, and it is hard to recover it by an act of will.

## Draft B

We have no measure for the length of history except the length of our own lives. The historian may give us his dates, and we may subtract the one from the other with perfect accuracy; but the figure we arrive at means nothing until we have translated it into the only currency we possess, which is the felt duration of our own years. A century is so many lifetimes, and a lifetime is so many of the years we have known; there is, I think, no other scale.

In childhood that scale is magnificent. The first decade of a life is huge--a whole world of seasons, each one wide and slow and distinct, so that the child who has lived ten years has lived, in his own reckoning, through an immense succession of ages. When he learns of kingdoms that rose and fell long before his birth, he measures them by this generous standard, and they become majestic accordingly. The past is vast because his own brief past is vast; its dynasties stretch away like the endless afternoons of his summers, and its centuries are almost beyond imagining.

But the man grows up, and the years grow short. His decades begin to pass with an alarming swiftness; the interval between one event and another, which once seemed an age, is now a moment, and he looks back upon ten years as he once looked back upon a week. By this diminished measure the centuries lose their weight. So many lifetimes? It is perhaps nothing--a few of these hurried decades placed end to end. The ancient kingdoms move closer, their grandeur thins, and the whole long procession of history contracts into something almost within reach. Is this wisdom, or is it merely accuracy of a kind? It is, at any rate, an accuracy that has cost him the majesty of the past, and I know not that the exchange was a good one. The child measured wrongly, and saw truly.

## Draft C

We have no measure of the past but the measure of our own years. Whatever we say of centuries, and however we reckon them in figures, it is by the felt length of a year--our year, as we are living it now--that we judge how long ago a thing befell. There is no other rod. The historian may give us dates; he cannot give us the sense of their distance. That we supply ourselves, and we supply it out of our own lives.

So it is that the child sees the past as majestic. His first ten years are a world in themselves. They are so long, so full, so separated one from another, that the decade seems to him a great epoch; and he multiplies that epoch to make a century. What is a hundred years, when ten of them are an eternity? A thousand years is beyond conceiving. The old kings stand at the end of a vista so long that they take on grandeur merely by being there. It is not that the child reveres them for what they did. It is that they are so far off that they cannot but seem great.

Then the man's years begin. They are not less crowded--rather more; but they are brief. They go by in a rush of business, and the decade that once was an epoch is now a little stretch between one change of lodging and another. And the rod shrinks, and all that was measured by it shrinks with it. Ten of these short decades make a century, and a century is now a thing he could almost span himself. The old kings come near, and in coming near they lose their stature. They seem no longer to belong to another order of time, but to an earlier part of this one. It is not a change in them; it is a change in the man, and he cannot help it. Who but would wish, now and then, to have back the child's long rod--if only to see the centuries once again at their proper, their unreasonable, height?
