# Judge packet synthetic-noor-10

## The passage (by the author)

I have a pair of wool socks that are 12 years old. They have been darned nine times. I can see every repair.

They were grey when I bought them. Now they are grey with patches of green, brown and one of bright red. The red was all I had that night. I do not regret it.

Darning is not hard. It is slow. That is different.

You need three things. A darning needle, which is long with a big eye. Wool, close to the weight of the sock. And something round to hold the sock tight while you work. There are wooden darning mushrooms made for this. I use a light bulb. An old one, dead, the big round kind. It fits inside the heel just right.

So here is how I do it.

First I turn the sock the right way out and find the hole. Heels go first on my socks. Then the ball of the foot. Then the big toe. The hole is rarely one clean hole. There is a hole, and around it a wide ring of thin, worn wool. You have to darn the thin part as well. Or next month the hole is back, one ring wider.

I slip the bulb in and pull the sock tight over it. Then I run a line of small stitches round the thin part, about one centimetre out from the edge. This marks the patch. It also stops it from spreading.

Then I lay the warp. That is a set of long stitches going one way, top to bottom, across the hole. Side by side. Close together. Each one picks up a little of the good wool at the top and bottom. On a hole the size of a coin, that is about 20 lines.

It looks like the strings of a small harp.

## Draft A

I darn my own socks. The woollen ones. It is slow work. I do not mind.

A good wool sock is worth saving. It wears thin long before it wears out. A hole starts small. Leave it and it grows. So I mend them as soon as I see one.

Here is the kit. A long needle with a big eye. Yarn that matches the sock, or near enough. And something round to stretch the sock over. There are proper tools for this. An old light bulb works as well. The glass is smooth, and the curve fits a heel.

First the sock goes over the bulb. Hole on top, the knit stretched flat but not tight. Then look at the hole. Not only the hole. The wool around it. Hold it to the light. You will see the thin part, where the knit has worn to a few strands. It is bigger than you think. Bigger than the hole itself.

That thin part is the real job. If you darn only the hole, the edge of your patch sits on weak wool. It tears there soon enough. Then you have a new hole, bigger than the old one. I learned that on my first pair. [example needed: what happened to it].

So I mark out the patch around all of it. Hole and thin wool together. An outline, a little past the worn area on every side. I run a line of small stitches round the edge to show where it is.

Then the long threads. These run lengthwise, from heel toward toe. Straight, side by side, close together. Each one starts in sound wool on one side of the outline and ends in sound wool on the other. Across the hole they cross open air. That is fine. They are the frame.

Tonight the sock sits on the bulb. The long threads are in. The cross threads are for tomorrow.

## Draft B

Woollen socks wear through in predictable places, and for a long time I simply threw them away when a hole appeared. Now I mend them by hand. It is not difficult, and it needs very little equipment.

There are three things to gather. The first is a long needle, long enough to span the damaged area in a single pass, with an eye large enough for yarn. The second is yarn that matches the sock as closely as possible, both in colour and in weight, so that the repair sits comfortably and does not create a hard ridge. The third is something rounded to support the fabric from inside while you work. A proper darning tool does the job, but an old light bulb works surprisingly well: slip it into the sock under the hole and the fabric stretches smoothly over its curve.

Before starting, it is worth looking closely at the area around the hole. A hole rarely appears on its own. The fabric around it has usually been thinning for some time, and if you repair only the hole itself, the thin area next to it will give way soon after. So the repair should cover the thinned area as well, extending out into fabric that is still sound. That gives the new threads something firm to anchor into.

With the sock stretched over the support, I outline the patch first. I run a line of small stitches around the whole area to be repaired, enclosing the hole and the thinned fabric around it. This outline marks the edges of the work and helps stabilise the worn section.

Then I lay the lengthwise threads. Starting at one edge of the outline, I run the yarn in parallel lines from one side of the patch to the other, back and forth, each thread close beside the last. Across the hole itself these threads bridge open space, and across the thinned areas they pass through the old fabric. When the whole outline is filled with parallel lines, the foundation of the darn is in place and ready for the crosswise weaving.

## Draft C

You need three things. A long needle. Yarn that matches the sock. And something round to stretch the sock over.

The needle should be long. Long enough to reach across the hole and into some of the good wool either side. [detail needed].

The yarn should match. The colour, yes, but more than that. The thickness. [example needed]. A thread much thinner than the sock will not fill the gap. A much thicker one makes a lump you feel in your shoe.

For the round thing, I use an old light bulb. It is the right shape. It fits inside the heel and holds the wool smooth. [detail needed]. Nothing to buy.

Put the bulb in the sock so the hole sits on top of it. Now look closely. Not at the hole. Around it.

The hole is not the whole problem. The wool around it has worn thin too. Hold it up to the light and you can see it. That is where the next hole is coming from. If you only fill the hole, you join new wool to weak wool. It pulls away. So the repair has to cover the thin part as well. Go out until the wool feels firm again.

Then mark it out. I run a line of small stitches round the area. A rough box, a little bigger than the thin patch. [detail needed]. That is your outline. Everything happens inside it.

Now the lengthwise threads. Start at one corner of the outline. Take a small stitch in the good wool. Then carry the yarn across to the other side. Small stitch. Turn. Back again. Each thread next to the last, parallel. Close together, not overlapping. Do not pull them tight. Over the hole they just lie on top of the bulb.

When the box is full of parallel lines, stop. That is half the job. The other half goes crosswise.
