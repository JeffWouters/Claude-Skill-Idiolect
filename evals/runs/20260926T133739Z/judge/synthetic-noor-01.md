# Judge packet synthetic-noor-01

## The passage (by the author)

The kitchen tap started to drip in March. One drop every four seconds. I timed it with the clock on the oven.

At first I did nothing. A drip is easy to live with. You stop hearing it after a day or two.

But then I put a jug under it. By morning the jug held 600 millilitres. So that was the end of living with it.

I have fixed taps before. Not this one. This one is old, with a brass body and two cross-shaped handles. Hot on the left. Cold on the right. The drip came from the cold side.

So on Saturday I cleared the counter. I laid a tea towel in the sink. That is the first rule: cover the plughole. Small parts fall. They always fall.

Then I turned off the water. There is a stop valve under the sink, behind the bleach and a bag of old sponges. It had not been turned in years. It took both hands and a cloth for grip. Then it gave.

I opened the tap to drain it. A cough of air. A thin run of water. Then nothing.

The handle came off with one screw under a small blue cap. I lifted the cap with the tip of a knife. The screw was a flat head, soft with age. I used the right driver and pushed hard as I turned. It came out whole.

Under the handle sat the headgear. That is the brass part that holds the washer. It takes a spanner. Mine is an adjustable one, 20 centimetres long, from my father's box. I set it snug on the flats and held the tap body with my other hand. You have to hold the body. Or the whole tap turns in the counter and you make a new problem.

Now the handle. On an old tap this is where the trouble starts. A cap on top hides the screw. I prised it off with a thin blade. Slowly. Old plastic is brittle. Under it the screw was stiff. I held the handle still with one hand and turned the screwdriver with the other.

Then the valve assembly. It sits inside the body of the tap, and it is often tight. This is where you can do real damage. Put a spanner on the valve and just heave, and the whole tap turns with it. Then you are twisting the pipes under the sink. Or cracking the joint at the worktop.

## Draft B

For a long time I told myself the kitchen faucet was only dripping a little. Then one evening I set a container under the spout and left it there overnight. In the morning there was [amount needed] of water in it, and that settled the matter. A leak you can measure is a leak you can no longer pretend is not there. The steady tick in the sink had been costing water every hour of every day.

So I decided to repair it myself. The faucet is old, and old fixtures reward patience more than strength, so I started with preparation rather than tools.

The first job was the drain. Small parts have a way of finding the one opening in a sink, and a screw or a washer lost down the pipe turns a simple repair into a much longer afternoon. I closed the drain and laid a cloth over it, so that anything I dropped would land on something soft and stay where I could see it.

The second job was the water supply. I found the shut-off valves under the sink and closed them, then opened the faucet to let the last of the pressure and water run out. Only when nothing more came from the spout did I feel ready to take anything apart.

The temptation is to lean on it, but on an old faucet that force goes straight into the body of the fixture and the pipes behind it. ## Draft C

I had ignored the kitchen tap for too long. Not any more.

The drip was slow. A drop now and then, nothing dramatic. Easy to live with. So one evening I put a measuring jug under the spout. In the morning I measured what had collected overnight. [number needed: how much water in the jug]. That was more than I thought. It is always more than you think. And a tap that loses that much in one night loses it every night. I could not ignore it after that.

Here is the plan. Protect the drain. Shut off the supply. Remove the handle. Remove the valve assembly. Find the worn part.

First the drain. Small parts fall. They fall, and they go straight down the plughole. So I put the plug in and laid a cloth over it. An old tea towel. Folded twice. If a screw can reach the drain, it will. That is the rule.

Then the water. The shut-off valves sit under the sink. Two of them. Hot and cold. I turned both off and opened the tap to be sure. A last trickle. Then nothing.

The handle came next. There is a small cap on top. Under it, a screw. I prised the cap off with a thin blade and took the screw out. The handle did not want to move. Old taps do not. Years of limescale and grease. I worked it off slowly, patiently. No hammer.

So to the valve. This is the part that needs care. The valve is tight, and the tap body is not. Turn the valve hard and the whole body turns with it. Then you are fixing the pipes as well as the tap. So I braced the faucet body with one hand. The spanner went in the other. Short, firm turns. It gave at last.

Tonight the valve sits on the tea towel. Next to the screw. Both of them dry.
