There is a poem by a Victorian poet which strikes the modern reader as a strange thing to have been written in a peaceful and prosperous age. It is a call to war. The poet looks about him and sees a nation grown fat and greedy, given over to buying and selling, and he can find no cure for it but the shock of battle. [quotation needed: the poet's lines longing for a heroic leader] He longs for a strong man to arise and lead his people out of their comfortable meanness, and he welcomes the coming conflict as a purifying fire.

We may think this a curious prescription. But we should remember to whom the poem was addressed. It spoke to young Britons who had grown up without any memory of war. They had heard of it from their grandfathers, and they had read of it in their histories, but to them it was a romantic thing, belonging to the same world as knights and crusades. What they knew at first hand was peace, and peace had shown them chiefly its idle and sordid side; they had seen the counting-house but not the battlefield.

It would be easy to dismiss all this as a relapse into savagery, and to congratulate ourselves on being wiser. I should hesitate to do so. The poet was not praising slaughter for its own sake. He was expressing, in the only language that seemed strong enough, a genuine disgust with a life that asked nothing of men but that they should grow rich.

That disgust recurs in every generation that has known only peace. It deserves to be taken seriously, not because its remedy is a good one, but because the disease it describes is real. If we will not provide some nobler occupation for the energies of youth, youth will go looking for one.
