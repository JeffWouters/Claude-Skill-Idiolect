The mining settlements produced a kind of storytelling that I have not met elsewhere. It was perfectly candid, and it was entirely without judgment. The narrator told you what had happened, and he left you to decide what to think about it, if indeed there was anything to think.

I remember hearing a parishioner describe the arrival of a new pastor. The pastor had come full of zeal, and he had not been long in the place before one of the elders of the church, having taken more whisky than was good for him, chased him out of town. The parishioner related all this with complete composure. He did not condemn the elder, nor did he pity the pastor. He mentioned that the elder was a good man in most respects and a generous contributor, and that the pastor was a fine speaker who perhaps did not understand the community. In a small town such a story would have been told very differently. There the gossip is a moral censor who enjoys his office; he collects the failings of his neighbours in order to display them, and he takes care that every anecdote ends with a verdict. But in the camps there was a rough equality among men that made this impossible. Nobody was in a position to look down upon anybody else, and nobody cared to try. The result was a frankness of speech that had no spite in it.

The humour of such talk was usually accidental. The narrator set the faults and the merits of his subject side by side, as a shopkeeper might set out his goods, without any attempt to strike a balance. It was the listener who noticed that the balance was absurd. The narrator never did.
