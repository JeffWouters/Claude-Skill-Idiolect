I once had a long conversation with a man whose trade had been the forging of checks. He was not in the least ashamed of himself, and he was very willing to discuss his case. What puzzled him, he said, was the distinction that society drew between his offence and the operations of the great financiers. He had altered a check. They had carried through great schemes in the market. [example needed: the scheme he named] Where, he asked, was the difference?

I confess that I had no ready answer. I could have told him that the one thing was against the law and the other was not, but I suspected that he knew this already, that he had turned it over in his mind through a good many idle hours, and that he did not regard it as an answer at all but only as a restatement of the question in the language of the courts. He was not asking about the law. He was asking about the principle behind it, and on that subject I found myself less well informed than I could have wished.

It became clear as we talked that he regarded himself as a man of principle. He had his own code, and he had kept it faithfully; it was merely that his code happened to differ at certain points from the conventions of the community. He spoke of those conventions without bitterness, rather as a scholar might speak of the opinions of a rival school. He had considered them carefully. He had rejected them.

This, I think, explained the most remarkable thing about him, which was his entire freedom from any sense of guilt. A man feels guilty when he has broken his own rules. My forger had broken only other people's, and for other people's rules he had never professed any great respect. His private ethics were complete and consistent, and they accounted for everything he had done. There was nothing left over for the conscience to trouble about.
