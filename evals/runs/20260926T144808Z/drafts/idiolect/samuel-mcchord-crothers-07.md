It is often asked why the nations that call themselves Christian have fallen so far short of their ideal of peace. The question is usually put in a tone that implies its own answer, namely that the profession was never sincere. But the matter is not quite so simple.

We should remember who it was that carried civilisation from one country to another. It was not a company of saints. It was a company of very imperfect men, traders and soldiers and adventurers, who took their faith with them as they took their tools, and who used both according to the habits they had already formed. The work they were engaged in was slow work, and it was done by hands that were not always clean. It would have been surprising if the result had been otherwise.

Milton understood this. In one of his poems he pictures the coming of a golden age, when the old evils shall be done away and peace shall return to the earth. [quotation needed: Milton's lines on the golden age] Yet the poet no sooner draws the picture than he admits that the time is not yet. The golden age must wait. There is a great deal of history between the vision and the fulfilment, and he does not pretend otherwise.

The Christian nations, then, are not frauds but learners, and rather slow learners. Their early faith was a faith of conquest and dominion, and it had little use for the gentler teachings on which it was supposed to rest; those teachings were read in the churches and ignored in the council chamber. It is only by degrees that a people discovers what its own creed requires of it. The discovery is not yet complete.
