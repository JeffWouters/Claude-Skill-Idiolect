I ought to say at the outset that I owe a great part of whatever reading education I possess to a certain novelist.  I read him before I ever saw him; I read him, indeed, with the uncritical devotion of a young man who has discovered that books may be about people very like the people he knows.  Later, behind the counter of a bookstore, I met him.  That is, I sold him books.  It is not quite the same thing as meeting a man, but it is, in its way, a fairly intimate acquaintance.

I am aware that there is something a little undignified in the position of one who sets down his recollections of the celebrated persons he has served.  The coachman who drove the great statesman has his memories.  So has the footman, and the lady's maid, and the whole chattering company of servants below stairs, who know, or believe they know, precisely what went on above.  There is even, I am told, a memoir by a waiter of ancient Rome, who observed the notable men of his day across the dishes he carried.  I do not claim to be better than these.  I claim only to be one of them; and I have noticed that the reading public is seldom unwilling to listen to the coachman.

Well, then.  Let me begin with a man waiting.

He stood near the front of the store, a little apart from the counters.  He was extremely well dressed: a coat of excellent cut, gloves of the proper shade, a stick held at the proper angle, the whole effect expensive, quiet, and faintly severe.  He was not looking at the books.  He was looking at the door.  Now and then he consulted his watch, put it away, and resumed his contemplation of the door.  Customers passed and repassed him; he did not seem to notice them.  Who he was I did not then know.
