What, in the end, is the weekly team meeting actually for? The question is usually asked with some irritation, by people who have just sat through another hour in which nothing was decided, and the answer they expect is that it serves no purpose at all. It may be that this answer comes too quickly, since it assumes that a meeting exists only to produce decisions and then judges it, rather harshly, by that standard alone. I suspect most standing meetings do something else, something harder to name and considerably harder to measure.

There is also the matter of what a regular gathering provides simply by recurring. It gives people who might otherwise work in isolation a predictable occasion to see one another, to notice who seems stretched and to hear about problems before they grow; it gives newer members of the team a place to learn how the group talks; it gives the group itself a rhythm that, in some sense, holds it together. None of this appears in the notes taken afterwards, and that is arguably why it is so easily dismissed whenever someone proposes to clear the calendar. You may have felt the loss of such contact only once the meeting that provided it was gone.

It seems to me that the better response is neither to defend every standing meeting nor to abolish them wholesale, but to ask each one to justify itself again at intervals. A team might pause, from time to time, to consider whether a given meeting still earns its place, what it would lose if the meeting stopped, and whether that loss would be felt by anyone at all. Many meetings would survive this kind of scrutiny in a trimmed or refocused form, shorter perhaps, or with a clearer purpose than before; some would come to a quiet end, and few people would mourn them. The exercise matters less for its verdicts than for the attention it brings to habits we rarely examine. Perhaps the question is not whether we should meet, but whether we still remember why we do.
