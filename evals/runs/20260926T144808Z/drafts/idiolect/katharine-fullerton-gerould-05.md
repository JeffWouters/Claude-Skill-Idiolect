The trouble with cultivated learning in a democracy is not that people dislike it. They are, for the most part, quite willing to admire it from a distance. The trouble is that it rests on an assumption which the democratic temper cannot bring itself to accept: the assumption that some minds are better than others. We have been taught, from the nursery up, that men are naturally equal. It is a noble sentiment, and in its proper place a useful fiction; but it is not true, and nobody who has spent an hour in a schoolroom believes that it is. Men are not equal in strength, in beauty, in industry, or in wit. Wherever they are gathered together, a hierarchy appears, whether we like it or not. We may abolish titles and forbid privileges; the hierarchy will simply reassert itself in another shape.

Culture is, by its very nature, exclusive. It ranks people; that is what it is for. But--and this is the point that its enemies always miss--it does not rank them by wealth or by birth. The son of a millionaire may be a barbarian, and the son of a village cobbler may be the most finished scholar of his generation. Culture asks only one question: what can your mind do? It is the least snobbish of all aristocracies, and it is an aristocracy none the less. That is precisely why the egalitarian distrusts it.

Now I have no quarrel with political equality. Let every one vote, by all means, and let every one stand equal before the law. The mischief begins when equality is demanded, not in politics only, but in every sphere of life--in taste, in learning, in the very estimate we form of one another's minds. For culture breeds two things which such a demand cannot tolerate. It breeds humility, because the cultivated person is always aware of how much greater minds have gone before him; and it breeds a certain disdain, because he cannot pretend that the second-rate is as good as the first. Neither humility nor disdain has any place in a world where every opinion is held to be as good as every other. The one is thought servile and the other insolent. And so, between the two, culture is squeezed out, and we are left with a comfortable, levelled mediocrity which calls itself enlightenment. I fancy it is nothing of the sort.
