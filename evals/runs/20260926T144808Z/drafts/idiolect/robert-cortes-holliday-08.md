I should like to recommend to anyone who finds time hanging heavy on his hands the pastime of looking for rooms.  I do not mean looking for rooms because you need them; that is a trial, and a grim one.  I mean looking for rooms as other people go to the theatre, or walk in the park, or collect old prints, for the pleasure of the thing.  There is no better entertainment to be had for nothing.

It is, to begin with, highly educational.  You learn more about your fellow creatures in an afternoon of ringing doorbells than in a month of reading novels about them.  You see how people live, what they put on their mantelpieces, what they consider a view, and what they think a reasonable thing to ask for it.  And it is, curiously enough, a wonderful remedy for low spirits.  One comes home from it, as a rule, in excellent humour, and very well satisfied with one's own lodging, whatever its deficiencies.

The advertisements alone are worth the price of the newspaper.  They are written in a language of their own, a language made up almost entirely of flattering stock words, each of which means something a little different from what it says. But if you would see the pastime at its best, you should go looking for rooms in the old legal inns of London.  There, I think, are the most charming rooms in the world.  You climb a worn stair; you pass a door with a name painted on it that has been there, apparently, from time immemorial; and you come into a room with deep windows, panelled walls and an atmosphere of learned quiet, as though generations of lawyers had sat there thinking, slowly and profoundly, about property.  Of the modern comforts there are, it must be admitted, few or none.  The heating is a matter of opinion.  The plumbing is a matter of history.  Nobody seems to mind.
