The humour of the prospector is not the humour of the backwoodsman, though the two are often confused by those who have met neither. Lowell's frontier figure is a brash fellow who boasts and swaggers and expects you to be amused by the sheer size of his claims. His wit is the wit of exaggeration, and it is always louder than the occasion requires.

The miner has another manner altogether. He is, in his own fashion, a courteous man, and he has a great fondness for grand titles; every acquaintance is promoted to some high rank, and the humblest shanty on the hillside is given a name that would not disgrace a palace. There is nothing brash about this. It is the instinct of a man who has very little else to give and therefore gives honour freely.

His wit, accordingly, lies in understatement. Where the backwoodsman would shout, the miner murmurs, and the murmur is the more effective of the two. I have in mind the kind of sign he would set up beside an open pit, which did not warn the traveller in capital letters of the danger of death but observed very politely that the ground was uneven at this point and that gentlemen would do well to take care. The same mildness appears in the story of a preacher who had been threatened by some of the rougher members of his congregation. He came into the pulpit and took out his gun and laid it deliberately upon the open Bible. Then, leaving the text of the sermon to take care of itself, he preached without any manuscript at all. It was, perhaps, an unusually attentive audience.
