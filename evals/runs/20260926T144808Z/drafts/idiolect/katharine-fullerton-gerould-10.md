We are often told that plain living and high thinking go together, as though a bare table and a cold room were somehow good for the intellect. I have never seen the slightest evidence for it. The great minds of the past, so far as one can make them out, preferred comfort when they could get it, and made no secret of the preference. Thoreau himself, the patron saint of the simple life, is a less convincing witness for frugality than his admirers suppose [example needed, how Thoreau's own life shows a taste for comfort]. And Shakespeare, who thought as loftily as any man has ever thought, tells the same story, for he too, I suspect, liked his comforts [example needed, the evidence for Shakespeare's taste for comfort]. Genius does not ask to be starved.

I do not say that there is any disgrace in doing our own chores. If there is nobody else to do them, we must do them, and we may do them cheerfully; there is nothing degrading in a broom. My quarrel is only with the notion that it is somehow more civilised to sweep one's own floor than to pay a skilled person to sweep it. It is not more civilised. It is merely more laborious, and the labour, in most cases, is rather badly done.

For the abolition of domestic service--which is what the reformers really want, whether they say so or not--would destroy a great deal more than a convenience. It would destroy, perhaps irrecoverably, a body of skill. The good cook, the good laundress, the good parlour-maid are the heirs of a tradition, handed down from one generation to the next and learned, as all real skills are learned, by long apprenticeship. Once the tradition is broken it cannot easily be mended. And what is offered in its place? A world in which every one is to be a generalist; in which every man is to be his own cook, and every woman her own charwoman, and nobody is to be very good at anything. Granted that this is democratic; but it is not efficient, and it is certainly not civilised. The division of labour was the beginning of civilisation, and to undo it is to go backwards. A nation of amateurs is not a nation of philosophers. It is merely a nation of incompetents, with no more time for lofty thought than it had before--and a good deal less comfort in which to think.
