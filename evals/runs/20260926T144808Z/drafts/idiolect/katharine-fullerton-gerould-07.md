I should like to say a word for caviar. It is not, I admit, a subject that seems to call for defence; but the fashion for plain living has gone so far of late that even caviar needs a champion. Not long ago the cult of simplicity was merely silly, and one could afford to smile at it, go home, and eat a proper supper with a clear conscience. It has become something worse than silly. It has become a doctrine, with its apostles and its martyrs, and it is beginning to do real harm.

It is curious how often the apostles of simplicity turn out to be socialists as well, and how often the socialists turn out to be vegetarians. The overlap is not an accident. Both creeds, at bottom, have the same end in view: they wish to level every one down to a common standard, and they are quite content that the standard should be a low one. The vegetarian would have us all eat lentils because some of us cannot get beef; the socialist would have us all do without because some of us cannot get enough. Neither of them, so far as I can discover, proposes to raise anybody up. Their equality, in short, is the equality of the bread-line.

Now caviar is, frankly, an unequal thing. It is costly, it is rare, and it is not, and in the nature of things cannot be, available to all. And part of its value--I will not pretend otherwise--lies precisely in that fact. Granted that this sounds shocking; but consider. A pleasure that every one, rich or poor, clever or stupid, can have at any moment of the day is hardly a pleasure at all, and nobody values it. The savour of a rare thing is partly the savour of its rarity, and to abolish the rarity would be to abolish the savour with it. You would not make the world richer by distributing caviar to the multitude. You would merely make caviar common.

There is more to it than that. Nobody, I fancy, likes caviar the first time he tastes it. It is an acquired taste, and like all acquired tastes, whether for olives, for poetry, or for the society of intelligent people, it represents a certain effort of education. The palate that has learned to relish it has been trained, as the ear is trained to relish a fugue. Such acquired indulgences are not vices to be apologised for. They are the small victories of cultivation over crude appetite, and they deserve to be raised into principles. The simple-lifers would throw them all away. I would rather keep them, and keep the caviar too.
