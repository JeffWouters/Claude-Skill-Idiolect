There is a certain type of critic who takes a peculiar delight in the believer in peace. He points to the armies and the navies, to treaties broken and wars declared, and invites us to observe that the nations which talk most eloquently of brotherhood are precisely the nations most ready to fight. The believer, he concludes, is either a fool or a fraud. Since he is evidently not a fool, he must be a fraud.

I have no wish to be unjust to the scoffer. He has a firm grasp of the facts, and the facts are on his side. He knows what has been done and what is being done, and he can recite the military budgets of the great powers with the accuracy of a clerk who has never been asked what the figures are for. What he does not know is what is going to be done. His knowledge is complete, but it is complete only because it stops at the precise point where the interesting part begins.

But consider the charge of fraud a little more closely. It rests upon the discovery that there is a gap between what men profess and what they practise, and the scoffer announces this discovery as though it were his own invention. Every man who has attempted to build anything, whether a house or a business or a character, made it long before him and made it every morning. There is always a plan, and there is always a structure that falls short of the plan. To call the difference a fraud is merely to reveal that one has never built anything at all. The architect is not a liar because the scaffolding is still up.

The truth is that an ideal, once it has been publicly declared, does not remain idle. It begins to work upon the conscience of the man who declared it, and it continues working long after he would gladly have dismissed it. He does not live up to it to-day, nor to-morrow, and possibly he never will; but he cannot entirely forget it, and it has an embarrassing habit of appearing at inconvenient moments to inquire what he means by his conduct. Conscience is a patient creditor.

I am reminded of the colleges that were founded on the frontier in the early days, when a few earnest men would erect a building, give it an imposing name, and open its doors to learning and to whatever pupil might wander in. Sometimes there was only one. The scoffer of that period could easily have ridiculed the institution. Here was a college, and where were the scholars? Yet the founders were not impostors. They were declaring what they intended to have, and eventually they had it.

So it is with the ideal of peace. It has at present only one solitary pupil in each nation, and he is not a particularly promising one. But the college is open, and the name is over the door. The scoffer may keep his facts; the believer is content with the future.
