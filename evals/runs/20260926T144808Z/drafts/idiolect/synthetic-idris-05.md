What does a progress report actually tell the person who reads it? On the surface it tells them a great deal, since it lists the tasks completed, the tasks in progress and the tasks still to come, often in careful detail. It may be that this detail is precisely what conceals the more important information, which is what the team has learned since the last report and how that learning has changed its view of the work ahead. A list of finished tasks describes activity; it says very little about understanding.

You may have written reports of this kind yourself, and noticed how naturally they fall into the shape of a ledger. The form asks what has been done, and so that is what gets written, while the discoveries that might matter more (that a component is less stable than assumed, or that a requirement means something different from what everyone thought) find no obvious place to go. I suspect most readers sense the gap without being able to name it, and compensate by asking for more reports, which only produces more lists.

There is also a quieter cost to reporting in this way. When the only acceptable content is completed work, the areas that remain unclear tend to disappear from view until they can no longer be ignored, and by then the options for dealing with them have narrowed considerably. A report that stated, alongside its list, how confident the team felt about the remaining work and which parts were still poorly understood would arguably be shorter and more useful at once; it would direct attention to where it is needed rather than spreading it evenly across everything.

It seems to me, however, that changing the template will achieve rather little on its own. People already know how to express doubt; what they lack is any assurance that doing so will be received as useful rather than as a sign of weakness. An organisation that adds a field for confidence to its reports, while continuing to treat every admission of uncertainty as bad news, will simply receive a field filled with optimism. The harder work lies in how leaders respond when someone does write that a part of the project is unclear, whether they ask what would help or ask why it was not already solved.

The progress report is, in the end, one of the few occasions in working life on which a team formally tells others what it knows. Perhaps its value depends less on what it lists than on what it is safe to admit.
