It is women who tell most of the white lies of society, and they can hardly be blamed for it. The burden falls to them by a simple division of labour. In most households it is the wife who manages the couple's invitations, answers the notes, remembers the anniversaries, and decides--with a tact the husband seldom suspects--which dinners must be accepted and which may be declined. Every refusal wants an excuse, and every acceptance of a dull evening wants a little show of pleasure. The husband signs nothing and pretends nothing. He is spared the fib because he is spared the business.

Men, besides, speak more bluntly among themselves. They can tell one another that a man is a bore, or that a proposal is nonsense, and part the best of friends; they have a sort of licence which does not extend to the drawing-room. Women rarely drop their public role, even with each other. They are always, perhaps, a little on duty.

There is another reason, and it is not a pretty one. The world is ready to suspect a woman's motives on the slightest provocation. If she does not praise another woman's gown, she is thought envious; if she is cool to a pretty neighbour, she is thought jealous. The suspicion is usually unjust--but it is none the less real, and a sensible woman might well be expected to take account of it. So she admires the gown she does not care for, and is warm to the neighbour she finds tiresome, not because she is false but because the truth would be misread. The small fib is her defence against a larger injustice. I fancy most men, placed in her position, would fib a good deal more.
