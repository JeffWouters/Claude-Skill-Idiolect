Why is it that each new method of estimation, however promising it looks at first, leaves software forecasts roughly as unreliable as they were before? A team exchanges hours for points, or points for sizes, or sizes for some other unit that has lately come into fashion, and for a while the change feels like progress. It may be that the unit was never the problem. What stays the same through every such exchange is the attitude to doubt, which is to say the habit of treating uncertainty as an embarrassment to be hidden, rather than as information to be shared.

An estimate, in the end, is only a statement of what we know at the moment we make it. We tend to treat it as a fixed quantity, to be defended or apologised for, when it would be more honest to expect it to move as our knowledge of the work grows; a forecast that never changes is arguably less trustworthy than one that does, since it suggests that nobody has been learning. I suspect many teams sense this already, but find it difficult to say aloud to the people waiting on the date.

It seems to me that the more useful step is to separate, before any number is given, the work that resembles things the team has done before from the work that is genuinely new. The first can be predicted with some confidence, because there is experience to draw on; the second can only be bounded, and perhaps revisited once the first uncertainties have been explored. Treating the two alike, in whatever unit, produces a figure that is too confident about the unknown and too cautious about the familiar. Perhaps what needs to change is not how we count, but how willing we are to admit what we cannot yet know.
