There is a kind of lie that nobody, I fancy, has ever been punished for telling, and that most of us tell, week in and week out, without the smallest qualm. It is the small polite untruth that spares another person's feelings. You thank your aunt for the ornament she has sent you, which you did not want and would never have bought, and you say it is exactly what you wanted; you do not say that it will go straight into a drawer and stay there. You are shown a new infant, red and wrinkled and entirely without features, and you declare that it is beautiful. Every one does it. The aunt is pleased, the mother is pleased, and nobody is a penny the worse--except, perhaps, the conscience of the very scrupulous, who ought to know better than to be troubled.

For there are people who insist upon blunt honesty in these matters, and who are rather proud of it. They will tell you that they "cannot pretend," that they "must say what they think." Granted; but why must they? Nobody has asked them for a verdict on the ornament. The truth, in such a case, serves no purpose whatever except to gratify the person who tells it. It is a selfish indulgence, and like most selfish indulgences it comes expensive in the end. The blunt people lose their friends, one by one, and they generally lose them without in the least understanding why. They have been honest--and honesty, they suppose, is its own excuse, and needs no apology to anybody. It is not.

There is another thing to be said. People who seldom praise anything are apt to become habitually critical; the faculty of admiration, like any other faculty, withers when it is not used. They begin by withholding a compliment out of scruple, and they end by being unable to see what is good in anything at all. And a compliment that is too long delayed loses most of its value. The friend who waits until she is perfectly sure, and who then weighs every word before she speaks, may be scrupulously accurate; but by the time her praise arrives, nobody wants it. My point is only this: a little generous exaggeration, offered promptly and without calculation, is worth more than a great deal of careful truth offered too late. We are not on oath at the dinner table. Heaven forbid that we should be!
