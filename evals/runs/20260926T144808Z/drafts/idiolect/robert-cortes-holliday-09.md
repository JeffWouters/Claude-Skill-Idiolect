It has often struck me as odd that so few people who have worked in bookstores have written about the trade.  Sailors write about the sea, and doctors about their patients, and policemen about their beats; every calling that brings a man into contact with the variety of mankind seems, sooner or later, to produce its memoirs.  But the bookseller's clerk is silent.  He stands all day at the meeting place of literature and the public, and he says nothing.

He cannot say that he has nothing to tell.  No one sees more of the human comedy, in its small and daily forms, than the man behind the book counter.  The vanities, the timidities, the enthusiasms, the pretences and the little absurdities of the reading public pass before him without end; people who would guard their secrets from a confessor will reveal the whole state of their minds to him by what they ask for, and by the manner in which they ask.  He observes it all.  He remembers a great deal of it.  And he keeps it to himself.  Why?  Partly he is tired at the end of the day.  Partly he thinks that nobody would believe him.  Partly, too, having spent so many hours among books, he has acquired a certain respect, as it were, for the difficulty of adding to their number.

There is another reason, and it has to do with what the public already believes about him.  The public holds, on this subject, two opinions, and they are flatly opposed.  According to the first, the bookseller is a learned person: an elderly man in spectacles, among dusty shelves, who has read everything, who knows the date of every edition and the history of every binding, and who parts with a volume reluctantly, as though it were a friend.  According to the second, the man who sells books is an ignorant clerk, who could not tell you the author of the most famous novel in the language, who confuses the poet with the publisher, and who would sell a hymnal and a treatise on poker with the same bright indifference.  The public holds both of these opinions at once, and sees no contradiction.

The second figure is a stock character of the comic papers.  He appears in them regularly. A man who knows that this is the picture in the reader's mind may be forgiven for hesitating before he takes up his pen.  Whatever he writes, he will be read, so to speak, through the comic papers.

So he says nothing, and goes on wrapping parcels.
