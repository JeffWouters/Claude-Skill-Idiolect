The first time I saw him I did not know who he was.  I knew only that he was a very unusual-looking person, and that he was talking.  [example needed: what was odd about his appearance]  He stood near the middle of the store, in conversation with a lady and a gentleman, and he was talking with an earnestness that seemed altogether out of proportion to the place, which was, after all, a shop, and to the hour, which was an ordinary afternoon.  He talked with his hands.  He gestured, not largely or theatrically, but continually, as though the thought he was expressing were a thing of some intricacy which had to be shaped in the air before it could be delivered.  His companions listened.  They appeared to be used to it.

The lady, I afterwards learned, was a novelist herself, a writer of the kind of fiction that is called, in the language of the reviews, "society" fiction; the gentleman was her husband.  They were, it was plain, old friends of his.  Presently the conversation, or rather the discourse, came to some natural pause, and the three of them went out together.  I happened to be near the window.  I saw them go up the street, arm in arm, the novelist in the middle, still talking, still gesturing with the hand that was free, the lady on one side and the husband on the other, making their way among the crowd on the pavement as if the crowd were not there.  It was, somehow, a charming sight.  Then they turned a corner, and the street closed over them.

Somebody told me, afterwards, who he was.

He came in again, later, and then fairly often.  On these later visits he was usually alone, and he did not talk.  He browsed.  I have seen a great many people browse in a bookstore, and I have come to believe that it is an art, like any other, which few practise well.  He practised it perfectly.  He would take down a book, open it, and pass instantly out of the world of the store into the world of the page; he was absorbed, lost, gone.  People moved around him.  Clerks reached past him for other volumes.  He did not notice.  He stood there turning the leaves with an expression of such complete and simple contentment that I can compare it to nothing so well as the face of a happy child with a new picture book, who has forgotten, for the moment, that there is anyone else in the room.
