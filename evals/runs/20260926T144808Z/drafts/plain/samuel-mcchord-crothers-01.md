There is a certain kind of critic who enjoys nothing so much as catching a believer in peace with blood on the nation's hands. He points to the armies, the treaties broken, the wars fought by people who sing hymns about brotherhood on Sunday, and he declares the whole business a fraud. The ideal, he says, is a mask. Take it off and you find the same old appetite for conquest underneath.

It is worth answering him, because he is not stupid. He sees clearly what has already happened. He can list the battles and the broken promises, and he is usually right about them. What he cannot see is what is still happening. He knows the settled facts and misses the ones still forming. He reads a nation as though it were a finished book, when it is a manuscript with most of its pages still blank.

His real mistake is about how anything gets built. He treats the gap between what people say they believe and what they actually do as proof of bad faith. But that gap is simply what building looks like. Every house has a stage when the plans are drawn and the ground is still bare. Every reform has a long season when the law says one thing and the street does another. To call that gap a fraud is to show that you have never tried to make anything yourself. Anyone who has started a business, raised a child or learned a trade knows that intention always runs ahead of performance. That is not hypocrisy. It is the order in which things happen.

A declared ideal does real work even while it is being betrayed. Once people have said out loud that peace is what they want, the saying stays with them. It becomes a standard they can be measured against, and more to the point, a standard they begin to measure themselves against. The first time a nation goes to war after declaring for peace, few may feel uneasy. The next time, more will. Conscience is slow, but it is stirred by words people cannot take back.

Think of the college founded on the frontier with a grand name, a grand charter and exactly one pupil. The scoffer would laugh at it, and in a narrow sense he would be right: one student is not a university. But the founders were not lying. They were declaring what they meant to build and committing themselves to building it. The charter came first because it had to. The buildings, the teachers and the crowds of students came later, if they came at all, and they came because someone had named the thing before it existed. [example needed]

The ideal of peace is in the same position. It has its charter and its handful of pupils. Mocking it for being small is easy. Seeing what it may become takes more patience than the scoffer usually has.
