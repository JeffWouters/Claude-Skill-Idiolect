Of all the forces that work against cultivated learning, none is more steady or more thorough than the ideology of equality. I do not mean equality before the law, or at the ballot box, which is a sound principle and a hard-won one. I mean the broader belief that people are, by nature, equal, and that any arrangement which ranks them is a wrong to be put right.

That belief is simply false. People differ in strength, in patience, in memory, in taste, in the quickness and depth of their minds. No amount of good will alters this, and no society has ever managed to organise itself without some form of hierarchy. Abolish one ranking and another appears in its place, often cruder than the one it replaced. The question is never whether there will be distinctions among people, but which distinctions we will honour. [example needed]

Culture is, by its nature, one of those distinctions. It is exclusive, and there is no use pretending otherwise. But it is worth being precise about what it excludes. It does not rank people by wealth, since a rich man may be entirely uncultivated and a poor one deeply read. It does not rank them by birth, since no one inherits a trained mind. It ranks minds: their range, their discipline, their capacity to recognise what is fine and to tell it from what is merely popular. That is an aristocracy anyone may enter, but only by the long road of effort, and many will not take it.

The egalitarian cannot rest content with political equality. He wants equality in every sphere, in taste and judgment and learning as well as in rights, and so he comes to regard any claim of superiority in these things as an offence. A person who says that one book is better than another, or one mind better furnished than another, is accused of snobbery. The very act of judging becomes suspect.

This leaves culture with nowhere to stand, because culture depends on judgment and on the feelings that judgment breeds. It breeds humility, in the person who measures himself against the great and finds himself small. It also breeds disdain, a quiet and necessary disdain for the shoddy, the facile and the second-rate. Both are unwelcome in a society that insists all things are equally worthy. Humility looks like self-abasement, and disdain looks like arrogance, and so both are discouraged, and with them the whole habit of mind that made learning worth pursuing.

We can keep our political equality and keep our culture too. What we cannot do is extend equality into every sphere and still expect cultivation to survive there.
