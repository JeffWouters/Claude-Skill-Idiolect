A salesman in a shop learns a good deal about people that they would never tell him, and he learns it without asking. He has to. His job is to stand behind the counter and take whatever the customer brings in with him, whether that is a good mood or a bad one, a hurry, a grievance, a headache, or the lingering irritation of some quarrel at home. He cannot answer back and he cannot walk away. He simply absorbs it, and serves the next one.

The result is that he sees people with their guard down. Nobody puts on a performance for a shop assistant. They do not need his good opinion, so they do not trouble to earn it, and what they show him is closer to what they are than anything they would show a friend or a colleague. A man who is gracious in a drawing room may be curt at a counter. A man who is shy in company may be unexpectedly kind to someone he will never see again. After a few years of this you come to trust the counter as a test of character more than almost anything else.

It is by that test that I remember the novelist. He was, by then, very famous, and he had every reason to treat a young assistant as part of the furniture. He did not. He spoke to me as an equal, and not in the careful, slightly performed way that some well-known people adopt when they want to be thought democratic. He was simply earnest. He asked questions because he wanted to know the answers, and he listened to what I said as though it might be worth hearing. His courtesy was not a manner laid on for the occasion. It seemed to go all the way through.

One of the things he wanted to know was how much of our stock was made up of English novels. He asked it seriously, the way a man might ask about the state of a trade he had some stake in, and I did my best to give him an honest estimate. He was curious about how books actually moved, who bought them, and what stayed on the shelves.

At some point the talk turned to another writer, a rival of sorts, whose name was often mentioned alongside his own. [name needed] I expected a compliment, or perhaps a careful silence. Instead he dismissed the man in a word: a journalist. He did not elaborate, and he did not sound bitter. He said it as though he were classifying a specimen, and then he went back to the books.

I have thought about that conversation many times since. It was not the remark about the rival that stayed with me, sharp as it was. It was the fact that he had talked to me at all, and talked to me as though I counted.
