We usually think of lying as a simple matter: a person says something he knows to be false, and that is a lie. But there are two kinds of deception that sit awkwardly with this definition, and they pull in opposite directions. One is exaggeration. The other is the deception that never says anything untrue.

Take exaggeration first. Every good storyteller embellishes. The fish grows a little in the telling, the storm grows a little louder, the rude stranger a little ruder. Strictly speaking, each of these touches is false. Yet the listener who hears the story often comes away with an accurate picture of what happened: that the fish was large, the storm alarming, the stranger unpleasant. The details have been heightened, but the overall impression is true, and it may even be truer than a flat recital of facts would have been, because it conveys how the thing felt. We do not usually call such a storyteller a liar, and I think we are right not to. [example needed]

This suggests something important about honesty. It concerns meaning, not only the literal wording. What matters is the picture that forms in the other person's mind, and whether we have tried to make that picture accurate or to make it false. Words are the means; understanding is the end.

Which brings us to the second kind of deception, and to the people who practise it. There are those who pride themselves on never telling an outright falsehood. They choose their words with great care, so that every sentence can be defended as literally correct, and they use those sentences to lead others to believe what is not so. They answer a question that was not asked. They let a wrong assumption stand. They say something true in such a way that it will be misread, and then, when challenged, point out that they said nothing false.

Such people think of themselves as scrupulous. In fact they understand truth poorly. They have mistaken its outward form for its substance, and kept the letter of honesty while abandoning its purpose. The embellishing storyteller, who bends the words to serve an accurate picture, is closer to the truth than they are, for they bend an accurate set of words to serve a false one.
