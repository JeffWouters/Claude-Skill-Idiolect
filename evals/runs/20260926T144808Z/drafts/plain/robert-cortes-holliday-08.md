Most people look for rooms only when they have to, and they treat it as a chore to be got through as quickly as possible. I have come to think of it the other way round. Looking for rooms, when there is no pressing need to find one, is among the most enjoyable ways I know of spending an afternoon.

For one thing, it teaches you a great deal about people. You are let into houses you would never otherwise enter, and shown how strangers live, what they keep on their mantelpieces, what they think a tenant ought to put up with, and what they think is worth boasting about. Every landlady has a theory of the world, and most of them will share it if you stand in the doorway long enough. For another, it cheers you up. However dull your own lodgings, there is always a room somewhere that is darker, damper, or more expensive, and seeing it does wonders for your contentment at home.

The search begins, of course, with the newspaper advertisements, which are a literature of their own. They are written in a small stock of flattering words that appear again and again, and a little experience teaches you what each one means. A room described as cosy is small. One described as bright has a window. Airy usually means draughty, and select usually means expensive. Of all the rooms I have looked at, the most charming are in the old legal inns of London. These are the courts and buildings where lawyers have lived and worked for generations, tucked away behind the busy streets, with quiet squares, worn staircases and doors painted with the names of their occupants. The rooms have panelled walls, deep window seats and views over lawns and old brick. They feel as if nothing much has changed in them for a very long time.
