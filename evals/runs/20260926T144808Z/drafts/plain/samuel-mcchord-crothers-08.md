The humour of the prospector is often lumped together with the humour of the backwoodsman, as though every joke told west of the settled country came from the same source. It does not. The two men are funny in quite different ways.

Lowell's frontier figure, the backwoodsman as he appears in Lowell's work, is brash. He boasts, he exaggerates, he shoulders his way into a conversation and fills it. His jokes are loud and his manners are rough, and much of the comedy comes from how little he cares who hears him.

The miner is another sort altogether. He prefers courtesy. He addresses rough men by grand titles and treats the most ordinary exchange with a formality that seems borrowed from a drawing room. [example of such a title needed] Where the backwoodsman shouts, the miner bows.

His wit follows from that. It lies in mild understatement, in saying far less than the situation calls for and saying it politely. The classic instance is the warning sign beside an open pit. Another man would write DANGER in large letters. The miner puts up a courteous little notice, something closer to a gentle suggestion than a warning, as if the chief risk of falling in were a small social embarrassment. The same spirit shows in a story about a preacher who came to a camp and was threatened. [details of the threat needed] He did not back down, and he did not bluster. He took out his gun and set it down on top of the scripture in front of him. Then, with the book held shut under the weapon, he spoke to the congregation without notes, unscripted, saying whatever he had to say. Nobody needed the gesture explained. It was polite, it was quiet, and it made its point completely.

That is the miner's humour at its best. It never raises its voice. It lets the facts, and a little courtesy, do the work.
