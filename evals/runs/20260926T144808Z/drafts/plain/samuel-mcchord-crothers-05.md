Mining settlements produce a particular kind of storytelling, candid and entirely without judgement. You hear it best from the ordinary people of the camp, who talk about what happened in their town the way other people talk about the weather.

Take a parishioner recounting how the camp got through a new pastor. The pastor arrived, began his work, and was soon chased out of town by one of the elders of his own church, who happened to be drunk at the time. [details of the chase needed] The parishioner tells all this quite calmly. There is no outrage in the telling and no particular sympathy either. The pastor came, the elder got drunk, the pastor left. That is what happened, and the teller sees no need to add anything to it.

This frankness comes out of the equality of camp life. In a mining settlement nobody has much standing over anybody else, and nobody has a reputation so delicate that it has to be protected by careful talk. People say what they saw. Compare that with gossip in a settled small town, where the same story would be passed along in whispers, shaped to wound, and loaded with hints about what the listener ought to think of everyone involved. Small-town gossip is spiteful because it is always keeping score. Camp talk is not keeping score at all.

The result is a kind of humour that nobody intends. The storyteller mixes faults and merits in the same breath without ever pausing to weigh them. The elder was a drunk who ran the pastor out of town, and also [merit needed]; the pastor was driven off, and also [merit needed]. All of it is laid side by side and left there, with no verdict attached. It is the absence of a verdict that makes it funny. The listener keeps waiting for the moral, and the teller, who never thought there was one, simply moves on to the next thing.
