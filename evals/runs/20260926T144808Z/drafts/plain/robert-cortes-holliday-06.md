People imagine that selling books is a matter of knowing where things are on the shelves. It is, in part. But the larger part is knowing how to answer anything at all, from anyone, at any moment, and then doing it again for the next person, who wants something entirely different.

The gift shoppers are the hardest. They come in wanting a present for someone and have no idea what. You make a suggestion and they say the person already has it. You make another and they say it is too serious. A third is too light, a fourth too long, a fifth too expensive, a sixth not expensive enough to look like a proper gift. You go through half the shop with them, and at the end they either buy the first thing you showed them or leave with nothing, thanking you warmly for your help.

What makes the job tiring is not any one of these customers but the constant change of gear. One minute a scholar is asking about an edition of [work needed] and expects you to know the difference between printings. The next, someone asks a question so slight that you are not sure it is about books at all. [example needed] You have to take both questions with the same seriousness, because both customers are paying, and because to each of them the question is the important one.

Over the years the requests pile up into a kind of catalogue of human need, and a long and odd catalogue it is. It begins, in my memory, with people asking for guides to the care of a baby, earnest and a little frightened, wanting to be told what to do. It runs on through requests I could never have predicted, [examples needed], each one delivered as though it were the most natural thing in the world to ask a stranger for. And it ends, somewhere, with prayer books: plain ones and bound ones, small enough for a pocket or large enough for a lectern. Between those two points lies nearly everything people hope a book can do for them.

You learn to listen for what is not being said. The person asking for a guide to baby care may really want reassurance. The person asking for a prayer book may be buying it for a death in the family, or for a wedding, and the right answer depends on which. None of this is in any catalogue, and none of it is taught. You pick it up at the counter, one customer at a time.
