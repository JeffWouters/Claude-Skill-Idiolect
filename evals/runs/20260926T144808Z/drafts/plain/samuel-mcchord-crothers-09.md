The impostor left a memoir, and a good part of it is taken up with explaining why the small crimes of his youth were not really crimes at all.

He had been to sea. [details of the voyages needed] When he came home he did nothing in particular. He idled, drifting about his home district without work or plans. Yet the idleness did not stop him from passing judgement. He watched the misdeeds of the people around him and criticised them freely, apparently without noticing that his own conduct gave him little standing to do so.

His views on theft are revealing. He wondered, he says, why the young men of the neighbourhood thought it harmless to raid a beehive or strip an orchard. They did it as a matter of course, as a kind of sport, and nobody treated them as thieves for it. He seems to have found this puzzling. But the puzzle cuts both ways. If taking honey or apples was harmless, then the line between that and other kinds of taking was not as firm as the neighbours pretended. It is hard to tell whether he meant this as a criticism of them or as an excuse for himself. Most likely it was both.

His argument about false money goes further. He maintained that fake coins hurt nobody. Money, he reasoned, has no worth of its own. Its value exists only because people believe in it. A coin that passes from hand to hand, accepted by everyone who takes it, does exactly the same work as a genuine one. So long as the belief holds, nobody is worse off. The counterfeit, on this view, is not a fraud but simply one more coin in a system that runs entirely on trust.

It is a clever argument, and like many clever arguments made by people with something to excuse, it stops just short of the point where it would hurt. It says nothing about what happens to the last person holding the coin when the belief fails. [example needed]

Reading the memoir, one has the sense of a man who could always find a reason. The reasons were never quite good enough, but they were always ready.
