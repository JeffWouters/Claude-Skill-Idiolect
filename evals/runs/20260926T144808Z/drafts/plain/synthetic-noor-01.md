The old kitchen faucet had been dripping for a while before I decided to do something about it. It is easy to ignore a slow leak, because one drop at a time does not look like much. But a slow leak wastes far more water than you would expect, and once I started thinking about how much was going down the drain every hour, I could not leave it alone any longer. It had to be dealt with.

Before touching a single screw, I spent my time on preparation. The first job was to isolate the supply. There is no point starting to dismantle a faucet while water can still reach it, because the moment you loosen the wrong part you will have a fountain on your hands and a wet floor to show for it. I shut off the supply, then opened the faucet to let the remaining pressure and water run out, so I could be sure nothing was still feeding it.

The second job was to protect the drain. Faucets are full of small parts, and small parts have a habit of slipping out of your fingers at exactly the wrong moment. I covered the drain opening so that anything I dropped would stay in the sink rather than disappear into the pipework. Only then did I start taking things apart. The key thing I had to keep in mind was to steady the faucet body while loosening the internal brass assembly. Old fittings can be stiff, and if you put force on the brass without holding the body firmly, you risk twisting the whole faucet, straining the connections underneath or damaging the sink around it. So one hand held the body still while the other worked the assembly loose, slowly and without forcing it.
