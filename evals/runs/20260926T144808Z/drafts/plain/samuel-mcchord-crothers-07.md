It is fair to ask why the countries that call themselves Christian fall so far short of the peace their faith describes. The question is usually asked with a sneer, but it deserves a real answer.

Part of the answer lies in who did the work. The people who carried civilisation outward were not saints. They were flawed men and women, with the same greed, fear and pride as anyone else, and the work they were doing was slow. Nothing that changes how whole peoples live happens in one generation, or in several. To expect a finished result from unfinished people is to misunderstand the job.

Milton understood this. In one of his poems he pictures a golden age, a time when peace and justice return to the world, and he describes it with real longing. Then he stops himself. The golden age, he admits, is not yet. It must wait. [quotation needed] The poem holds both things at once: the vision is true, and it is not here. That is a more honest position than either the believer who pretends the vision is already realised or the scoffer who says that because it is not realised it was never meant.

Seen this way, the so-called Christian nations are learners, not frauds. A learner is someone who has accepted a standard he does not yet meet. His failures are real, but they are the failures of someone still in school, not of someone lying about having graduated.

It also helps to remember what those nations first took from their faith. Their early belief did not dwell on the gentle teachings. It took up the parts that suited conquering peoples and passed over the parts about meekness and mercy. [example needed] Those gentler teachings were there all along, waiting, much like Milton's golden age. They are still waiting to be read with the attention they always asked for.
