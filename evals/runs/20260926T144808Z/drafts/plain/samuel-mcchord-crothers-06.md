I once had a conversation with a man who had forged a check, and he put a question to me that I could not answer on the spot.

He did not deny what he had done. He had altered a check, and he knew perfectly well that the law called it a crime. What he wanted to know was how it differed from the schemes that go on every day in the big markets. Men there move sums far larger than anything he had touched, and do it by methods that come to much the same thing, making paper say something that is not quite true in order to take money that is not quite theirs. They are called financiers. He was called a forger. Where, he asked, was the line?

I had no ready reply. I felt there was a difference but could not put it into words quickly enough, and he saw that. [example of the schemes he named needed]

What struck me most was how he saw himself. He did not think of himself as a criminal who had slipped. He thought of himself as a man of principle who happened to be at odds with convention. The world had its rules, and he had his, and his were at least honest about what they were.

That private code explained everything about him. It explained the forgery, it explained his calm, and above all it explained why he felt no guilt. Guilt needs a rule you believe in and have broken. He had broken no rule he believed in. By his own ethics he had done nothing wrong, and so there was nothing for his conscience to take hold of. He was not hiding remorse. He had none to hide.

I have thought about his question since. I still believe there is an answer, but I notice it is not as quick as I would like.
