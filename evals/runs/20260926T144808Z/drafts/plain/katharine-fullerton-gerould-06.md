If the small social untruths of daily life are a burden, it is a burden that falls mostly on women. This is not because women are less honest than men. It is because of the work they are given to do.

In most households it is the wife who handles a couple's invitations and social dealings. She accepts the dinner and declines the lecture, writes the note of thanks, and explains why they cannot come on Thursday. Every one of those exchanges carries its small demand for tact, and tact, pressed hard enough, becomes the polite fib. The husband, who never sees the letters, is spared the need to tell them. There is a difference, too, in how the sexes speak when they are among their own. Men speak more bluntly with one another. They contradict, they grumble, they say plainly that the dinner was dull, and nobody minds much. Women rarely drop their public role in this way. Even among friends the courtesies continue, the compliments are paid, the awkward truth is softened, because the habit of managing other people's feelings is not something that can be set down at the door.

And then there is a quieter pressure, one that is seldom admitted. Women's motives are too readily suspected. If a woman says that another woman's gown is unbecoming, or her party tiresome, it is assumed that she is jealous, however just the remark may be. A man may say the same thing and be thought merely candid. Knowing this, a woman learns to praise what she does not admire, not from any love of falsehood but to avoid an accusation she has done nothing to earn. The minor fib becomes a kind of self-defence.

So the next time someone remarks that women are the more practised at white lies, it is worth asking who handed them the practice.
