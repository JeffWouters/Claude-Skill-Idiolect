There is one idea, more than any other, which stands in the way of cultivated learning in this country; and that is the idea that men are naturally equal. I know it is not a popular thing to say. But it seems to me that the idea is simply false, and that everybody who has ever watched a nursery or a schoolroom knows it to be false. Children are not equal; grown people are not equal; and no amount of legislation will make them so. Wherever human beings are gathered together, some of them will rise and some of them will sink, and a hierarchy of one sort or another will appear. We may abolish one kind of rank. We shall only find another springing up in its place.

Now culture is, by its very nature, exclusive. I do not mean that it is exclusive in the way that a fashionable club is exclusive. It does not care who your grandfather was, and it does not care what is in your purse. The poorest boy may enter it, and the richest may be turned away at the door. But it ranks. It ranks minds--it says, quite frankly, that this mind is finer than that, and this taste truer, and this judgment sounder. That is what it is for. A culture which refused to make such distinctions would not be a culture at all; it would be merely an accumulation of information, and information (it is always information that we are offered, nowadays!) is a very different thing.

Let us admit, by all means, that political equality is a good thing. I do not propose to quarrel with the ballot. But we are no longer content with equality in politics. We demand it in every sphere--in manners, in speech, in education, in taste. We are made uneasy by anyone who seems to think himself better informed than his neighbour, and we call him a snob; and we are made still more uneasy by anyone who admits that he knows less, and we call him a fool for admitting it. And there, I think, is the heart of the matter. For culture breeds two things which the thoroughgoing egalitarian cannot abide. It breeds humility, because the cultivated man knows how much finer minds than his own have gone before him. And it breeds disdain--a decent, quiet disdain for the shoddy and the second-rate. The two go together; one cannot have the first without the second. But in a world where everyone must be as good as everyone else, there is no room for either. We must not bow, and we must not scorn. And so, very naturally, we cease to be cultivated.
