There is a kind of lie which nobody, so far as I know, has ever been hanged for, and which a great many excellent people tell every day of their lives. I mean the small, polite untruth that is told to spare somebody's feelings. Let us not pretend that we are above it. Who among us has not written to an aunt to thank her for a gift that went, the same afternoon, to the back of the highest shelf? Who has not bent over a perambulator and declared that the infant within was the loveliest thing he had ever seen--when it was, frankly, rather like every other infant, and not a very handsome one at that? I, for one, cannot manage them without fibbing; and I have long since ceased to be ashamed of it.

There are, of course, people who will not fib. They make a point of it. They tell you that your new hat is unbecoming, that your aunt's gift was hideous, and that the baby looks like a boiled lobster; and they expect you to admire them for their candour. I am sorry, but I do not admire them. Their bluntness seems to me the most selfish of indulgences. It costs them nothing, and it costs other people a good deal; for the pleasure of feeling honest, they are quite willing to hurt, and they are quite willing, too, to lose their friends. They do lose them, in the end. One cannot go on forever being told the truth about one's hats.

But there is a subtler danger in the habit, and it is this. The person who will not praise unless praise is absolutely and literally deserved soon finds that he does not praise at all. He has grown so watchful against flattery that he has forgotten how to be generous. He notices, instead, what is wrong; and noticing what is wrong becomes, with him, a second nature. He is not a liar, certainly; but he has become something almost as tiresome--a habitual critic, who cannot look at a dinner or a drawing-room or a friend without mentally marking it down.

And even when he does bring himself to a compliment, it is usually too late. He waits until he is quite sure; and by that time the moment has passed. A kind word, like a cup of tea, is best when it is hot. Offered a week afterward, with all the proper reservations, it is worth very little to anybody. I would rather have a warm fib at the right moment than a chilly truth at the wrong one; and I suspect that most of us, if we are honest, would say the same.
