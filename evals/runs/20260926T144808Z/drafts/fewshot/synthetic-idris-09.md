There is a small moment in the life of most engineering teams that reveals a great deal about their culture: the moment when someone is asked a question and does not know the answer. What happens next — whether they say so plainly, or deflect, or offer a confident guess and hope for the best — tells you more about how the team learns than any stated policy on training or growth. In teams where it is acceptable to say "I don't know", the admission tends to open a conversation, and the gap is filled quickly, often by someone who had been quietly wondering the same thing. Admitting a gap publicly, in other words, does not only help the person who admits it. It helps everyone who shared the gap without saying so, and it makes the team as a whole learn faster than it otherwise would.

Why, then, is the admission so difficult? Part of the answer lies in the way knowledge functions as a form of status in technical work. The engineer who knows the obscure corner of the system, who can answer the question others cannot, gains a kind of standing that is not easily surrendered, and saying "I don't know" can feel like giving some of it away. Another part lies further back, in habits formed long before anyone joined a team. Schooling, for most of us, rewarded the right answer and penalised its absence; the student who admitted ignorance was rarely praised for honesty. It would be surprising if those lessons simply vanished on entering professional life.

Changing this requires, I think, a different understanding of what competence actually is. We tend to picture it as a store of facts, accumulated over years and drawn upon when needed, so that a competent engineer is one whose store is large. Yet the store is always incomplete and always ageing, and much of what it contains will be obsolete within a few years. What distinguishes the genuinely capable engineer is not the size of the store but the disposition to keep adding to it — a continuing curiosity, a readiness to notice what one does not understand and to go and find out. Seen this way, the admission of ignorance is not a failure of competence but one of its clearest expressions.

A team that wishes to make learning open might therefore begin by examining how it responds to the words "I don't know", and particularly how its most senior members respond when they say them.
