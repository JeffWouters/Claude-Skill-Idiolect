The memoir of an impostor is seldom dull, for the author has had a good deal of practice in making himself interesting. The one I have in mind [title needed] is especially instructive on the subject of excuses.

After several voyages at sea our author returned home and settled down to a life of idleness. This did not prevent him from taking a lively interest in the morals of his neighbours. On the contrary, a man with nothing to do has the more time to notice what others are doing wrong. He was very severe upon the misdeeds of the place, and he records them with the indignation of a man whose own hands were, for the moment, unoccupied.

He was much puzzled by the attitude of the young men of the neighbourhood toward certain kinds of property. They would not dream of taking a neighbour's purse. Yet they saw no harm in raiding his beehives or stripping his orchard on a summer night. Why, he asked, should honey and apples be fair game when money was not? It was a reasonable question, and I do not know that anybody ever gave him a satisfactory answer. I suspect that he did not want one. He wanted only to establish that the line between honest and dishonest was drawn in a very arbitrary place, so that a man of intelligence might be excused for drawing it somewhere else.

He drew it, in fact, so as to leave room for counterfeiting. His argument on this point is really ingenious. Money, he says, has no worth in itself. Its value lies entirely in the belief of those who use it. So long as a coin is believed in, it is as good as any other coin. Who, then, is harmed by a false one? The man who receives it is content, and the man who passes it on is content, and the belief goes on doing its work. It is only when someone insists on looking too closely that any damage is done, and the fault, he implies, is with the one who looked.
