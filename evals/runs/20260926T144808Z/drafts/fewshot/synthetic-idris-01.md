Few things attract such reliable complaint as the standing team meeting, and few are defended so rarely. The case against it is familiar enough: an hour each week, or a quarter of an hour each morning, in which little is decided and much is repeated, and which could, we are told, be replaced by a short written update that nobody would need to attend. There is some truth in this. Yet the complaint rests on a particular assumption about what meetings are for, namely that their purpose is to produce decisions, and that a meeting which decides nothing has therefore failed. I am not convinced the assumption holds. A good part of what a standing meeting provides is not decision at all but contact — the regular occasion on which people who might otherwise work in parallel for weeks are obliged to see one another, to notice who seems stretched or uncertain, and to maintain the loose familiarity on which later cooperation depends. That kind of social glue is difficult to measure, which is perhaps why it is so easily dismissed.

None of this means that every standing meeting deserves to survive. The more modest proposal is that a team should, at regular intervals, ask each of its recurring meetings to justify itself afresh, as though it were being proposed for the first time. What is this meeting actually for? Would we create it today if it did not already exist? Who needs to be in the room, and who attends only out of habit or a vague fear of missing something? Questions of this kind are uncomfortable, because they imply that the arrangement we have been tolerating may not have been worth tolerating, but they are also the only honest way of telling a meeting that serves the team from one that merely occupies it.

My expectation is that most meetings would come through such a review in altered form rather than disappearing altogether: shorter, perhaps, or given a narrower focus, or held less often. A few would end, and their ending would be a relief to everyone involved. What seems worth resisting is the all-or-nothing framing, in which the only choices are to keep every meeting as it is or to abolish the lot. Neither honours what the meetings were meant to do.
