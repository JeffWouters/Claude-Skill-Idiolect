There is a certain kind of critic who takes great delight in exposing the believers in peace. He has caught them, he thinks, in the very act. They talk of brotherhood and they pay their taxes for battleships. They preach good will on one day of the week and read the war news with lively interest on the other six. What is this but fraud? And he goes about with the air of a man who has looked behind the scenes and found the painted canvas and the ropes.

I do not quarrel with his facts. He is an excellent observer of whatever has already come to pass. Show him a thing that is finished, with its corners squared and its accounts closed, and he will tell you exactly what it is. His trouble is with the things that are not finished. He has no eye for what is still in the making. A half-built house is to him merely a house without a roof, and he is quite ready to prove that no one can live in it.

Now it must be confessed that there is always a gap between what a man says he believes and what he does. There is a gap between the plan of a bridge and the bridge. If we are to call every such gap a fraud, we shall have to put the whole race of builders in the dock, and the architects first of all. The critic who is so shocked to find that the ideal is not yet the fact shows, not his shrewdness, but a curious innocence. He has never tried to build anything. He does not know that every enterprise begins with a declaration which the performance lags far behind.

What is the use, then, of declaring an ideal before we are ready to live up to it? The use is that it will not let us alone. Once a principle has been publicly announced, it takes up its lodging in the conscience and begins to make itself disagreeable. It asks awkward questions. It compares. It remembers what we said last year. Little by little it works upon us, till we are ashamed to fall so far short, and then, being ashamed, we do a little better.

I think of those colleges that were founded on the frontier when the frontier was very new. There was a charter and a board of trustees and a president, and a name that promised learning to a whole commonwealth. There was also, in the first term, a single pupil. A scoffer might have made merry with it. A university with one student! Yet the founders were not deceiving anybody. They were stating an intention and giving it a place to grow. The pupil came first; the others came afterward, because somebody had been bold enough to open the door before the crowd arrived.

The believers in peace are in much the same case. Their college has few students as yet. But the charter is written, and the door is open.
