Anyone who has looked carefully at a working calendar will have noticed that it contains a certain number of meetings whose purpose nobody can quite recall. They recur weekly or fortnightly, they are attended with reasonable diligence, and they proceed according to a pattern that everyone understands, yet if you were to ask the participants why the meeting exists, the answers would be vague, various, or faintly embarrassed. It is tempting to regard these meetings simply as waste. I think it more illuminating to regard them as ritual — as practices that persist not because they continue to serve their original function but because they have become part of the texture of working life, and because stopping them would require a kind of decision that nobody feels entitled to make.

Most such meetings, it is worth remembering, did not begin as ritual. They began as a response to something real: a shared problem that needed several people's attention, a project that required close coordination, a period of difficulty in which regular contact was genuinely valuable. At the moment of their creation, they made a good deal of sense. The trouble is that the problem they addressed was eventually solved, or dissolved, or migrated elsewhere, while the meeting itself remained in the calendar, repeating on schedule with the quiet persistence that recurring invitations seem designed to encourage.

What is striking is how rarely anyone notices the moment at which a meeting stops being useful. There is no single occasion on which it becomes pointless; its usefulness simply thins, week by week, until what remains is the form without the substance. Because the decline is gradual, it passes without comment, and because the meeting is shared, responsibility for questioning it is shared too — which is to say that it belongs to no one in particular. Perhaps the most useful habit a team could adopt is not to cancel such meetings wholesale but to ask, from time to time, whether each one would still be created today.
