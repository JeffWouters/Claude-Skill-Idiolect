Every few years a new method of estimation arrives in software teams with the promise that, this time, forecasting will become reliable. Hours give way to ideal days, ideal days to story points, story points to T-shirt sizes or to the refusal to estimate at all, and each change is accompanied by a brief period of optimism before the familiar disappointments return. It is tempting to conclude that we simply have not yet found the right unit. I suspect the more accurate conclusion is that the unit was never the problem. What these methods tend to leave untouched is the attitude a team and its organisation hold towards uncertainty — the expectation that a number, once spoken, should remain fixed, and that any later change to it is a kind of failure. Replacing hours with points does nothing to that expectation; it merely gives it a new vocabulary.

A more useful starting point, it seems to me, is to treat an estimate as a statement about what we currently know rather than a prediction we are bound to honour. Understood this way, an estimate ought to move as knowledge grows, and a team that revises its figures as it learns is not being unreliable but is doing exactly what an honest forecaster should. The discomfort arises only when the organisation receiving the estimate has not been invited to share this understanding, and so reads every revision as a broken promise.

There is also a distinction worth drawing within the work itself. Some of what a team is asked to do closely resembles things it has done before, and for this work past experience offers a reasonable guide; other work is genuinely new, and experience offers much less. Predictions tend to go wrong when the two are blended into a single figure, as though the familiar and the unknown carried the same kind of risk. Separating them, and being candid about which is which, would not make forecasting certain. It might, however, make it truthful.
