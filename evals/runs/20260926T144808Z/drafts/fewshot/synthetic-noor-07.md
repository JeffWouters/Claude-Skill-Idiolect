I used to work on the train. Laptop open before we had left the station. Emails, notes, a report half done. I thought it was clever. An extra hour a day, for free.

It was not free. I got to the office already tired. I got home still thinking about work. So I stopped. Now the laptop stays in the bag. I sit by the window and I do nothing. Sometimes I doze. Mostly I look out.

The route is the same every day. The view is not. In winter it is dark both ways. I see my own face in the glass and the lights of farms far off. Then the days get longer. One morning in [month needed] there is a grey light over the fields when we pull out. A few weeks later it is sunrise. Low and orange, straight in through the carriage. People pull down the blinds. I leave mine up.

There are landmarks. [landmark needed] The bridge over the river. A church spire that shows up over the trees just before the second stop. A field that floods every winter and turns into a lake with swans on it. I wait for them. I would notice if one went.

That is the odd thing about a routine. It makes you sharper, not duller. When you see the same thing every day, you see when it changes. A new fence. A tree down after a storm. A different car in the same drive.

And the people. There is a man who plays in an orchestra, I think. Or a band. He carries an instrument case every day. Same seat, same case, for years. Old and scuffed, with a sticker on the side. Then one Monday he got on with a new one. Black and hard and clean. I noticed it at once. I felt, for a moment, that something had happened. I will never know what. I did not ask. We do not talk.

But I noticed. The train taught me that.
