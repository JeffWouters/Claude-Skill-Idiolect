There comes a point in many engineering careers when the technology one has mastered is quietly superseded, and a person who has spent years being the one others consult finds themselves, once again, a beginner. The experience is rarely discussed in these terms. We tend instead to talk about resistance to change, about engineers who dismiss a new framework or cling to familiar tooling, as though the objection were purely technical. Sometimes it is. More often, I suspect, the pushback conceals something less comfortable to name: the discomfort of feeling unskilled after a long period of feeling competent, and the fear that colleagues will notice. Criticising a tool is considerably easier than admitting that one does not yet understand it.

Organisations do not make this easier. Almost every workplace speaks warmly about professional development, and many publish statements about continuous learning and growth. Yet the calendar tells a different story. The time in which learning is supposed to happen is seldom set aside; it is assumed to occur somewhere between deliverables, in the evenings, or in the brief lulls that appear between one urgent task and the next. The message received by the experienced engineer is therefore a mixed one: learn the new thing, certainly, but do not let it slow anything down.

The consequence is a particular kind of knowledge — acquired in fragments, never quite consolidated, adequate for the task at hand but liable to give way under pressure. Learning squeezed into the gaps tends to be shallow, because depth requires sustained attention, and fragile, because what has not been properly understood cannot easily be adapted when circumstances change. It would be worth asking whether an organisation that wants its senior people to master new technology is prepared to accept the temporary loss of productivity that genuine learning demands. If it is not, it should perhaps be less surprised when the learning does not take.
