If anyone doubts that our educational standards have fallen, let him consider what has become of Greek. It was not so long ago that no one could call himself an educated man without it. It has now been dropped--quietly, and almost without protest--from school after school and college after college. And the reasons given for dropping it are the most instructive thing about the whole affair. We are told that Greek was too difficult; we are told that it was not practical. Those reasons seem to me to be exactly the reasons that culture has never recognised. Nothing worth learning was ever easy, and nothing worth learning was ever "practical" in the sense that the word is used by the people who use it most. To throw away Greek because it is hard is to confess that we no longer believe in hard things.

Nor is it only Greek. Anyone who has taught in a college can tell you what kind of student now arrives at the door. He has read very little--frankly, in many cases, almost nothing that he was not compelled to read. He cannot spell with any certainty. He cannot write a paragraph that is clear, or a sentence that is correct. [example needed] It is not his fault, altogether; he has simply never been asked to do these things, and he is quite honestly surprised that anyone should ask him now.

Who is to blame? A good many people, I think, and a good many things. There are the fads--the endless succession of new methods, each one warranted to make learning painless, and each one making it a little more so, and a little less like learning. There is the general laxity of discipline, which will not insist, because insisting is unpleasant. There are the public schools, so crowded that the best-intentioned teacher can hardly do more than keep order. Let us admit that the crowding is nobody's wish. It is none the less a fact, and a very damaging one.

And last, and perhaps worst of all, there is the teaching profession itself. It no longer draws, as it once drew, the ablest men and women of each generation. The capable young person looks at the salary, and the prestige, and the conditions, and goes elsewhere; and who shall blame him? But the result is that the schools are more and more in the hands of people who were themselves badly taught. The bad pupil of yesterday is the teacher of to-day; and the circle, once begun, is very hard to break.
