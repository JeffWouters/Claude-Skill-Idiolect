There are two kinds of lying which are seldom called by that name, and which seem to me worth a moment's attention. The first is exaggeration; the second is the deception that keeps carefully within the letter of the truth. They are commonly lumped together, as though they were equally venial or equally wicked. I think they are nothing of the sort.

Take the born story-teller. He cannot recount the smallest adventure without improving it. The dog that barked at him becomes a dog that very nearly had him by the throat; the dull dinner becomes a dinner of positively heroic dullness. [example needed] He is, in the strict sense, a liar; he says a great many things that did not happen. And yet, oddly enough, his hearers very often come away with a truer notion of the occasion than they would have had from a sober witness. He has heightened the colours, but he has not changed the picture. The dinner really was dull; the dog really was alarming. What he conveys, in the end, is accurate--more accurate, perhaps, than the bare record.

For honesty, after all, is a matter of meaning, not merely of words. We do not tell the truth by stringing together sentences each of which can be verified; we tell the truth by leaving in the mind of the listener an impression that corresponds to the facts. That is the whole test. A man may use nothing but true statements and yet be a thorough liar; and a man may use a good many false ones and yet, in the larger sense, be honest.

Which brings me to the second kind--and I confess I have much less patience with it. There are people (it is always the most scrupulous-looking people!) who would not, for the world, utter a downright falsehood. They never say the thing that is not. But they arrange the things that are so cunningly that you come away believing something entirely false; and they know, perfectly well, that you will. They pride themselves on their truthfulness; I cannot see why. They have kept the letter and broken the spirit, which is precisely the opposite of what the story-teller does; and of the two, I would a great deal rather dine with the story-teller. Such people have, I think, a very poor understanding of what truth is. They have mistaken it for a form of words--and truth was never that.
