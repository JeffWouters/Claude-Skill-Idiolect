The saga tells the story with a directness that leaves little to the imagination. The king [name needed] came among the farmers who still clung to the old gods. He did not argue with them. He went into the place where their idols stood and threw them down, and while the farmers were considering this affront, his men struck down the chief who had been their spokesman. [example needed]

The farmers were now in an awkward position. They had come prepared to defend their gods, but it is hard to defend anything without someone to tell you how. The man who had been accustomed to speak for them lay dead, and there was no one ready to take his place. The king, on his part, was quite willing to make the choice easy. They might be baptized, or they might have war. They chose baptism, and the saga records it without any sign of surprise.

It is not a very edifying story, if we look for edification of the ordinary kind. There is no account of a missionary reasoning with the heathen, no struggle of a soul between two faiths, no moment of illumination. The farmers were not convinced; they were overpowered. What passed for conversion was in reality a change of allegiance, such as might take place between one chieftain and another.

And yet we should misread the North if we were too much scandalized. The men of that time had a very simple test of the worth of a god. A god was to be judged by what he could do for his followers. If the old gods could not keep their images standing, it was a fair inference that they were not so strong as had been supposed. The new faith came to them, not as a body of doctrine to be examined, but as a display of power to be acknowledged. It won its way in the North by the same argument that had always prevailed there, which was the argument of the stronger arm.
