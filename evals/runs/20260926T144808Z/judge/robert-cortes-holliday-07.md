# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-07

## The passage (by the author)

Happening, by our close proximity, to turn to us the first time in the shop that he required attention, upon each succeeding visit he sought out us to attend to his wishes. The position of retail salesman "on the floor" is one completely exposed to every human attitude and humour. Against arrogance, against contempt of himself as a shop person, a species of "counter-jumper," against irascibility, against bigoted ignorance, against an indissoluble assumption, perhaps logical, that he is of inferior mentality, this factotum has no defence. His very business is to meet all with amenity. It is his daily portion, included in the material with which he works.

It (he finds) injures him not, essentially; it ceases to particularly affect him, beyond his inward appraisement of the character before him. Toward him one acts simply in accordance with the instincts of one's nature. His status counsels no constraint, invites no display, has no property of stimulation. Thus the view of a famous man's character from the position of retail clerk is valuable. Mr. James's manner with Mr. Brownell would hardly be the same as toward us. But it was, exactly. There was present in his mind at the moment, was quite apparent, absolutely no consciousness of any distance of mind, or position, between him and us. He sought conversation (any suggestion of so equalising a thing as conversation with a clerk is not uncommonly repressed by the important as preposterous). In his own talk with us, he seemed to us to be a man consciously striving with the material of words and sentences to express his thought as well as he could.

He was very earnest. He looked up at us constantly (we are a little tall) with fixed concentration of gaze, and moved his hand to and fro as though seeking to balance his ideas. He asked questions with deference. Among other things, he desired very much to know what per cent. of the novels on the fiction table was the product of writers in England. "I live in England myself," he said, very simply, "and I am curious to know this." He expressed a little impatience at the measureless flood of mediocre fiction, making a fluttering gesture conveying a sense of impotence to give it attention. He barely glanced at the pile of his own book, and did not mention it. He did not seem at first (though we believe later he changed this opinion) to think highly of Arnold Bennett (this was at the first bloom of Mr. Bennett's vogue here), nor to have read him. "Oh, yes, yes; he is an English journalist," in a tone as though, merely a journalist. Clear artist in fibre. When he took his departure he bade us "Good day," and lifted his hat.

## Draft A

A salesman in a shop learns a good deal about people that they would never tell him, and he learns it without asking. He has to. His job is to stand behind the counter and take whatever the customer brings in with him, whether that is a good mood or a bad one, a hurry, a grievance, a headache, or the lingering irritation of some quarrel at home. He cannot answer back and he cannot walk away. He simply absorbs it, and serves the next one.

The result is that he sees people with their guard down. Nobody puts on a performance for a shop assistant. They do not need his good opinion, so they do not trouble to earn it, and what they show him is closer to what they are than anything they would show a friend or a colleague. A man who is gracious in a drawing room may be curt at a counter. A man who is shy in company may be unexpectedly kind to someone he will never see again. After a few years of this you come to trust the counter as a test of character more than almost anything else.

It is by that test that I remember the novelist. He was, by then, very famous, and he had every reason to treat a young assistant as part of the furniture. He did not. He spoke to me as an equal, and not in the careful, slightly performed way that some well-known people adopt when they want to be thought democratic. He was simply earnest. He asked questions because he wanted to know the answers, and he listened to what I said as though it might be worth hearing. His courtesy was not a manner laid on for the occasion. It seemed to go all the way through.

One of the things he wanted to know was how much of our stock was made up of English novels. He asked it seriously, the way a man might ask about the state of a trade he had some stake in, and I did my best to give him an honest estimate. [figure needed] He considered it for a moment and seemed to find it interesting rather than flattering or disappointing. He was curious about how books actually moved, who bought them, and what stayed on the shelves.

At some point the talk turned to another writer, a rival of sorts, whose name was often mentioned alongside his own. [name needed] I expected a compliment, or perhaps a careful silence. Instead he dismissed the man in a word: a journalist. He did not elaborate, and he did not sound bitter. He said it as though he were classifying a specimen, and then he went back to the books.

I have thought about that conversation many times since. It was not the remark about the rival that stayed with me, sharp as it was. It was the fact that he had talked to me at all, and talked to me as though I counted.

## Draft B

A salesman in a store is, in a certain sense, a public servant without the protection of a uniform. Whatever mood a customer brings in with him from the street, the salesman must receive. If the customer is irritable, the salesman is the thing he is irritable at. If he is hurried, the salesman is in his way; if he is bored, the salesman is expected to amuse him; if he is in a condescending humour, somebody must be condescended to, and there the salesman stands, conveniently placed behind his counter, for the purpose. It is not a position of dignity. But it is, I have come to think, a position of remarkable advantage for the study of human nature, for the manner in which a man treats someone who cannot answer back tells you more about him than a volume of his published opinions. You see people, so to say, with the company manners off.

The novelist of whom I speak was a man I had long admired from a distance. What I had not expected, when at last he came to my counter, was the manner of his address. He spoke to me as an equal. I do not mean that he was familiar, or hearty, or that he made any display of democratic sentiment; he did nothing of the kind. He simply talked to me as one man talks to another about a matter in which both are interested. He was earnest. He was courteous, with a courtesy that did not seem to have been put on for the occasion, to be taken off again at the door. He listened to what I said, and he considered it, and he answered it.

His questions were those of a man who wished to know. He asked what proportion of our stock of fiction was made up of English novels. [number needed: the share given] He appeared interested in the answer, and turned it over in his mind for a moment, as though it bore upon some larger question he had been pondering. Then the talk moved to another writer, a contemporary of some reputation, whose books we carried in quantity. I mentioned him, I think, with a certain respect.

"Oh," said the novelist, without any heat at all, "he's a journalist."

That was all. He did not elaborate. He did not need to; the word, as he pronounced it, contained the whole of his verdict, delivered mildly, and without appeal. Then he thanked me and went out.

## Draft C

A salesman in a shop is a kind of sponge. He must take up whatever is spilt upon him. Customers come to him in every temper under the sun: in a hurry, in a sulk, in a fine good humour, in the aftermath of a quarrel at breakfast, in a state of grievance against the world at large and the retail trade in particular. He must absorb all of it and give out, in return, nothing but civility. It is not an education I would recommend. But it has this to be said for it: it shows you people as they are. Nobody troubles to put on his character for a clerk. A man's manner to the person across the counter is, I have come to believe, about as true a test of him as you will find.

I am led to this by the recollection of [novelist].

He did not, in the first place, speak to me as to a clerk. He spoke to me as to another man, who happened for the moment to be standing on the other side of a table of books. He was earnest. He was courteous, with a courtesy that had nothing condescending in it, nothing of the great person being gracious to the small one. He asked questions, and he waited for the answers, and he listened to them, which is rarer. When I said something he had not thought of, he considered it, gravely, and said so.

What he wished to know, on one occasion, was how the English novel fared among us. What proportion of our stock, he asked, was made up of English novels, as against the others? How many did we carry? Did they sell? To whom? He put these questions with great care, turning each one over, qualifying it, going back to it, as though it were a matter of real consequence, as I dare say to him it was. I gave him such answers as I could, which were not, I fear, very exact. He received them as though they had been.

In the course of this talk a name came up; the name of another novelist, a writer then very much read, and very much talked of, whose books we sold in great numbers. [rival novelist]. I mentioned him, I suppose, as an instance of what did sell. The great man paused. A shade passed over his face, not of anger, but of something like weariness. "Ah," he said, "but he is a journalist." And that was all. He did not say it unkindly. He said it as one states a classification, as a naturalist might say that a certain creature, however it may look, is not really a bird.

Then he thanked me, at some length, and went.
