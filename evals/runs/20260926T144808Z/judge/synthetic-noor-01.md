# Judge packet synthetic-noor-01

## The passage (by the author)

The kitchen tap started to drip in March. One drop every four seconds. I timed it with the clock on the oven.

At first I did nothing. A drip is easy to live with. You stop hearing it after a day or two.

But then I put a jug under it. By morning the jug held 600 millilitres. So that was the end of living with it.

I have fixed taps before. Not this one. This one is old, with a brass body and two cross-shaped handles. Hot on the left. Cold on the right. The drip came from the cold side.

So on Saturday I cleared the counter. I laid a tea towel in the sink. That is the first rule: cover the plughole. Small parts fall. They always fall.

Then I turned off the water. There is a stop valve under the sink, behind the bleach and a bag of old sponges. It had not been turned in years. It took both hands and a cloth for grip. Then it gave.

I opened the tap to drain it. A cough of air. A thin run of water. Then nothing.

The handle came off with one screw under a small blue cap. I lifted the cap with the tip of a knife. The screw was a flat head, soft with age. I used the right driver and pushed hard as I turned. It came out whole.

Under the handle sat the headgear. That is the brass part that holds the washer. It takes a spanner. Mine is an adjustable one, 20 centimetres long, from my father's box. I set it snug on the flats and held the tap body with my other hand. You have to hold the body. Or the whole tap turns in the counter and you make a new problem.

## Draft A

The old kitchen faucet had been dripping for a while before I decided to do something about it. It is easy to ignore a slow leak, because one drop at a time does not look like much. But a slow leak wastes far more water than you would expect, and once I started thinking about how much was going down the drain every hour, I could not leave it alone any longer. It had to be dealt with.

Before touching a single screw, I spent my time on preparation. The first job was to isolate the supply. There is no point starting to dismantle a faucet while water can still reach it, because the moment you loosen the wrong part you will have a fountain on your hands and a wet floor to show for it. I shut off the supply, then opened the faucet to let the remaining pressure and water run out, so I could be sure nothing was still feeding it.

The second job was to protect the drain. Faucets are full of small parts, and small parts have a habit of slipping out of your fingers at exactly the wrong moment. I covered the drain opening so that anything I dropped would stay in the sink rather than disappear into the pipework. I also laid out the parts in order as they came off, so reassembly would not turn into guesswork. [example needed: what was used to cover the drain]

Only then did I start taking things apart. The key thing I had to keep in mind was to steady the faucet body while loosening the internal brass assembly. Old fittings can be stiff, and if you put force on the brass without holding the body firmly, you risk twisting the whole faucet, straining the connections underneath or damaging the sink around it. So one hand held the body still while the other worked the assembly loose, slowly and without forcing it.

## Draft B

The kitchen tap had dripped for months. A slow drip. One drop, a pause, another drop. I told myself it was nothing. It was not nothing. A slow leak wastes more water than you would guess. I put a jug under it one night to see. By morning it held [amount needed]. That settled it. The tap had to be fixed.

It is an old tap. Older than the kitchen, I think. The body is heavy and the chrome has worn thin near the handle. Inside there is a brass assembly that holds the seal. That is the part that fails. Getting to it is the job.

Turn the water off first. Not at the main if you can avoid it. Under the sink there should be a small isolating valve on the supply pipe. Mine is behind the bin and the bleach. I cleared them out. I turned the valve with a screwdriver until the slot sat across the pipe. Then I opened the tap and let it run dry. A little water came out. Then nothing. Good.

Then the drain. Put the plug in. Lay a cloth over it as well. Small parts go down plugholes. They go down fast and they do not come back. I have lost [example needed] that way before. I do not intend to lose another.

Now the hard part. The brass assembly is tight. It has been tight for years. You need a spanner on it, and you need to turn it hard. But the tap body will want to turn with it. If it does, you strain the pipes below and the joint at the sink. So hold the body still. One hand on the tap, firm. One hand on the spanner. Pull steadily. Do not jerk it. It will give. It gives all at once, with a small crack, and then it spins free.

That is the preparation. The repair itself comes next.

## Draft C

The kitchen tap leaks. Not much. A slow drip from the spout, day and night.

I told myself it could wait. It could not. A slow leak wastes more water than you think. [number needed, how much it wastes in a day]. That was enough. So the tap had to be dealt with.

Here is the order I work in.

First the water. There is a small valve under the sink on each supply pipe. I turned both off. Then I opened the tap to let the last of the water run out. It gave a cough and a trickle. Then nothing. That is how you know the supply is shut.

Then the drain. This is the step that is easy to skip. A small part dropped in a sink goes straight down the plug hole. So I put the plug in. And I lay an old towel over the bottom of the sink. A dropped screw lands soft, and it stays put.

Then the tools. A spanner, a screwdriver, a cloth to wrap the chrome. I lay them out on the draining board in the order I will need them.

The tap is old. The body is loose on the worktop. It turns if you lean on it. That matters. The brass part inside is tight after years of use. If the whole body turns with the spanner, you twist the pipes below. Not good.

So I hold the body with one hand. Firmly. With the other I take the spanner to the brass nut. Slow pressure, not a jerk. It does not move. Then it does. A small turn. Then it spins free.

I have not fixed anything yet. I have only got ready. But the ready part is most of the job.

Tonight the tap is in pieces on the towel. The spout is dry.
