# Judge packet synthetic-noor-02

## The passage (by the author)

I take the 7.42 train. I have taken it for six years.

The walk to the station is 14 minutes. I have timed it. With a heavy bag it is 16. In rain it is 12, because I walk faster and take the short cut past the bins.

So I leave the house at 7.25. That gives me three minutes of slack. Three minutes is the right amount. Less and I run. More and I stand on the platform in the cold.

The platform has a shape I know by heart. The front two carriages stop by the yellow bench. The middle stop by the clock. The back ones stop past the end of the roof, where you get wet. I stand by the clock. The doors open right in front of me. Most days.

There is a crowd of regulars. We do not speak. But we know each other.

There is the man in the green coat who reads the paper folded in four. There is the woman with two phones, one in each hand. There is a boy with a cello case taller than he is. He gets off after two stops. I have never heard him play. But I think about the cello most mornings.

The train is late one day in five. I have kept count, on and off. Mostly by two or three minutes. Once by 40. That was the day of the fallen tree. We stood on the platform and watched the board change. Delayed. Delayed. Then a new time. Then delayed again.

And the odd thing about that day: nobody left. Nobody went for a bus. We just stood there. A few people rang work. The man in the green coat read his whole paper, both sides.

I have a seat I like. Left side, facing forward, by the window. The left side gets the sun in winter. In summer I move to the right.

## Draft A

My morning commute by rail is a routine I have kept for a long time, long enough that most of it now runs without much thought. It starts before I even leave the house. I time my departure carefully, not to the last second, but with a small buffer built in. Enough to absorb a slow traffic light or a missing key, not so much that I end up standing on the platform with nothing to do. Getting that margin right took some trial and error, and now I rarely think about it. I just leave when it is time to leave.

On the platform and on the train, there are faces I know well. We have shared the same carriage for years, often the same end of it, and yet we never talk. Not a word. There is a kind of silent familiarity in that. I know who will be standing near the doors and who will take the seat by the window. I notice when someone is missing, and I suspect they notice when I am. We nod sometimes, or we do not. Nobody seems to feel the need for more, and there is something comfortable about that unspoken arrangement. It is company without obligation.

Only once did that routine really get tested. There was a major delay one morning. [example needed: cause and length of the delay] The train stopped and simply stayed where it was, and for a long time there was no sign of it moving again. I half expected people to give up, to get off, make phone calls, find another way in. That is not what happened. Everyone waited. Patiently, without complaint, the same quiet as every other morning. Nobody left.

I still think about that sometimes. It said something about the people who make this journey every day. Perhaps when you have built your mornings around a routine, you learn to trust it, even on the day it lets you down. Or perhaps we simply knew that waiting together was easier than going it alone.

## Draft B

I leave the house at the same time every morning. Not roughly the same time. The same time. I have worked it out over years. Enough to walk to the station without hurrying. Enough to stand on the platform for a minute or two before the train comes in. No more than that. A long wait on a cold platform is wasted. A short one is a cushion.

The buffer matters. Once I left it too thin. A shoelace, a lost key, I forget which. I ran the last stretch and caught the train with the doors closing. I sat down with my heart going and my shirt damp. I did not enjoy the journey. So now I keep the buffer. Small, but there.

The same people get on with me. I know them all by sight. The man with the folded paper. The woman who always stands though there are seats. The student with the headphones and the big bag. [example needed] We have travelled together for years. We have never spoken. Not once. We nod sometimes. More often we do not even do that. It is not unfriendly. It is just how it is. I would find it strange now if one of them said good morning.

Then one morning the train stopped. Not at a station. In the middle of nowhere, between fields. The driver said there was a problem ahead. Then he said nothing for a long time. We sat. The heating ticked. Someone's phone buzzed and was ignored.

I expected people to give up. To ring for lifts. To get off at the next stop, when we reached it, and find another way. Nobody did. We all stayed. We waited, quietly, for [duration needed], the way we wait on the platform every day. Nobody complained out loud. When the train moved again there was no cheer. Just a small shift in the carriage. Papers opened. Heads went back down.

I got to work late. I did not mind much.

## Draft C

I take the same train every morning. I have done so for years. [number needed, how many years].

Here is the routine.

First the clock. I leave the house at a set time. Not the last possible minute. A few minutes before it. [number needed, the size of the gap]. That small gap is the whole point. It covers a lost key, a slow road, a queue at the ticket machine. Without it I run. With it I walk. I do not like to run for a train.

Then the platform. I stand in the same spot each day. So do the others. There is a group of us. I do not know their names. They do not know mine. We have never spoken. Not once.

And yet I know them. Their coats, their bags, the way each of them waits. [example needed, one of the regulars and a detail about them]. If one of them is missing, I notice. I think they notice too. Nobody says so. That is the rule.

So we stand, and we board, and we sit in much the same seats. It is a quiet sort of company. It asks for nothing.

Once, the train was badly late. [example needed, the cause and how long it lasted]. The board kept changing. Then it went blank. I thought people would give up. Leave and find a bus or a taxi. That is what I would have guessed.

They did not. Nobody left. We all stood there and waited. No fuss. No sighs worth hearing. Just the same silent group on the platform. When the train came at last, we got on as we always do.

I still think about that morning. Not the delay. The patience.

This morning I left at my usual time. The others were already there. Nobody spoke. We all looked down the track.
