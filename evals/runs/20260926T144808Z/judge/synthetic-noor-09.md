# Judge packet synthetic-noor-09

## The passage (by the author)

There is a hiss. Air coming out. It smells of metal and old water. The hiss can go on for a while. On the back bedroom one it went on for almost a minute.

And then the sound changes. The hiss goes wet. A spit. Then a bead of water. That is the moment. Turn the key back, clockwise, snug. Not tight. You can strip the little valve if you force it.

Wipe the water. Check the cloth. Move on.

Eight of the nine had some air. The one in the hall had none. It never does. I do not know why. It is the lowest one in the house and the closest to the boiler. Something to do with that.

When they are all done, I go to the boiler. There is a pressure gauge on the front. A round dial with a needle. It should sit between 1 and 1.5 bar when cold. After bleeding it drops, because you let water out. This year it was down to 0.6.

So I top it up. Under the boiler there is a short silver hose with two taps. The filling loop. I open both taps, slowly, and watch the needle climb. At 1.2 I close them. The pipes groan while the new water finds its way round.

Then the heating goes back on.

I walk round the house and feel each radiator again. Top to bottom. Hot all over. The back bedroom one too. It is the best part of the job. You put your hand flat on the metal and the heat is even from edge to edge.

That night the house was quiet. No knocks. No tick. Just the low hum of the pump in the cupboard and the click as the thermostat turned it off.

I will do the same next October. The air comes back. It always does. Small bubbles, a little at a time, over the year.

## Draft A

With the bleed valve open, the air comes out first, with a steady hiss. That part is easy to recognise. What matters is paying attention to what comes after it. Once the hissing stops and water appears at the valve, the air is out, and it is time to close the valve again. I close it as soon as I see water, not a moment later, and I keep a cloth ready to catch the drips. There is no benefit in letting water run once the air has gone. Then I move on to the next radiator and repeat the same thing, working my way through the house.

Releasing air from the system also lowers the pressure. Every bit of air that escapes leaves a gap, and after going through all the radiators the pressure in the system will have dropped. So the job is not finished when the last valve is closed. The next step is to restore the boiler pressure.

That is done by refilling the system through the boiler's filling hose. I open it carefully and let water in slowly while keeping an eye on the pressure gauge. The aim is to bring it up to the right reading, not beyond it. It would be nice if that were the end of it for good, but it is not. Air accumulates in the system again over time. It works its way in slowly, collects at the top of the radiators, and eventually the pipes start making noise again. So this is a task that repeats annually. Every autumn, when the heating comes back on, I will be doing it again.

## Draft C

The valve is open and hissing. Wait. Keep the cloth under it. The hiss goes on for a while, then it changes. It sputters. A few spits of water. Then a steady dribble. That is the sign. The air is out and water has reached the valve.

Close it straight away. Turn the key back until it is snug. Do not force it. The little screw is soft and the seat is easy to damage. Snug is enough. Wipe the valve dry and look at it for a moment. If it still weeps, give it a touch more.

Then the next radiator. And the next. Up the stairs. Same thing each time. Open slowly, let it hiss, wait for water, close.

Now the part people forget. Every hiss was air leaving the system. Every dribble was water leaving too. It does not look like much. But it adds up, and the boiler notices. Go and look at the pressure gauge on the front of it. Mine had dropped. Below where it should be. [reading needed]

So you top it up. Under the boiler there is a filling hose. A short braided loop with a small tap at each end. On mine the taps are stiff. Open both, slowly. You hear water running in. Watch the gauge. The needle climbs. Let it climb to the right reading, then shut both taps. Shut them fully. Do not guess at the reading. Check the boiler's manual, or the label on the front. Too high is as bad as too low.

Then switch the system back on. Let it run. Go round again with your hand. Warm at the top now. Warm all over. The knocking has stopped. The house is quiet.

It will not last. Air gets back in. It always does. A little with every heating season, through the joints and out of the water itself. By next autumn the pipes will be talking again. So the key goes back in the drawer. I know where it is now.
