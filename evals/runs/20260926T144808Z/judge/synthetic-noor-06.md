# Judge packet synthetic-noor-06

## The passage (by the author)

It came loose on the third try.

And there was the washer. Black rubber, once. Now it was grey and hard and split on one edge like a dry lip. No wonder. It could not seal against anything.

I took it to the shop on the corner. The man there has a drawer of washers sorted by size. He held mine up to the light. Then he picked one out without looking. Half inch. 30 cents. I bought three.

At home the new washer went on with a small nut. I tightened it by hand, then a quarter turn with pliers. Not more. Rubber needs room to press.

Then back in with the headgear. Snug, then a little more. I wiped the threads first. Grit in the threads is how you strip them.

Handle on. Screw in. Blue cap pressed home with my thumb.

So then the valve under the sink. I turned it slowly. You want the pipes to fill without a bang. There was a hiss. Then a knock somewhere in the wall. Then quiet.

I opened the cold tap. It spat twice and ran clean. I closed it.

And I waited.

I stood at the sink and watched the spout for a full minute. Nothing. I went and made a coffee and came back. Nothing.

The whole job took 40 minutes. Most of it was the stop valve and the walk to the shop. The actual work was ten minutes. That is how it goes with small repairs. The work is short. The getting ready is long.

I put the two spare washers in a jar marked TAP. The jar lives in the drawer by the back door. It holds the old one too. I keep the old one so I remember the size.

That night the kitchen was very quiet. I noticed it when I switched off the light. No tick. No small wet sound in the dark.

Then the brass part back into the body. I held the body still with one hand, as before. I turned the brass in by hand until it stopped. Then the spanner. Firm. Not hard. Turn it too far and you squash the new seal. So I stopped as soon as it felt snug.

Then the handle. Then the cap. Then I took the towel out of the sink.

Then the water. I did not open the valves all at once. A small turn first. I watched the pipes below. Dry. Then a little more. Still dry. Then all the way. The tap coughed out some air and then ran clean. I shut it off. I watched the spout. Nothing. I watched it longer. Still nothing.

The fix itself took minutes. [number needed, how long the fix took]. The getting ready took far longer. Water off, sink shut, tools out, tap apart. That is always the way. The work is quick. The care around it is slow.

The spare went in a small tin. The tin went in the drawer under the sink. Next time I will not need the shop.

This morning I turned the tap on and off again. The spout stayed dry.

## Draft B

The old seal came out in pieces. It had gone hard and flat and cracked across. No wonder the tap dripped. I put the bits in a bag and took them to the hardware shop down the road.

The man behind the counter looked at them for a second. Then he went to a drawer and came back with a new one. Same size. He did not need to measure. It cost [price needed]. I bought [number needed]. I will come back to that.

Fitting it was quick. The new seal pressed onto the end of the brass assembly. It went on with a small push. I checked it sat flat. Then the assembly went back into the tap body. By hand first, a few turns, to make sure the thread had caught. A crossed thread in old brass is a disaster. Then the spanner. Hold the tap body still, the same as coming apart. Tighten it firmly. Not as hard as you can. Firmly.

Then the handle back on. Then the little cap on top.

Now the water. Do not just open the valve. Open it a little. Listen. There is a hiss and a knock as the pipe fills. Then a quiet. Look at the tap and at the joint below. Nothing. Open it a little more. Look again. Then all the way. Open the tap. It coughs, spits some air, then runs clean. Close it. Watch the spout. Count slowly. Then count again.

No drip.

That is the thing about this job. The fixing took [time needed]. The getting ready took most of an afternoon. Finding the valve. Clearing the cupboard. Loosening the stuck assembly without twisting the pipes. Finding the right part. The part that looks like the repair is the smallest part.

The spare seals are in a jar in the cupboard under the sink. Labelled. Next time the tap drips, and it will, I will not need the shop. I will not need to wonder what size. I will just need the afternoon.

## Draft C

Once the faucet was apart, the cause of the leak was obvious. The seal inside had worn out. It was the one part doing the work of keeping the water where it belonged, and after years of use it simply could not do that job any more.

The fix itself was not complicated. I took the worn seal with me to a local hardware seller to make sure I came back with the right one. The replacement was cheap, to the point that it felt slightly absurd how much water had gone down the drain because of such a small, inexpensive part. [example needed: price of the seal] While I was there, I bought a few spares as well.

Back home, swapping the seal took very little time. Out with the old one, in with the new one, making sure it sat properly and evenly in its place. Then came the reassembly, in the reverse order of taking it apart. Because I had laid out the parts in sequence as they came off, putting them back was straightforward. I steadied the faucet body again while tightening the internal assembly, firm enough to seal but without forcing anything.

Restoring the supply is a step I do not rush. Rather than opening it fully in one go, I brought it back gradually, watching the faucet and the connections underneath for any sign of water where it should not be. Opening it slowly gives you the chance to catch a problem before it becomes a flood. Everything stayed dry. I opened the faucet, let it run, closed it, and watched. No drip.

The thing that struck me most was the balance of time. Preparation takes far longer than the hands-on fix. Isolating the supply, protecting the drain, laying out the parts, getting the right seal: that is where the hours went. The actual swap was over in minutes.

The spares are now stored away for later, alongside the tools I used. When the seal wears out again, as it eventually will, the next repair should be even quicker.
