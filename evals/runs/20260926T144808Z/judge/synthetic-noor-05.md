## The passage (by the author)

And then the weft. This is the part I like. I go across, side to side, over one line and under the next. Over, under, over, under. At the end of each row I turn and come back. Under where I went over. Over where I went under.

Slowly a small square of cloth grows over the hole. A woven patch. Firm and flat.

The rule is to keep it loose. Not slack. Loose. If you pull tight, the patch puckers. Then it rubs a blister on your heel. I learned that on darn number two.

A patch the size of a coin takes me 30 minutes. A big heel hole takes an hour. I do it in the evening, in the chair by the lamp. The radio on. A cup of tea going cold on the arm.

Some people buy new socks. I know. Socks are cheap. A new pair costs less than an hour of my time, if you count it that way.

But I do not count it that way. The hour is not work. It is a quiet hour with my hands busy. And the sock is better after. A darned heel is thicker than the old heel. It lasts longer than the wool around it. So the next hole is always somewhere new.

My grandfather darned his own socks. He was a fisherman. He could darn in the dark by feel. He showed me once, when I was small. I did not pay much attention. Years later I found his mushroom in a box. Dark wood, worn smooth. I tried to use it. I could not remember how. So I taught myself from a book. The bulb works better than the mushroom for me. I kept the mushroom anyway.

These grey socks will not last for ever. The wool at the ankle is getting thin now, all the way round. That is hard to darn. When that goes, they go.

## Draft A

The lengthwise threads are done. Now the other way. Crosswise.

This is the weaving. The needle goes over one thread and under the next, all the way across. Then back again, under where it went over, over where it went under. Row by row the gap closes. It starts as a ladder. Then it looks like a grid. Then it looks like cloth.

Watch the tension. That is the thing to get right. Pull too tight and the patch puckers. It draws the sock in around it. It looks neat enough on the bulb. Then you put the sock on and there is a hard ridge under your heel. By the end of the day you feel it with every step. So keep the yarn loose. Looser than feels tidy. Let each row just sit against the last. The wool will settle when it is washed.

When the gap is filled I run the end back through the weave and snip it. Then I turn the sock the right way out and look. It is never invisible. It is not meant to be.

I do not think of it as work. That surprised me. I expected it to be a chore, like ironing. It is not. It is quiet. The hands know what to do after the first few rows and the head goes somewhere else. I sit by the window with it. [example needed] At the end I feel rested, not tired. I cannot say that about much.

## Draft B

The last part of mending a sock is the weaving. With the lengthwise threads in place, I work crosswise, taking the needle over one thread and under the next, then back again on the following row, the other way around. Row by row, a patch builds up where the hole used to be. It is slow, and it is meant to be slow. Each pass needs to sit close to the one before, but not so close that it bunches.

The thing I have had to learn is tension. It is tempting to pull every row nice and tight, because a tight patch looks neat and feels solid. But excessive tension causes discomfort. The patch puckers, the surrounding fabric draws in, and when you wear the sock you feel a hard ridge under your foot. A good darn lies flat and gives a little, the way the original knit did. So I keep the yarn relaxed and check the surface every few rows with my thumb.

What surprises people when I tell them I mend my socks is that I do not experience it as a chore. The time spent feels restful rather than like labour. There is nothing to rush and nothing to decide beyond the next stitch. My hands are busy and my head gets quiet. When I finish, I have a sock that works again, and I also have had a stretch of time that was calm in a way not much else in my week is.

There is a family side to this as well. My grandparent mended.
