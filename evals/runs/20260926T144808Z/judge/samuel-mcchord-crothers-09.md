## The passage (by the author)

Certainly not. To a mind constituted as was his there was an absurdity in the very suggestion. The brute creation should not have any monopoly of comfortable sensations, so he cheered up immediately and spent the next year loafing around his father’s house.

He had been on the coast of Africa and had taken part in some strange scenes, but his moral sense had not been blunted to such an extent that he could not grieve over some infractions of the moral law which he observed in peaceful Hanover. He regretted that he had been led inadvertently by a young man named Huntington to join a party which robbed a farmer’s beehive.

“For some unaccountable reason or other, youth are carried away with false notions of right and wrong. I know, for instance, that Huntington possessed those principles of integrity that no consideration would have induced him to deprive another of any species of property, except fruit, bees, pigs, and poultry. And why it is considered by youth that depriving another of these articles is less criminal than stealing any other kind of property, I cannot tell.”

Burroughs himself was inclined to take a harsher view of these transgressions than he did of some others; for example, of counterfeiting, in which he was afterwards for a time engaged during one of his brief pastorates.

The argument by which his scruples in this particular were overcome are worth repeating. The law was indeed violated in its letter, but might not a justification be found by one who interpreted it in a large spirit of charity?

“Money is of itself of consequence only as we annex to it a nominal value as the representation of property. Therefore we find the only thing necessary to make a matter valuable is to induce the world to deem it so; and let that esteem be raised by any means whatever, yet the value is the same, and no one becomes injured by receiving it at the valuation.”

## Draft A

The impostor left a memoir, and a good part of it is taken up with explaining why the small crimes of his youth were not really crimes at all.

He had been to sea. [details of the voyages needed] When he came home he did nothing in particular. He idled, drifting about his home district without work or plans. Yet the idleness did not stop him from passing judgement. He watched the misdeeds of the people around him and criticised them freely, apparently without noticing that his own conduct gave him little standing to do so.

His views on theft are revealing. He wondered, he says, why the young men of the neighbourhood thought it harmless to raid a beehive or strip an orchard. They did it as a matter of course, as a kind of sport, and nobody treated them as thieves for it. He seems to have found this puzzling. But the puzzle cuts both ways. If taking honey or apples was harmless, then the line between that and other kinds of taking was not as firm as the neighbours pretended. It is hard to tell whether he meant this as a criticism of them or as an excuse for himself. Most likely it was both.

His argument about false money goes further. He maintained that fake coins hurt nobody. Money, he reasoned, has no worth of its own. Its value exists only because people believe in it. A coin that passes from hand to hand, accepted by everyone who takes it, does exactly the same work as a genuine one. So long as the belief holds, nobody is worse off. The counterfeit, on this view, is not a fraud but simply one more coin in a system that runs entirely on trust.

It is a clever argument, and like many clever arguments made by people with something to excuse, it stops just short of the point where it would hurt. It says nothing about what happens to the last person holding the coin when the belief fails. [example needed]

Reading the memoir, one has the sense of a man who could always find a reason. The reasons were never quite good enough, but they were always ready.

## Draft B

The memoir of an impostor is seldom dull, for the author has had a good deal of practice in making himself interesting. The one I have in mind [title needed] is especially instructive on the subject of excuses.

After several voyages at sea our author returned home and settled down to a life of idleness. This did not prevent him from taking a lively interest in the morals of his neighbours. On the contrary, a man with nothing to do has the more time to notice what others are doing wrong. He was very severe upon the misdeeds of the place, and he records them with the indignation of a man whose own hands were, for the moment, unoccupied.

He was much puzzled by the attitude of the young men of the neighbourhood toward certain kinds of property. They would not dream of taking a neighbour's purse. Yet they saw no harm in raiding his beehives or stripping his orchard on a summer night. Why, he asked, should honey and apples be fair game when money was not? It was a reasonable question, and I do not know that anybody ever gave him a satisfactory answer. I suspect that he did not want one. He wanted only to establish that the line between honest and dishonest was drawn in a very arbitrary place, so that a man of intelligence might be excused for drawing it somewhere else.

He drew it, in fact, so as to leave room for counterfeiting. His argument on this point is really ingenious. Money, he says, has no worth in itself. Its value lies entirely in the belief of those who use it. So long as a coin is believed in, it is as good as any other coin. Who, then, is harmed by a false one? The man who receives it is content, and the man who passes it on is content, and the belief goes on doing its work. It is only when someone insists on looking too closely that any damage is done, and the fault, he implies, is with the one who looked.

## Draft C

The memoir of the impostor is a curious document, and not least for the light it throws on the workings of a mind that could excuse almost anything. After several voyages at sea he returned home, and for some time he did nothing in particular. He was idle, and he appears to have been perfectly contented with idleness. Yet this did not prevent him from taking a lively interest in the misdeeds of his neighbours, which he criticised with a severity that would have done credit to a magistrate.

His criticism extended even to certain offences which his neighbours were inclined to overlook. He could not understand, he says, why the young men of the district should regard the raiding of a beehive or an orchard as a harmless amusement. [example needed: the passage in which he says this] The reader is at first disposed to applaud this scruple, and it is only on reading further that he begins to wonder whether the writer objected to the practice or merely to the smallness of its ambitions.

It was by this road that he arrived at his defence of counterfeit money. The argument is ingenious, and he presents it with evident satisfaction. Money, he points out, has no value in itself; its worth consists entirely in the belief of those who use it. A false coin that is believed in is therefore precisely as good as a true one. Nobody is injured so long as nobody finds out, and when somebody does find out, the injury is caused not by the coin but by the discovery.

One is tempted to answer him, and it is not easy to know where to begin. Perhaps the safest reply is that a currency which depends upon belief is exactly the kind of thing that ought not to be trifled with by those who do not believe in anything else.
