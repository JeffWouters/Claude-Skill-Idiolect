# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-09

## The passage (by the author)

The panorama before his view is the human mind. He panders to its divers follies, consults its varied wisdom. He stands umbrellaless in the rain of all its idiosyncrasies. Why has he not lifted up his voice? He, the book clerk, that lives among countless volumes of confessions! Whose daily task is to wrestle hour by hour with a living Comedie Humaine! Has the constant spectacle of so many books been astringent in its effect upon any latent creative impulse? Or has he been dumb in the colloquial sense, forsooth; a figure like Mr. Whistler's guard in the British Museum? Sundry "lettered booksellers" of England have, indeed, given us some reminiscences of bookselling and its humours. But they were the old boys. They belonged to an old order and reflected another day. "As physicians are called 'The Faculty' and counsellors-at-law 'The Profession,'" writes Boswell, "the booksellers of London are called 'The Trade.'" Let us look into this Trade as it is to-day, we said. So for a space we played we were a book clerk.

There are two, decidedly contradictory, popular conceptions of the man whose business it is to sell books. One is the sentimental notion of an old gentleman in a "stovepipe hat," a dreamer and an idealist, who keeps a second-hand stall. The most delightful pictures of him are in the pages of Anatole France. He is a man of much erudition. And books are his wife and family, food and drink. Then there is the other idea. "Why is it," we report the remark of an important looking gentleman in a high hat, "that clerks in book stores never know anything about books?" (or anything else, was perhaps not far from his thought.) This gentleman, it was readily perceived, had an idea that he had said something rather good. But it was not new. This conception of the book clerk is one of the world's seven jokes--brother to that of the mother-in-law. The book clerk of this view is a familiar figure in the pages of humour, like the talkative barber or the comic Irishman of the vaudeville stage--a stock character. His illiteracy is classic; his ignorant sayings irresistable. He was sired by Charles Keene and damned by Punch. Phil May was his godfather; and every industrious humourist employs him periodically. These two ideas of the book business are perhaps reconciled by the popularly cherished sentiment that book sellers are not what they were. Newspapers from time to time print feature articles about the days "When Book Sellers Knew Books." If you ask a salesman in a modern book shop if he has "Praed," you of course expect him to reply, "I have, sir (or madam), but it doesn't seem to do any good."

## Draft A

It has often struck me as odd that so few people who have worked in bookstores have written about the trade. Sailors write about the sea, and doctors about their patients, and policemen about their beats; every calling that brings a man into contact with the variety of mankind seems, sooner or later, to produce its memoirs. But the bookseller's clerk is silent. He stands all day at the meeting place of literature and the public, and he says nothing.

He cannot say that he has nothing to tell. No one sees more of the human comedy, in its small and daily forms, than the man behind the book counter. The vanities, the timidities, the enthusiasms, the pretences and the little absurdities of the reading public pass before him without end; people who would guard their secrets from a confessor will reveal the whole state of their minds to him by what they ask for, and by the manner in which they ask. He observes it all. He remembers a great deal of it. And he keeps it to himself. Why? Partly he is tired at the end of the day. Partly he thinks that nobody would believe him. Partly, too, having spent so many hours among books, he has acquired a certain respect, as it were, for the difficulty of adding to their number.

There is another reason, and it has to do with what the public already believes about him. The public holds, on this subject, two opinions, and they are flatly opposed. According to the first, the bookseller is a learned person: an elderly man in spectacles, among dusty shelves, who has read everything, who knows the date of every edition and the history of every binding, and who parts with a volume reluctantly, as though it were a friend. According to the second, the man who sells books is an ignorant clerk, who could not tell you the author of the most famous novel in the language, who confuses the poet with the publisher, and who would sell a hymnal and a treatise on poker with the same bright indifference. The public holds both of these opinions at once, and sees no contradiction.

The second figure is a stock character of the comic papers. He appears in them regularly. [example needed: a typical joke about the ignorant clerk] He is always young, always obliging and always wrong; the joke never varies, and it never seems to wear out. A man who knows that this is the picture in the reader's mind may be forgiven for hesitating before he takes up his pen. Whatever he writes, he will be read, so to speak, through the comic papers.

So he says nothing, and goes on wrapping parcels.

## Draft B

It is a curious fact that the people who work in bookshops so seldom write about what they see there. They spend their days among writers' work, they handle more books in a week than most readers handle in a year, and a fair number of them, one would think, must have literary ambitions of their own. And yet the trade has produced very little in the way of memoirs. Soldiers write about the army, doctors about their patients, actors about the theatre. The bookseller, for the most part, keeps his mouth shut.

It is not for lack of material. Few jobs give a better view of human oddity. Every day the door opens and in comes someone with a request, a habit, a vanity or a confusion that could fill a page. [example needed] The bookseller sees all of it, and sees it at close range, and says nothing. Perhaps he is too tired at the end of the day. Perhaps he thinks no one would believe him. Perhaps he simply assumes that what is ordinary to him must be ordinary to everyone.

Part of the difficulty may be that the public already has its own picture of him, and in fact two pictures, which do not agree. In the first, the bookseller is a learned man, a kind of scholar who has chosen the shop over the library. He knows every edition, can quote from memory, and will tell you with gentle authority which translation to buy and why. He is a little old, a little dusty, and wholly to be trusted. In the second picture, he is the opposite: a clerk who knows nothing whatever about the goods he sells, who could not tell a poet from a publisher, and who hands over whatever book is nearest when you ask for something by name.

The second picture is by far the more popular, because it is funnier. The ignorant book clerk is a stock figure in the comic magazines, as dependable as the absent-minded professor or the mother-in-law. The joke is always the same. A customer asks for a famous work, and the clerk misunderstands the title, or confuses the author with someone else, or offers something absurdly unsuitable with complete confidence. [example needed] The reader laughs, and the image settles a little more firmly in the public mind.

Neither picture is accurate, of course. Most of us who have stood behind the counter were somewhere in between: not scholars, but not fools either, and a good deal more observant than we let on. It may be that the silence of the trade is partly a reaction to these two images. It is hard to write about yourself when everyone already knows who you are, and when both of the versions they know are wrong.

## Draft C

It is a curious thing that booksellers so seldom write. I do not mean that they do not write books; that would be too much to ask of anybody who has spent the day among them. I mean that they do not write about their trade. And yet what a trade it is to write about! Consider. The bookseller stands all day at the crossroads of humanity. Everybody comes to him, sooner or later: the scholar and the schoolgirl, the parson and the actress, the man who reads everything and the man who is buying his first book because somebody told him he ought to. All day long they pass before him, each with his odd little wants, his odd little notions, his odd little ways of asking for what he does not know the name of. Year in and year out the bookseller watches this procession. And he says nothing. He goes home, he has his supper, and he keeps it all to himself.

Part of the reason, I think, lies in the public's idea of him. The public has, as it happens, two ideas of the bookseller, and they are exactly opposite.

The first is the learned dealer. He is an old man, generally, and a little bent. He wears spectacles, pushed up on the forehead. He sits in the back of a dim shop, among heaps of old volumes, and knows everything. He can tell you the date of an edition by the smell of it. He is courteous, but he is not, you understand, in trade; he is, rather, a kind of priest, and the shop is his temple, and the customer is admitted, if at all, on sufferance.

The second is the ignorant clerk. He is young. He is flippant. He does not know the difference between an author and a title, and he does not care. Ask him for a classic and he will suggest that it is "not in yet" but that he has something by a newer writer that is "going very well." Ask him who wrote a book and he will look in the front of it.

It is this second figure who has had by far the more successful career. He is, indeed, one of the permanent characters of the comic papers. You will find him there, week after week, in the drawings and in the jokes at the bottom of the page: a fashionable young lady asking for a well-known work, and the clerk replying with some perfectly idiotic misunderstanding. [example needed]. The public laughs. The joke is a good joke; it has been going for a long while now and shows no sign of wearing out.

The actual bookseller, who is neither a priest nor an idiot, reads these jokes, and smiles, perhaps, and says nothing. It is hard to know where one would begin.
