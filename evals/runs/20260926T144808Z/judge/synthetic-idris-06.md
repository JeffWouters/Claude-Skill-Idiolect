# Judge packet synthetic-idris-06

## The passage (by the author)

What is a meeting, really, once it has been placed on the calendar and allowed to recur indefinitely? It may be that the recurring meeting is the closest thing most organisations have to a ritual, in the anthropological sense of the word — a gathering whose original purpose has quietly dissolved while its form persists, maintained by a vague institutional reluctance to be the person who suggests cancelling it. We arrive, we exchange the customary pleasantries, and we wait for someone to articulate why we are there; perhaps someone does, and perhaps the explanation is convincing, but more often the conversation drifts toward whatever happens to be most visible that week. I suspect that very few of us would defend the meeting if asked to justify it from first principles, and yet we continue to attend with considerable loyalty. There is something in this that deserves more attention than the usual complaints about wasted time, because the persistence of the ritual tells us something about what teams actually need from one another.

Consider the way a meeting begins its life. Usually it is created in response to a genuine difficulty — a project that has lost its sense of direction, a dependency between teams that nobody quite owns, a decision that keeps being deferred — and in those first weeks it is arguably the most valuable hour anyone spends. The people in the room have a shared problem; they bring information that cannot easily be written down; they leave with a clearer sense of who is doing what. The difficulty is that the problem eventually resolves, or changes shape, while the invitation remains exactly as it was, reproducing itself week after week with the indifference of any automated process. Does anyone notice the moment at which the meeting stops serving its purpose? In my experience the transition is almost always invisible, since the attendees are still talking, the notes are still being taken, and the calendar still insists that this is where we ought to be.

## Draft A

Look at any team's calendar and you will find a handful of meetings that repeat every week, fortnight or month, and have done for as long as anyone can remember. They are easiest to understand not as tools but as rituals: things a group does together at fixed times, partly because it has always done them.

Most of them did not start that way. A recurring meeting almost always begins with a real shared problem. A launch is coming and several teams need to stay in step. An incident has exposed a gap that needs watching. A new group is forming and needs a regular place to work out how it will operate. Someone books a repeating slot, the right people come, and for a while the meeting does exactly what it was meant to do.

Then the problem changes. The launch ships, the gap gets closed, the group settles into its way of working. The reason for the meeting fades, but the meeting does not. It keeps its slot on the calendar, the invitations keep going out and people keep turning up, because the default for a recurring event is to recur. It has become a ritual that has outlived its reason.

What makes this hard is that nobody notices the moment it happens. There is no single meeting where the purpose visibly ends. Usefulness drains away gradually: the agenda gets thinner, updates start being repeated from elsewhere, a few people join with cameras off and do other work. Each of those signs is small enough to ignore, and ending a meeting feels like a bigger decision than letting it carry on. So it carries on. [example needed]

The people in the room are often the worst placed to see it, because the meeting has become part of the shape of their week. Someone has to step back and ask what problem this meeting is solving today, and whether it would be booked if it did not already exist. Rituals can be valuable, but they are better chosen deliberately than inherited by accident.

## Draft B

Anyone who has looked carefully at a working calendar will have noticed that it contains a certain number of meetings whose purpose nobody can quite recall. They recur weekly or fortnightly, they are attended with reasonable diligence, and they proceed according to a pattern that everyone understands, yet if you were to ask the participants why the meeting exists, the answers would be vague, various, or faintly embarrassed. It is tempting to regard these meetings simply as waste. I think it more illuminating to regard them as ritual — as practices that persist not because they continue to serve their original function but because they have become part of the texture of working life, and because stopping them would require a kind of decision that nobody feels entitled to make.

Most such meetings, it is worth remembering, did not begin as ritual. They began as a response to something real: a shared problem that needed several people's attention, a project that required close coordination, a period of difficulty in which regular contact was genuinely valuable. At the moment of their creation, they made a good deal of sense. The trouble is that the problem they addressed was eventually solved, or dissolved, or migrated elsewhere, while the meeting itself remained in the calendar, repeating on schedule with the quiet persistence that recurring invitations seem designed to encourage.

What is striking is how rarely anyone notices the moment at which a meeting stops being useful. There is no single occasion on which it becomes pointless; its usefulness simply thins, week by week, until what remains is the form without the substance. Because the decline is gradual, it passes without comment, and because the meeting is shared, responsibility for questioning it is shared too — which is to say that it belongs to no one in particular. Perhaps the most useful habit a team could adopt is not to cancel such meetings wholesale but to ask, from time to time, whether each one would still be created today.

## Draft C

Why do so many meetings on our calendars continue to recur long after anyone can say what they are for? The invitation arrives each week, the same people attend, and the same agenda is worked through with a kind of practised patience. It may be that these meetings are better understood as rituals than as tools, since they persist less because they are useful than because they have always been held.

Consider how such a meeting usually begins, before it has had time to settle into routine. There was once a genuine shared problem, something that required several people to coordinate closely for a period, and a regular meeting was the sensible way to handle it; the early sessions were probably lively, focused and difficult to miss. At some point the problem was solved, or it faded, or it moved to another team, and yet the meeting remained on the calendar as though nothing had changed. I suspect most recurring meetings follow roughly this course, from necessity to habit, without anyone deciding that they should.

There is also the curious fact that nobody seems to notice the moment at which a meeting stops being useful. The change is gradual rather than sudden, and each individual session is only slightly less valuable than the one before; nobody wishes to be the person who suggests cancelling something that others might still value, and so the question is never asked aloud. Arguably this is how rituals work in general, sustained by the reasonable assumption that someone else must know why they matter.

It seems to me that the answer is not to abolish recurring meetings, which often serve a real purpose while they last, but to give each of them a natural point at which its continuation must be chosen rather than assumed. Perhaps the remedy is simply to remember that every recurring meeting was once an answer to a question, and to ask occasionally whether the question is still there.
