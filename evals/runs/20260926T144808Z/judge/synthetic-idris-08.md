## The passage (by the author)

Where do deadlines actually come from? The question may seem odd, since most deadlines arrive with an air of inevitability, as though they had been imposed by the nature of things rather than decided by particular people for particular reasons. Some are genuinely external — a regulatory requirement, a contractual obligation, an event that will happen whether or not the software is ready — but a considerable proportion are, in some sense, invented; they emerge from planning conversations, from the desire to have something to announce, from a general feeling that work without a date attached is work that will never be finished. I do not say this to dismiss them, since invention is not the same thing as arbitrariness. It may be that invented deadlines serve a real purpose, concentrating attention and forcing decisions that would otherwise be postponed indefinitely; the difficulty is that we so rarely distinguish between the invented and the unavoidable, and we therefore tend to treat all of them with the same anxious reverence.

Consider what happens to a team as a deadline approaches. There is often a gradual narrowing of attention, in which everything not directly connected to the immediate goal begins to seem like a distraction; conversations become shorter, decisions become quicker, and the careful deliberation that characterised the early stages of the project gives way to a more pragmatic, sometimes more ruthless, style of working. Some of this is healthy — the pressure clarifies priorities and dissolves the kind of endless refinement that can paralyse a project. Some of it, arguably, is not; corners are cut that will have to be repaired later, people work hours that they would never accept in calmer periods, and the quality of judgement declines precisely when the stakes are highest. Who decides whether the trade-off is worth making, and on what basis? In my experience the decision is almost never made explicitly; it simply happens, as the logic of the deadline asserts itself.

## Draft A

Where do the due dates on our projects actually come from? Some of them are imposed from outside, by a regulation, a contract or an event that will happen whether the team is ready or not, and there is little to be done about those except to plan around them. Many others, I suspect, are made up within the organisation itself, chosen because a date was needed and this one seemed plausible at the time. It may be that the difference between the two kinds matters considerably more than we usually allow.

A made-up date is not necessarily a bad thing. It can give a team a shared point to aim at, and a reason to decide what matters most, where otherwise the work might drift without end; in some sense it lends shape to effort that would otherwise have none. The difficulty is that such a date, once written down, is soon treated exactly like an imposed one, and the knowledge that it was chosen rather than required quietly disappears from every conversation about it.

There is also the question of what an approaching date does to the people working towards it. Its first effect is often useful, since priorities become sharper and discussions that had been circling for some time are suddenly settled. Its later effects are less welcome: shortcuts that seemed reasonable under pressure accumulate into problems for someone else to solve, and the team works longer hours than it can sustain, arguably for a deadline that nobody outside the room would have noticed moving.

It seems to me that a team should know, for every date it works towards, which kind of date it is. Perhaps the healthiest deadline is one that everyone remembers was invented, and treats with exactly the seriousness it deserves.

## Draft B

Every project seems to have a due date, but it is worth asking where that date actually came from. The answer shapes how a team should treat it, and it is often less obvious than it looks.

Some dates are imposed from outside. A regulatory deadline, a contractual commitment, a trade event or a customer's own launch can set a date the team has no power to move. Those dates are real constraints, and planning around them is simply part of the job.

Many dates, though, are made up internally. Someone picked the end of a quarter, a round number of weeks or a date that sounded ambitious in a planning meeting. [example needed] There is nothing wrong with that in itself. A made-up date can be genuinely useful: it gives a team something to aim at, forces conversations about scope and stops work from expanding to fill whatever time is available. The problem is that, once written down, made-up dates tend to get treated exactly like imposed ones. The origin is forgotten, and a figure that was chosen for convenience starts being defended as if something outside the organisation depended on it.

As a date approaches, its effect on a team becomes stronger in both directions. On the positive side, a near deadline sharpens priorities wonderfully. Discussions that dragged on for weeks get settled in a day, nice-to-have features quietly drop out of scope and people focus on what really matters for the release.

On the negative side, the same pressure pushes teams towards shortcuts. Tests get skipped, reviews get lighter, known problems get deferred to "after the release", and people work longer hours to close the gap. Some of that is a reasonable trade-off for a short period. Sustained, it leaves behind fragile systems and tired people.

The useful habit is to know which kind of date you are dealing with. Respect the imposed ones. Use the made-up ones for focus, but be willing to move them when the cost of holding them becomes higher than the benefit.

## Draft C

Where do project due dates actually come from? The question sounds almost too simple to ask, and yet the answer is often less obvious than the confidence with which dates are discussed would suggest. Some dates are genuinely imposed from outside: a regulatory deadline, a contractual obligation, an event that will take place whether or not the work is ready. These have a hard edge to them, and teams generally understand why they matter. A great many others, however, are made up internally — chosen in a planning session, proposed by a manager as a reasonable aspiration, or simply arrived at because some date needed to be written down. Such dates are not necessarily arbitrary, but neither are they required by anything beyond the organisation's own preferences.

There is nothing wrong with invented dates as such. A target, even an artificial one, can help a team to focus, giving shape to work that might otherwise expand indefinitely and providing a shared point towards which effort can be directed. The difficulty is that invented dates have a way of forgetting their own origins. Within a few weeks of being proposed, a date that began as a helpful fiction comes to be treated with the same seriousness as one imposed by law, and questioning it begins to feel like questioning the team's commitment.

The effect on teams as the date approaches is, I think, genuinely double-edged. On the one hand, a looming deadline sharpens priorities in a way that little else does; discussions that had drifted for weeks are suddenly resolved, and work that was merely desirable is set aside in favour of work that is essential. On the other hand, the same pressure encourages shortcuts — tests deferred, documentation skipped, known weaknesses left for later — and it draws people into long hours that are sustainable only briefly. Whether the trade is worthwhile depends heavily on whether the date was worth that price in the first place.
