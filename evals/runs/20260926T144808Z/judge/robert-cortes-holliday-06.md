# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-06

## The passage (by the author)

'Tis a merry life, the book clerk's, and a hard one. Customers: Two youngish women. "Can you wait on us?" They want to get something, do not know just what, for a present. "Oh, no!" they say, "we don't want anything like so big a set as that. Something nicely bound." A copy of "Cranford" is near by. "Oh, when I read it I didn't think it much good." "Poetry?" "No, I don't think she is much interested in poetry." "Do you suppose an art book?"------"No, she is not interested in art." "Memoirs, then?" "No, she would not care for that." "Why, I had no idea," said one somewhat reprovingly to us, "that it would be as hard as this."

A calling which requires the practitioner to turn easily from the recondite gentleman inquiring the author of "Religious Teachers of Ancient Greece" to consideration of the problem (no less recondite) of a lady anxious to find something to entertain a child of five and a half inculcates some degree of mental agility. "I want," said the very fashionable lady, "to get a book for an old man--a" (with some petulance) "very stupid old man." "I want," from a serious old lady, "to get a book for a young man studying for the ministry." "I want," exclaimed a very smart apparition, "a dashing book for a man!" "What is the best book on Russia?" "Do you know, now, if this is a good story?--there are so many poor books nowadays." Says a large, uncommonly black lady, "I want 'Spears of Wheat, No. 3.'" (Discovered to be a prayer book.) "I want the latest book, please, on how to bring up a baby." "I'd like to see what you have on 'physical research.'" "Can you recommend a book for a young man with softening of the brain? Poor fellow, he's in Bloomingdale." "Is there any discount to Christian workers?" "Do you know," a demure person, an awful blank look coming over her face, "what I want has gone quite out of my head." There is an appealing look for help. "Something American," in a patrician voice, "for the ladies to read going over on the boat. This is American, now, is it? New York society? Ah, very good! Have you anything about the Rocky Mountains, or that sort of thing?"

## Draft A

Somebody wants a present. It is for a friend, or an aunt, or a young man who is going away. You are to find it. You bring out a novel. No, he has that--or, if he has not, he would not care for it. You try a volume of essays. Oh, he does not read essays. Poems? Poems, the customer says, are hardly the thing. Something in a handsome binding? Too showy. Something plain? Too plain. A book of travel? He has been everywhere. And so on, down the length of the store and back, until the customer, looking at you with a kind of mild reproach, as though you had failed her personally, says that she will "think about it," and goes out to think about it somewhere else.

This, you might suppose, is the worst of it. It is not. The real difficulty of the bookseller's trade is the jump. At one moment you are called upon to discuss, with a grave elderly gentleman, the relative merits of two editions of an ancient author, the one learned and the other more learned, and you had better know which is which. At the next moment a young woman wishes to know whether you have "that book with the red cover that everybody is reading." You must go from the one to the other without so much as a change of face. You must be, within the space of a minute, a scholar and a guesser of riddles.

And the requests. I set down some of them, as they come to mind, in no particular order. A book on the care of the baby. A book on the care of the dog. A book to teach a parrot to talk. A book "on etiquette, but not too strict." A book of recitations suitable for a church social. A book of jokes suitable for after-dinner. A dream book. A cook book. A book "about the war," no war specified. A book of [example needed]. A book that "will be good for a boy." A book that a nephew, who is clever, has not read. A prayer book, a small one, to go in a pocket. A prayer book, a large one, to go on a table.

All of these are bookselling. All of these, indeed, are literature. The customer is quite sure of it. And so, after a while, are you.

## Draft B

Of all the customers who come to a bookstore, the most exacting is the one who is buying a present. He does not want a book for himself; he wants a book for somebody else, and he has, apparently, no notion whatever of what that somebody else might like. He relies upon you. You suggest a novel. No, not a novel. You suggest a volume of essays. He turns it over, reads the title twice, and puts it down with the expression of a man who has been offered something slightly unwholesome. You suggest travel, biography, verse, a handsome illustrated edition of something standard. Each suggestion is considered, courteously, and each is rejected. In the end he buys, very often, the first thing you showed him. Or he goes away to think it over, which comes to the same thing for the store, only less profitably.

The difficulty of the salesman's position is not that any single question is hard. It is that the questions come one after another, without warning, from opposite ends of human interest. One moment a gentleman wishes to discuss the merits of rival editions of a learned work, and you are expected to have an opinion, and a sound one. The next moment a lady wants to know whether you have "anything nice" for a girl of a certain age, and she will not tell you the age. You must change your mind, so to speak, as a man changes trains, quickly and without looking back, and you must never let the one customer perceive that you were, a moment ago, attending gravely to the other.

Consider, if you will, what is asked for in the course of an ordinary day. A guide to the care of babies. A prayer book. [examples needed: further odd requests] And so on, through every department of life, from the cradle, as it were, to the church door. Some of these requests are perfectly reasonable. Some are a little strange. A few of them are of such a nature that no bookstore in the world could satisfy them, though the customer is quite confident that yours should.

Well, you do what you can. You find the prayer book. You find the book about babies. And you wait, with what patience you possess, for the gentleman who is buying a present.

## Draft C

People imagine that selling books is a matter of knowing where things are on the shelves. It is, in part. But the larger part is knowing how to answer anything at all, from anyone, at any moment, and then doing it again for the next person, who wants something entirely different.

The gift shoppers are the hardest. They come in wanting a present for someone and have no idea what. You make a suggestion and they say the person already has it. You make another and they say it is too serious. A third is too light, a fourth too long, a fifth too expensive, a sixth not expensive enough to look like a proper gift. You go through half the shop with them, and at the end they either buy the first thing you showed them or leave with nothing, thanking you warmly for your help.

What makes the job tiring is not any one of these customers but the constant change of gear. One minute a scholar is asking about an edition of [work needed] and expects you to know the difference between printings. The next, someone asks a question so slight that you are not sure it is about books at all. [example needed] You have to take both questions with the same seriousness, because both customers are paying, and because to each of them the question is the important one.

Over the years the requests pile up into a kind of catalogue of human need, and a long and odd catalogue it is. It begins, in my memory, with people asking for guides to the care of a baby, earnest and a little frightened, wanting to be told what to do. It runs on through requests I could never have predicted, [examples needed], each one delivered as though it were the most natural thing in the world to ask a stranger for. And it ends, somewhere, with prayer books: plain ones and bound ones, small enough for a pocket or large enough for a lectern. Between those two points lies nearly everything people hope a book can do for them.

You learn to listen for what is not being said. The person asking for a guide to baby care may really want reassurance. The person asking for a prayer book may be buying it for a death in the family, or for a wedding, and the right answer depends on which. None of this is in any catalogue, and none of it is taught. You pick it up at the counter, one customer at a time.
