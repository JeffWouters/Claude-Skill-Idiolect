# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-06

## The passage (by the author)

It occurs to me that my examples of conventional falsehood are largely feminine. So, I fancy, they should be. One of the reasons, surely, why women have been credited with less perfect veracity than men is that the burden of conventional falsehood falls chiefly on them. A man expects his wife to do this kind of thing for him. It is she who accepts or refuses their common invitations, directs their joint social manœuvres, encounters the world for them both on the purely social side. He is not expected to do it any more than he is expected to order the dinner. There is more straight-from-the-shoulder talk, I imagine, among men by themselves than among women by themselves; but that is partly because women slip out of the social harness less frequently and less easily. A man among men is perhaps (I speak under correction) more inveterately his personal self; a woman among women more inveterately her social self. It may be that it is easier to wear the harness constantly than to gall one’s shoulders afresh each day with putting it on. I am inclined to think that women are as honest with their intimate friends as are men; but--they have had an age-long training in the penalties of making one’s self unpleasant. So many low motives are imputed to women--and most of them, at the present day, quite unjustly--that they are driven to the lesser mendacities for the sake of getting some justice done them. When Mr. A. asks Mrs. B. if she does not think Mrs. C. beautiful, she is almost bound to say that she does, though she does not. Otherwise, she will be taken for a jealous fool. One lie is better than two; and it is better to be thought a fool when you are not, than jealous and a fool when you are neither.

## Draft A

It is women who tell most of the white lies of society, and they can hardly be blamed for it. The burden falls to them by a simple division of labour. In most households it is the wife who manages the couple's invitations, answers the notes, remembers the anniversaries, and decides--with a tact the husband seldom suspects--which dinners must be accepted and which may be declined. Every refusal wants an excuse, and every acceptance of a dull evening wants a little show of pleasure. The husband signs nothing and pretends nothing. He is spared the fib because he is spared the business.

Men, besides, speak more bluntly among themselves. They can tell one another that a man is a bore, or that a proposal is nonsense, and part the best of friends; they have a sort of licence which does not extend to the drawing-room. Women rarely drop their public role, even with each other. They are always, perhaps, a little on duty.

There is another reason, and it is not a pretty one. The world is ready to suspect a woman's motives on the slightest provocation. If she does not praise another woman's gown, she is thought envious; if she is cool to a pretty neighbour, she is thought jealous. The suspicion is usually unjust--but it is none the less real, and a sensible woman might well be expected to take account of it. So she admires the gown she does not care for, and is warm to the neighbour she finds tiresome, not because she is false but because the truth would be misread. The small fib is her defence against a larger injustice. I fancy most men, placed in her position, would fib a good deal more.

## Draft B

It is worth asking why the social white lie should fall, as it undoubtedly does, so largely to women. I do not think it is because women are less truthful than men. I think it is simply because women have the social business of the world to do.

Consider the married pair. Who answers the invitations? Who writes the notes of regret, and the notes of thanks, and the notes that explain why one cannot possibly come on Thursday? The wife, of course. The husband, as a rule, does not even know that the invitation has arrived. He goes where he is taken, and he stays away when he is told; and the whole delicate apparatus of excuse and evasion is managed for him, without his knowledge, by the woman at his side. If a fib is needed--and a fib very often is needed--it is she who must tell it.

Men, moreover, are allowed a bluntness among themselves which women seldom permit to one another. A man may tell another man that he is talking nonsense, and they will dine together the next evening quite amicably. Women do not, as a rule, drop their public manner even in private. They are always, in a sense, on duty; and a person on duty cannot afford to say exactly what she thinks.

But there is another reason, and it is not a pleasant one. Women are suspected. If a woman declines to praise another woman's frock, or her house, or her husband, she is at once accused (it is always the accusation of jealousy!) of envy. Her honesty is never taken for honesty; it is taken for spite. And so, very naturally, she learns to say that the frock is charming and the house delightful--not because she is a hypocrite, but because the truth, in her mouth, would be misread. I do not blame her. I am only sorry that the world should have made the small fib so much safer than the plain word.

## Draft C

If the small social untruths of daily life are a burden, it is a burden that falls mostly on women. This is not because women are less honest than men. It is because of the work they are given to do.

In most households it is the wife who handles a couple's invitations and social dealings. She accepts the dinner and declines the lecture, writes the note of thanks, and explains why they cannot come on Thursday. Every one of those exchanges carries its small demand for tact, and tact, pressed hard enough, becomes the polite fib. The husband, who never sees the letters, is spared the need to tell them. [example needed]

There is a difference, too, in how the sexes speak when they are among their own. Men speak more bluntly with one another. They contradict, they grumble, they say plainly that the dinner was dull, and nobody minds much. Women rarely drop their public role in this way. Even among friends the courtesies continue, the compliments are paid, the awkward truth is softened, because the habit of managing other people's feelings is not something that can be set down at the door.

And then there is a quieter pressure, one that is seldom admitted. Women's motives are too readily suspected. If a woman says that another woman's gown is unbecoming, or her party tiresome, it is assumed that she is jealous, however just the remark may be. A man may say the same thing and be thought merely candid. Knowing this, a woman learns to praise what she does not admire, not from any love of falsehood but to avoid an accusation she has done nothing to earn. The minor fib becomes a kind of self-defence.

So the next time someone remarks that women are the more practised at white lies, it is worth asking who handed them the practice.
