# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-06

## The passage (by the author)

“You wanted to know,” he said, “what was the difference between stealing and check-raising. Now, let me ask you a question. What’s the difference between check-raising and some of those big financial operations we’ve all been reading about? Suppose I have a check for five dollars, and I put my brains into it and I manipulate it so that I can pass it off for five hundred. I shove it in to the cashier and he takes it. Before he finds out his mistake, I have made myself scarce. What’s the difference between that transaction and what the ‘big fellows’ on the street are doing?” He mentioned several names that I had not thought of in that connection.

“The difference,” said I, “is--” Then it occurred to me that it was a subject to which I should give further thought. So we postponed the conversation till he should call again--which he never did.

I may be doing injustice to my friend the forger, but he gave me the impression that he considered himself to be, on the whole, a rather admirable character. His proposed change of business seemed to be rather a concession to the prejudices of the legal profession than the result of any personal scruple. As he saw himself he was a man of idealistic temper whose ideals conflicted with social usage. Society was all the time getting into his way, and in the inevitable collisions he had usually the worst of it. He regretted this, but he bore no malice. By the time a man has reached middle life and accumulated a good deal of experience he takes the world as he finds it.

He had encased himself in a moral system which was self-consistent and which explained to his own satisfaction all that had happened to him. One thing fitted into another, and there was no room for self-reproach.

## Draft A

I once had a conversation with a man who had forged a check, and he put a question to me that I could not answer on the spot.

He did not deny what he had done. He had altered a check, and he knew perfectly well that the law called it a crime. What he wanted to know was how it differed from the schemes that go on every day in the big markets. Men there move sums far larger than anything he had touched, and do it by methods that come to much the same thing, making paper say something that is not quite true in order to take money that is not quite theirs. They are called financiers. He was called a forger. Where, he asked, was the line?

I had no ready reply. I felt there was a difference but could not put it into words quickly enough, and he saw that. [example of the schemes he named needed]

What struck me most was how he saw himself. He did not think of himself as a criminal who had slipped. He thought of himself as a man of principle who happened to be at odds with convention. The world had its rules, and he had his, and his were at least honest about what they were.

That private code explained everything about him. It explained the forgery, it explained his calm, and above all it explained why he felt no guilt. Guilt needs a rule you believe in and have broken. He had broken no rule he believed in. By his own ethics he had done nothing wrong, and so there was nothing for his conscience to take hold of. He was not hiding remorse. He had none to hide.

I have thought about his question since. I still believe there is an answer, but I notice it is not as quick as I would like.

## Draft B

I once had a long talk with a man who had been convicted of forging checks. I had expected to find him either penitent or defiant. He was neither. He was argumentative, in the manner of one who has thought the matter out and is anxious to have his conclusions tested.

"What is the difference," he asked me, "between what I did and what is done every day in the great markets? I changed a figure on a check. They change the value of whole companies, and nobody sends them to prison. [example needed] Tell me where the line is."

I am sorry to say that I did not tell him. I had a feeling that there was a difference, and that it was an important one, but I could not at the moment put my finger on it. It is embarrassing to be caught in this way by a man whom one has come to improve. I made some general remarks about the law, which he received with polite indulgence, as a teacher receives the answer of a backward pupil.

It soon became plain that he did not regard himself as a criminal at all. He regarded himself as a man of principle who had had the misfortune to disagree with the conventions of his time. He had his own code, and he had lived up to it. That the rest of the world had a different code was, in his view, a fact about the world rather than about him.
What struck me most was the completeness of the arrangement. His private system of ethics explained everything. There was no corner of his conduct that it did not cover, and so there was no corner left over for remorse. A man who is troubled by his conscience has at least two standards, the one he keeps and the one he fails to keep. My forger had only one, and he kept it perfectly. It was not a very good standard, but it gave him a serenity that many better men might envy.

## Draft C

I once had a long conversation with a man whose trade had been the forging of checks. He was not in the least ashamed of himself, and he was very willing to discuss his case. What puzzled him, he said, was the distinction that society drew between his offence and the operations of the great financiers. He had altered a check. They had carried through great schemes in the market. [example needed: the scheme he named] Where, he asked, was the difference?

I confess that I had no ready answer. I could have told him that the one thing was against the law and the other was not, but I suspected that he knew this already, that he had turned it over in his mind through a good many idle hours, and that he did not regard it as an answer at all but only as a restatement of the question in the language of the courts. He was not asking about the law. He was asking about the principle behind it, and on that subject I found myself less well informed than I could have wished.

It became clear as we talked that he regarded himself as a man of principle. He had his own code, and he had kept it faithfully; it was merely that his code happened to differ at certain points from the conventions of the community. He spoke of those conventions without bitterness, rather as a scholar might speak of the opinions of a rival school. He had considered them carefully. He had rejected them.

This, I think, explained the most remarkable thing about him, which was his entire freedom from any sense of guilt. A man feels guilty when he has broken his own rules. My forger had broken only other people's, and for other people's rules he had never professed any great respect. His private ethics were complete and consistent, and they accounted for everything he had done. There was nothing left over for the conscience to trouble about.
