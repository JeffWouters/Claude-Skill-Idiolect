# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-10

## The passage (by the author)

From the evils of a soulless commercialism, and from the inanities of fashion, what is the way of escape? From the evils of peace he turns to the heroism of war.

I wish I could hear again The chivalrous battle song.

Ah God, for a man with heart, head, hand, Like some of the simple great ones gone For ever and ever by, One still strong man in a blatant land.

At last, breaking in upon the deadly stupidity and selfishness of the common life, is the noise of battle:--

it lightened my despair When I thought that a war would arise in defence of the right, That an iron tyranny now should bend or cease, The glory of manhood stand on his ancient height, Nor Britain’s one sole God be the millionaire.

Let it go or stay, so I wake to the higher aims Of a land that has lost for a little her lust of gold, And love of a peace that was full of wrongs and shames, Horrible, hateful, monstrous, not to be told; And hail once more to the banner of battle unroll’d!

That was an appeal to Young England, the England that was too young to remember the Napoleonic wars and was thirsting for an experience of its own.

We may see in such an outburst of the militant spirit only the recrudescence of savagery. It is better to treat it seriously, for it is something which each generation must reckon with. Tennyson sums up the matter from the standpoint of ardent youth:--

Let it flame or fade, and the war roll down like a wind, We have proved we have hearts in a cause, we are noble still, And myself have awaked, as it seems, to the better mind. It is better to fight for the good than to rail at the ill; I have felt with my native land, I am one with my kind, I embrace the purpose of God, and the doom assign’d.

## Draft A

There is a poem by [poet needed], written in the middle of the Victorian peace, which startles the modern reader. It is a call to war, and it is made, not in the name of conquest, but in the name of morality. The poet looked about him and saw a nation grown fat with trade. Men cheated one another behind their counters and called it business. Peace, as he saw it, had become the cover for a meaner kind of warfare, in which the weak were beaten down without any of the risks of honest battle.

Out of this disgust came his longing for a hero. [quotation needed] He wished for a leader strong enough to sweep away the petty and the sordid, and he was ready to welcome the sound of the guns if they should bring such a man. [quotation needed] War, in his eyes, would be a purging fire. It would burn out the greed and the idleness and leave the nation clean.

We must remember to whom these lines were addressed. They fell upon the ears of young Britons who had never known war at first hand. To them it was a thing of old stories and fine pictures. They had no memory of its cost. The poet's words did not have to overcome any stubborn recollection of suffering; they found the ground prepared.

It is easy for us to dismiss all this as a relic of savagery, and to congratulate ourselves on having outgrown it. But I do not think that is the wise course. The poet was not a savage. He was a sensitive man who saw real evils in the peace of his day and could think of no remedy strong enough except war. That is a feeling which has come before, and which will come again. Every generation must reckon with it for itself. If we would have peace, we must show that peace can do what the poet hoped war would do, and that it is not merely a comfortable name for idleness.

## Draft B

There is a poem by a Victorian poet which strikes the modern reader as a strange thing to have been written in a peaceful and prosperous age. It is a call to war. The poet looks about him and sees a nation grown fat and greedy, given over to buying and selling, and he can find no cure for it but the shock of battle. [quotation needed: the poet's lines longing for a heroic leader] He longs for a strong man to arise and lead his people out of their comfortable meanness, and he welcomes the coming conflict as a purifying fire.

We may think this a curious prescription. But we should remember to whom the poem was addressed. It spoke to young Britons who had grown up without any memory of war. They had heard of it from their grandfathers, and they had read of it in their histories, but to them it was a romantic thing, belonging to the same world as knights and crusades. What they knew at first hand was peace, and peace had shown them chiefly its idle and sordid side; they had seen the counting-house but not the battlefield.

It would be easy to dismiss all this as a relapse into savagery, and to congratulate ourselves on being wiser. I should hesitate to do so. The poet was not praising slaughter for its own sake. He was expressing, in the only language that seemed strong enough, a genuine disgust with a life that asked nothing of men but that they should grow rich.

That disgust recurs in every generation that has known only peace. It deserves to be taken seriously, not because its remedy is a good one, but because the disease it describes is real. If we will not provide some nobler occupation for the energies of youth, youth will go looking for one.

## Draft C

A Victorian poet once wrote verses calling for war, and not reluctantly. He saw war as a cure. The long peace, as he described it, had made his country greedy and idle, and he believed only conflict could purge it of both.

The verses are worth reading closely. [quotation needed] They long for a heroic leader, a strong man who will rise up and lead the nation out of its comfort. They do not merely accept the prospect of fighting; they welcome it. War appears in them not as a grim necessity but as something close to a blessing, a fire that will burn away the softness and the money-grubbing of a peaceful age.

It is easy to see why these lines found an audience. They spoke above all to young Britons who had no memory of the country's past wars. Those readers had grown up in the peace the poet was complaining about. They knew war only from books and songs, where it is always glorious and nobody's letters home go unanswered. To them, a call to arms sounded less like a threat than like an invitation to finally matter. [example needed]

The tempting response is to dismiss all this as savagery, a relic of older and cruder instincts that civilised people should have outgrown. That response is comfortable, and it is a mistake. The poet was not a savage, and neither were his young readers. He was naming something real: the restlessness of people who feel that peace has given them nothing to live for, and the suspicion that comfort has made them smaller. Waving that away does not make it go away. It only means that the next time the feeling arises, nobody will have prepared an answer to it.

Each generation should take this seriously, and each has to do it for itself, because each arrives without the memories of the last. The longing the poet voiced will come back. The question is whether it will find something better than war to satisfy it.
