# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-02

## The passage (by the author)

Now there are some who, believing that all is for the best in the best of possible worlds, and that to-morrow is necessarily better than to-day, may think that if culture is a good thing we shall infallibly be found to have more of it than we had a generation since; and that if we can be shown not to have more of it, it can be shown not to be worth seeking. Having, myself, a congenital case of agoraphobia, I habitually say nothing to the professional optimists in the public square. The wilderness is a good place to cry in; the echoes are magnificent. So I shall not attempt to deprive any one of Candide’s happy conviction. If any person is kind enough to listen, I will simply ask him to contemplate a few facts with me. No one will be too optimistic, I fancy, to grant that there are proportionally fewer Americans who care about culture--and who know the real thing when they see it--than there were one or two generations ago. Contact with “the best that has been thought and said in the world” is not desired by so large a proportion of the community as it was. That there are new and parvenu branches of learning, furiously followed, I, on my part, shall not attempt to deny. But culture is another matter. Perhaps the sociologists can show that this is a good thing. I do not ask any one to deplore anything. I only ask the well-disposed to examine the change that has come over the spirit of our American dream.

If I were asked to give, off hand, the causes of the gradual extirpation of culture among us, I should name the following:

The increased hold of the democratic fallacy on the public mind.

The influx of a racially and socially inferior population.

## Draft A

We are all of us, in America, brought up to believe in progress. I do not propose, here, to question it very far. But the belief that things are always getting better has one grave disadvantage: it makes us unwilling to count our losses. We are so busy looking at what we have gained that we do not notice what has quietly slipped out of the back door. And I am convinced that one thing, at least, has slipped out within the memory of people who are not yet old--I mean the respect for cultivated learning.

I do not say that there are no cultivated people left. There are; but there are fewer of them, and--what is worse--there are fewer people who know one when they see one. The man who has read widely and well, who carries his reading lightly and yet is shaped by it, is no longer an object of general reverence. Let us admit that the universities flourish; that new departments spring up every year; that there are more specialists, in more subjects, than our grandfathers ever dreamed of. Nobody disputes it. But a specialist is not necessarily a cultivated man, and a thriving faculty of [example needed] does not make up for a public that has forgotten what cultivation is for.

How has it come about? I shall not pretend to give all the causes; but some of them are plain enough to be named. There is, first of all, our egalitarian habit of mind, which finds it faintly offensive that one person should know more, and better, than another. There is, too, the enormous change in the make-up of the population. Immigration has brought us great numbers of people (I do not quarrel with the fact; I merely record it) to whom our older traditions of learning mean little or nothing, and who could hardly be expected to cherish what they never inherited.

## Draft B

We are a people who believe, almost as an article of religion, that things are always getting better. It is a comfortable belief, and on the whole a useful one; but it has the disadvantage of making us blind to our losses. We count what we have gained, and we count it very carefully, while what has slipped away from us goes unrecorded because nobody thinks to look for it. Yet some things have slipped away. One of them, I am afraid, is cultivated learning--not scholarship, which flourishes, but the cultivation that used to be the ordinary possession of the educated person.

I do not say that learning is dead. New academic fields spring up every year; there are more professors, more departments, and more published research than our grandfathers could have imagined. Granted; but the professor of some brand-new science is not necessarily a cultivated man, and he would very likely not care to be thought one. What has declined is not knowledge but the valuing of a certain kind of knowledge. Fewer people possess genuine cultivation, and--what is worse--fewer people are able to recognise it when they meet it. The thing has become a little quaint.

How did this come about? The causes are many, and I can do no more here than begin to list them. The first, I fancy, is the egalitarian habit of mind, which distrusts anything that cannot be shared equally by every one and is therefore suspicious of culture on principle. Another is the great change in our population brought about by immigration, which has filled our schools with children whose traditions, however admirable, are not the traditions on which our older learning rested. ## Draft C

We are a people who believe in progress, and we believe in it so steadily that we rarely stop to ask what it has cost. Each generation, we assume, knows more, does more, and lives better than the one before. In many ways that is true. But a faith in constant improvement can make us blind to the things we have quietly lost, and one of them, I think, is cultivated learning.

By cultivation I do not mean information, of which we have more than ever, nor expertise, which flourishes. I mean the slow, unhurried familiarity with the best that has been thought and written, the habit of reading for its own sake, the ear for a good sentence and the memory stocked with them. A generation or two ago this was something a fair number of people aspired to, even if few achieved it. Today fewer people value it, and, more telling, fewer are able to recognise it when they meet it. The cultivated man is no longer admired or envied; he is simply not noticed.

This is not to say that learning has withered everywhere. New academic fields spring up and thrive. Departments multiply, journals multiply, and specialists grow ever more precise within their chosen corners. What has thinned is not scholarship but the general, humane culture that once surrounded it and gave it an audience. [example needed]

Why should this be so? The causes are several, and I can only begin to list them. The first is the spread of egalitarian thinking, which is suspicious of anything that sets one mind above another and so looks on cultivation as a form of snobbery. The second is the great demographic change brought about by immigration, which has filled our schools with children for whom the older tradition was never an inheritance, and which has pressed those schools toward teaching what is useful rather than what is fine. There are other causes, and they deserve their turn.
