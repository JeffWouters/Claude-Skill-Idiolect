## The passage (by the author)

Then it should be remembered that the missionaries of civilization have not themselves been above reproach. The “multitudes of the heavenly hosts” might be heard for a moment singing of good will among men, but they did not remain to do the work. The men of good will who were to work out the plan were very human indeed. Milton, in the Hymn “On the Morning of Christ’s Nativity,” warns us of the long interval between the Christmas prophecy and its historical fulfillment.

For, if such holy song Enwrap our fancy long, Time will run back and fetch the age of gold; And speckled vanity Will sicken soon and die, And leprous sin will melt from earthly mould;

Yea, Truth and Justice then Will down return to men, Orbed in a rainbow; and, like glories wearing, Mercy will sit between, Throned in celestial sheen, With radiant feet the tissued clouds down steering: And Heaven, as at some festival, Will open wide the gates of her high palace hall.

But all the imagery of the gala day of peace fades away before the immediate reality.

But wisest Fate says no, This must not yet be so.

This veto of “wisest Fate” is not absolute. It only calls a halt upon our imagination until the rest of our nature catches up with it. Mankind is not to have peace till it has suffered for it and worked for it. The workmen must do their work over and over again till they have learned the right way.

That the “Christian nations” are not hypocrites, but novices who have been making some progress toward the Christian ideal, becomes evident when we look back over their history. They are not the descendants of the simple shepherds of the plains of Bethlehem. Far from it! When they first began to “profess and call themselves Christians,” they were not thinking of the beatitudes. They had not got that far.

## Draft A

It is often asked, with an air of triumph, how nations that call themselves Christian can be so little given to peace. The question assumes that the name was a promise of immediate performance. It was nothing of the sort.

We must remember what kind of people were entrusted with the spreading of civilization. They were not angels sent down for the purpose. They were men and women of ordinary clay, with tempers and appetites and a great deal of stubborn ignorance. The work they had in hand was slow, and they did it slowly, as such work has always been done. To expect that they should have finished it in a few centuries is to misunderstand both the workers and the work.

The poets have understood this better than the critics. Milton, in one of his poems, pictures the coming of a golden age, when the old wrongs shall be done away and peace shall reign. [quotation needed] But he does not leave us there. Having raised the vision, he admits frankly that it must wait. [quotation needed] The time is not yet. There is a good deal to be gone through before the dream can be realized. That is not the confession of a man who has been deceived. It is the sober judgment of one who knows how long it takes to build.

I would say, then, that these nations are not frauds but learners. A learner is not dishonest because he has not mastered his lesson. He is dishonest only if he pretends to have mastered it, or if he refuses to go on with it. And we should not forget how the lesson began. The faith that first came to the Western peoples came to races that had little use for gentleness. They took what suited them and passed over the rest. The fighting virtues they understood; the gentle teachings they quietly put aside, as something to be attended to later. We are only now getting round to them.

## Draft B

It is often asked why the nations that call themselves Christian have fallen so far short of their ideal of peace. The question is usually put in a tone that implies its own answer, namely that the profession was never sincere. But the matter is not quite so simple.

We should remember who it was that carried civilisation from one country to another. It was not a company of saints. It was a company of very imperfect men, traders and soldiers and adventurers, who took their faith with them as they took their tools, and who used both according to the habits they had already formed. The work they were engaged in was slow work, and it was done by hands that were not always clean. It would have been surprising if the result had been otherwise.

Milton understood this. In one of his poems he pictures the coming of a golden age, when the old evils shall be done away and peace shall return to the earth. [quotation needed: Milton's lines on the golden age] Yet the poet no sooner draws the picture than he admits that the time is not yet. The golden age must wait. There is a great deal of history between the vision and the fulfilment, and he does not pretend otherwise.

The Christian nations, then, are not frauds but learners, and rather slow learners. Their early faith was a faith of conquest and dominion, and it had little use for the gentler teachings on which it was supposed to rest; those teachings were read in the churches and ignored in the council chamber. It is only by degrees that a people discovers what its own creed requires of it. The discovery is not yet complete.

## Draft C

It is fair to ask why the countries that call themselves Christian fall so far short of the peace their faith describes. The question is usually asked with a sneer, but it deserves a real answer.

Part of the answer lies in who did the work. The people who carried civilisation outward were not saints. They were flawed men and women, with the same greed, fear and pride as anyone else, and the work they were doing was slow. Nothing that changes how whole peoples live happens in one generation, or in several. To expect a finished result from unfinished people is to misunderstand the job.

Milton understood this. In one of his poems he pictures a golden age, a time when peace and justice return to the world, and he describes it with real longing. Then he stops himself. The golden age, he admits, is not yet. It must wait. [quotation needed] The poem holds both things at once: the vision is true, and it is not here. That is a more honest position than either the believer who pretends the vision is already realised or the scoffer who says that because it is not realised it was never meant.

Seen this way, the so-called Christian nations are learners, not frauds. A learner is someone who has accepted a standard he does not yet meet. His failures are real, but they are the failures of someone still in school, not of someone lying about having graduated.

It also helps to remember what those nations first took from their faith. Their early belief did not dwell on the gentle teachings. It took up the parts that suited conquering peoples and passed over the parts about meekness and mercy. [example needed] Those gentler teachings were there all along, waiting, much like Milton's golden age. They are still waiting to be read with the attention they always asked for.
