# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-08

## The passage (by the author)

Some people say that it is the most awful trial.

But it isn't so at all.

One of the most entertaining things that can be done in the world, so full of interesting things, is to go hunting lodgings. Also, it is one of the most enlightening things that can be done, for, pursued with intelligence and energy, it gives one an excellent view of humankind; that is, of a particularly human kind of humankind. It is a confoundly Christian thing to do--hunting lodgings--because it opens the heart to the queer ways, and speech, and customs of the world.

Now, I myself hunt lodgings as some men hunt wild game.

Nothing is better when one is out of sorts, somewhat run down, and peevish with the world generally than to go out one fine afternoon and hunt lodgings In some remote part of town.

When in a foreign city, especially, the first thing I myself do, as soon as I am comfortably settled somewhere--and after, of course, having looked up the celebrated sights of the place, the Abbey, the Louvre, Grant's Tomb---is to put in a day or so hunting lodgings.

Even to read in the papers of lodgings to let is refreshing and educational. All lodgings are "sunny"--in the papers. They are let mainly by "refined" persons, and are wonderfully "quiet." I remember last summer in London there was "a small sitting to let to a young lady." Lodgings, by the way, are usually "apartments" in England, as you know. Though, indeed, it is true that when a gentleman rents over there what we call a "furnished room" he is commonly said to "go into lodgings." A fine phrase, that; it is like to that fine old expression "commencing author." And that reminds me: the most fascinating lodgings to hunt, perhaps, anywhere, are called "chambers." These which I mean are in the old Inns of Court in London. And the most charming of these remaining is Staple Inn, off Holborn. I used frequently to hunt chambers in "the fayrest Inne of Chancerie." There are no "modern conveniences" there. You draw your own water at a pump in the venerable quadrangle, and you "find" your own light. But to return:

## Draft A

I do not know of any pastime more to be recommended than looking for rooms. I do not mean taking them. Taking them is another business altogether, and often a melancholy one. I mean looking. You may do it on any afternoon, it costs nothing but shoe leather, and you will learn more about your fellow creatures in a week of it than in a year of ordinary society. You go in at one door after another, and behind each there is a life, and a person to show it to you, and a manner of showing it. And, somehow, you come away cheered. I cannot altogether account for this. Perhaps it is that one sees so many people getting on, one way or another, in so many odd arrangements, that one's own arrangements begin to look reasonable.

You begin with the newspaper. The advertisements for rooms are written in a language of their own, a small and select vocabulary, very pleasant to the ear. Every room is "attractive." Every neighbourhood is "desirable." Rooms are "sunny," "airy," "homelike." They are in a house that is "refined." The company is "congenial." The locality is "select." Nobody, it appears, ever lets a room that is dark, or dull, or up four flights in the back, although I have been in a good many that were. It is a kind of poetry, the advertisement for rooms, and like other poetry it is not to be read too literally.

But if you would see rooms at their most charming, you must go, I think, to London, and to the old inns where the lawyers were once lodged, and some are still. There, through a gateway off a roaring street, you come upon a courtyard, and quiet, and a tree, and an old building of dark brick with a staircase that has been going up and down for longer than anybody can remember. The rooms up that staircase are the most delightful rooms in the world. They have panelling. They have deep window seats. They have fireplaces that were built when fireplaces meant something. They have, also, no bath to speak of, and very little in the way of heat, and conveniences of a kind that the modern tenant would describe, if he were polite, as historical. One cannot have everything. It is not certain, looking out of those windows, that one would want to.

## Draft B

I should like to recommend to anyone who finds time hanging heavy on his hands the pastime of looking for rooms. I do not mean looking for rooms because you need them; that is a trial, and a grim one. I mean looking for rooms as other people go to the theatre, or walk in the park, or collect old prints, for the pleasure of the thing. There is no better entertainment to be had for nothing.

It is, to begin with, highly educational. You learn more about your fellow creatures in an afternoon of ringing doorbells than in a month of reading novels about them. You see how people live, what they put on their mantelpieces, what they consider a view, and what they think a reasonable thing to ask for it. And it is, curiously enough, a wonderful remedy for low spirits. One comes home from it, as a rule, in excellent humour, and very well satisfied with one's own lodging, whatever its deficiencies.

The advertisements alone are worth the price of the newspaper. They are written in a language of their own, a language made up almost entirely of flattering stock words, each of which means something a little different from what it says. But if you would see the pastime at its best, you should go looking for rooms in the old legal inns of London. There, I think, are the most charming rooms in the world. You climb a worn stair; you pass a door with a name painted on it that has been there, apparently, from time immemorial; and you come into a room with deep windows, panelled walls and an atmosphere of learned quiet, as though generations of lawyers had sat there thinking, slowly and profoundly, about property. Of the modern comforts there are, it must be admitted, few or none. The heating is a matter of opinion. The plumbing is a matter of history. Nobody seems to mind.

## Draft C

Most people look for rooms only when they have to, and they treat it as a chore to be got through as quickly as possible. I have come to think of it the other way round. Looking for rooms, when there is no pressing need to find one, is among the most enjoyable ways I know of spending an afternoon.

For one thing, it teaches you a great deal about people. You are let into houses you would never otherwise enter, and shown how strangers live, what they keep on their mantelpieces, what they think a tenant ought to put up with, and what they think is worth boasting about. Every landlady has a theory of the world, and most of them will share it if you stand in the doorway long enough. For another, it cheers you up. However dull your own lodgings, there is always a room somewhere that is darker, damper, or more expensive, and seeing it does wonders for your contentment at home.

The search begins, of course, with the newspaper advertisements, which are a literature of their own. They are written in a small stock of flattering words that appear again and again, and a little experience teaches you what each one means. A room described as cosy is small. One described as bright has a window. Airy usually means draughty, and select usually means expensive. [example needed] The words are never quite lies, but they are never quite the truth either, and part of the fun is working out the translation before you climb the stairs.

Of all the rooms I have looked at, the most charming are in the old legal inns of London. These are the courts and buildings where lawyers have lived and worked for generations, tucked away behind the busy streets, with quiet squares, worn staircases and doors painted with the names of their occupants. The rooms have panelled walls, deep window seats and views over lawns and old brick. They feel as if nothing much has changed in them for a very long time.

That is their charm, and it is also their drawback. Nothing much has changed. The comforts that newer buildings take for granted are often missing: [details needed]. You pay for the atmosphere with a certain amount of inconvenience, and some people find the price too high. Even so, they are worth the visit, whether or not you ever sign for one.
