# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-05

## The passage (by the author)

The manner of narration savored of the soil. The Honest Miner under such circumstances would subordinate everything to emphasis on the correct homiletical method. No matter how able the minister might be, it was evident that if he were closely confined to his notes, his delivery could not be effective.

A good woman described the way in which her minister, a young man fresh from the theological school, made one of his first parish calls. He found his parishioner, who had been extolled as one of the pillars of the church, in a state of intoxication, and he was chased out of the house and some distance down the street.

“We were sorry it happened, for it gave him an unpleasant impression of the congregation. You know Mr. ---- met with several rebuffs.”

The unconventional episode was related with all the prim propriety of “Cranford.”

The perfect democracy of a mining camp develops a certain naïve truth-telling, which has all the unexpectedness which belongs to the observations of a boy. There is no attempt to reduce everything to uniformity, or to prove any particular thesis. The gossip of a conventional village where people know each other too well is apt to be malicious. A creditable action is narrated, and then comes the inevitable “but.” The subject of conversation falls in the estimation of the hearers with a sudden thud.

The Honest Miner does not attempt to pass final judgment or to arrange his fellow men according to any sort of classification. He speaks of them as he sees them, and so virtues and failings jostle one another and take no offense. The result is a moral inconsequence which has all the effect of studied wit. This is what delights us in the characterization of Thompson of Angel’s:

Frequently drunk was Thompson, but always polite to the stranger.

## Draft A

The mining settlements produced a kind of storytelling that I have not met elsewhere. It was perfectly candid, and it was entirely without judgment. The narrator told you what had happened, and he left you to decide what to think about it, if indeed there was anything to think.

I remember hearing a parishioner describe the arrival of a new pastor. The pastor had come full of zeal, and he had not been long in the place before one of the elders of the church, having taken more whisky than was good for him, chased him out of town. The parishioner related all this with complete composure. He did not condemn the elder, nor did he pity the pastor. He mentioned that the elder was a good man in most respects and a generous contributor, and that the pastor was a fine speaker who perhaps did not understand the community. [example needed: the parishioner's own words] Then he went on to the next item of news.

In a small town such a story would have been told very differently. There the gossip is a moral censor who enjoys his office; he collects the failings of his neighbours in order to display them, and he takes care that every anecdote ends with a verdict. But in the camps there was a rough equality among men that made this impossible. Nobody was in a position to look down upon anybody else, and nobody cared to try. The result was a frankness of speech that had no spite in it.

The humour of such talk was usually accidental. The narrator set the faults and the merits of his subject side by side, as a shopkeeper might set out his goods, without any attempt to strike a balance. It was the listener who noticed that the balance was absurd. The narrator never did.

## Draft B

Mining settlements produce a particular kind of storytelling, candid and entirely without judgement. You hear it best from the ordinary people of the camp, who talk about what happened in their town the way other people talk about the weather.

Take a parishioner recounting how the camp got through a new pastor. The pastor arrived, began his work, and was soon chased out of town by one of the elders of his own church, who happened to be drunk at the time. [details of the chase needed] The parishioner tells all this quite calmly. There is no outrage in the telling and no particular sympathy either. The pastor came, the elder got drunk, the pastor left. That is what happened, and the teller sees no need to add anything to it.

This frankness comes out of the equality of camp life. In a mining settlement nobody has much standing over anybody else, and nobody has a reputation so delicate that it has to be protected by careful talk. People say what they saw. Compare that with gossip in a settled small town, where the same story would be passed along in whispers, shaped to wound, and loaded with hints about what the listener ought to think of everyone involved. Small-town gossip is spiteful because it is always keeping score. Camp talk is not keeping score at all.

The result is a kind of humour that nobody intends. The storyteller mixes faults and merits in the same breath without ever pausing to weigh them. The elder was a drunk who ran the pastor out of town, and also [merit needed]; the pastor was driven off, and also [merit needed]. All of it is laid side by side and left there, with no verdict attached. It is the absence of a verdict that makes it funny. The listener keeps waiting for the moral, and the teller, who never thought there was one, simply moves on to the next thing.

## Draft C

In the mining camps one heard a kind of storytelling that I have never met with elsewhere. It was entirely candid and entirely without malice. The narrator told what happened, and there he stopped. He did not feel called upon to pass sentence.

I remember a good woman of the congregation who gave me an account of the arrival of a new pastor. She told it in the most matter-of-fact way. The pastor had come full of zeal, and had been received kindly. Then one of the elders of the church, having taken more than was good for him, chased the minister out of the camp. [example needed] She related this as she might have related a change in the weather. The elder was a good man in many ways, she said, and very generous to the church; he had his failings, like the rest of us, and that was one of them. As for the pastor, he was a nice young man, but perhaps he was not suited to the place.

Now in a small town such a story would have been told very differently. It would have been told in a lowered voice, with a relish for the scandal and a keen eye to the damage it might do. The gossip of the village is seldom candid, for it is seldom disinterested. Everybody has a position to keep up, and every fall of a neighbour is a gain to someone.

In the camp there were no such positions to keep up. Men had come from everywhere and were starting even. Where all were equal there was no need to pull anybody down, and so people could afford to speak the plain truth about one another.

The result was a humour that was quite unintended. When faults and virtues are set side by side, and no verdict is given, the effect is irresistibly comic. The narrator does not mean to be funny. She is only being fair. But fairness of this thoroughgoing kind is so rare that it takes us by surprise, and we laugh.
