# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-04

## The passage (by the author)

Turn to the Heimskringla and read how King Olaf converted the pagan bonders.

“So King Olaf went into the God-house and a certain few of his men with him, and a certain few of the bonders. But when the king came whereas the gods were, there sat Thor the most honored of all the gods, adorned with gold and silver. Then King Olaf hove up the gold-wrought rod that he had in his hand and smote Thor that he fell down from the stall; and therewith ran forth all the king’s men and tumbled down all the gods from their stalls. But whiles the king was in the God-house was Iron-Skeggi slain without, even at the very door, and that deed did the king’s men. So when the king was come back to his folk he bade the bonders take one of two things, either all be christened, or else abide the brunt of battle with him. But after the death of Skeggi there was no leader among the folk of the bonders to raise up a banner against King Olaf. So the choice was taken of them to go to the king and obey his bidding. Then King Olaf christened all folk that were there and took hostages of the bonders that they would hold to their christening. Thereafter King Olaf caused men of his wend over all parts of Thrandheim; and now spoke no man against the faith of Christ. And so were all folk christened in the country-side.”

That is the way the nations of the north were first christianized. What is the difference between Thor and the Christ? the simple-hearted people would ask. “The difference,” said King Olaf, “is very fundamental, and it requires little theological training to see it. It is this: the Christ is stronger. If you don’t believe it, I’ll”--but they did believe it.

## Draft A

The saga tells the story of the conversion with a directness that the modern missionary would hardly care to imitate. The king arrived among the farmers and summoned them to an assembly, where he urged upon them the claims of the new faith. The farmers were not persuaded. They had their own gods, and the gods had served them tolerably well. They saw no reason to exchange a familiar arrangement for one recommended by a stranger, however royal. The king thereupon entered the temple, struck down the idols, and left them lying in pieces before their astonished worshippers, while his men killed the local leader who had spoken most boldly for the old religion.

This was a theological argument of a kind the farmers could understand. Without a leader they had no one to organise resistance, and without resistance there was nothing to be done but submit. The king offered them a simple choice between baptism and battle. They chose baptism, and the saga records that they were duly received into the Church. It says nothing of any instruction in doctrine, and I should suppose that none was thought necessary.

We are accustomed to think of conversion as a change of heart, brought about by preaching and by the slow persuasion of example. That is not how the North was converted. The northern peoples respected strength above everything else, and a god who could not protect his own images was evidently not a god worth serving; the new faith recommended itself less by what it taught than by what its champion could do. Doctrine came later, if it came at all, and when it came it found the ground already cleared for it by the axe. It was might, openly displayed, that carried the day.

## Draft B

The old sagas do not pretend that the North was won for the new faith by argument. One of them tells how a Norse king set out to bring the farmers of a district over to Christianity, and the method it describes has very little to do with preaching.

The king came to the place where the farmers kept their idols. He did not debate the matter with them. He had the images toppled, pulled down in front of the people who worshipped them, so that everyone could see their gods lying broken on the ground and doing nothing about it. At the same time his men killed a local leader, the man the farmers would have looked to for direction and, if it came to it, for resistance. [name needed]

That killing settled the matter. Without their leader the farmers had no one to organise them and no one to speak for them. The king offered them a plain choice: be baptised, or face war. They were in no position to fight, and they knew it. So they gave in and accepted baptism, not because they had been persuaded of anything, but because the alternative was worse.

It would be a mistake to treat this as an unusual or especially brutal case. It fits a pattern. Conversion in the North rested on displayed might rather than on doctrine. The new religion won because it came with the stronger king, and the proof of its truth was that its champion could break the old gods and kill their defenders without being struck down. Anyone watching could draw the conclusion without a sermon to help them.

Teaching, where it came at all, came afterwards. First the idols fell, then the leader, then the people were baptised. The doctrine had to catch up later with those it had already claimed.

## Draft C

The saga tells the story with a directness that leaves little to the imagination. The king [name needed] came among the farmers who still clung to the old gods. He did not argue with them. He went into the place where their idols stood and threw them down, and while the farmers were considering this affront, his men struck down the chief who had been their spokesman. [example needed]

The farmers were now in an awkward position. They had come prepared to defend their gods, but it is hard to defend anything without someone to tell you how. The man who had been accustomed to speak for them lay dead, and there was no one ready to take his place. The king, on his part, was quite willing to make the choice easy. They might be baptized, or they might have war. They chose baptism, and the saga records it without any sign of surprise.

It is not a very edifying story, if we look for edification of the ordinary kind. There is no account of a missionary reasoning with the heathen, no struggle of a soul between two faiths, no moment of illumination. The farmers were not convinced; they were overpowered. What passed for conversion was in reality a change of allegiance, such as might take place between one chieftain and another.

And yet we should misread the North if we were too much scandalized. The men of that time had a very simple test of the worth of a god. A god was to be judged by what he could do for his followers. If the old gods could not keep their images standing, it was a fair inference that they were not so strong as had been supposed. The new faith came to them, not as a body of doctrine to be examined, but as a display of power to be acknowledged. It won its way in the North by the same argument that had always prevailed there, which was the argument of the stronger arm.
