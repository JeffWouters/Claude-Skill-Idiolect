# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-09

## The passage (by the author)

In regard to the lie of exaggeration, a word should perhaps parenthetically be said. We all know the child who has seen two elephants in the garden eating the roses. We also know the delightful grown-up who “embroiders” his narratives. He will never tell the same adventure twice with the same details. The fact remains that he may each time leave you with precisely the same impression of the adventure in its entirety. It is quite possible that you trust him exceedingly. Of course it is also possible that his ben trovato is never vero. You will have to determine after long experience of him whether he is fundamentally false, or merely has a sense of style. Personally, I know exaggerators of both kinds: people whose lies are only picturesque adjectives, and people whose picturesque adjectives are only lies. There is a subtle distinction between the two. At the risk of being at loggerheads with the rhetoricians, one must say that truth goes deeper than words, and that there is not much in a truthfulness which is only phrase-deep.

The old ladies who are shivering on the back porch will disapprove of me for saying these things, almost as much as I disapprove of them for being on the back porch. To speak frankly, I have not found that the people who cling to the letter are always the people who cling to the spirit of the law. Some of the men and women who will not say in so many words the thing which is not, will deliberately give a false impression. They are not the servants of truth; they are the parasites of truth. The ladies I have referred to may be technically “out”; but they are really “out” only to the undesired visitor--exactly as much as if they had stopped in their own sitting-rooms. (Remember, please, that I am not speaking of the people who receive the unwelcome caller rather than permit a maid to fib--they are in a very different case.) I should not instinctively go to these people for an accurate account of a serious situation. Any one whose conscience is satisfied with that kind of loyalty to fact knows very little about the spirit of truth.

## Draft A

We usually think of lying as a simple matter: a person says something he knows to be false, and that is a lie. But there are two kinds of deception that sit awkwardly with this definition, and they pull in opposite directions. One is exaggeration. The other is the deception that never says anything untrue.

Take exaggeration first. Every good storyteller embellishes. The fish grows a little in the telling, the storm grows a little louder, the rude stranger a little ruder. Strictly speaking, each of these touches is false. Yet the listener who hears the story often comes away with an accurate picture of what happened: that the fish was large, the storm alarming, the stranger unpleasant. The details have been heightened, but the overall impression is true, and it may even be truer than a flat recital of facts would have been, because it conveys how the thing felt. We do not usually call such a storyteller a liar, and I think we are right not to. [example needed]

This suggests something important about honesty. It concerns meaning, not only the literal wording. What matters is the picture that forms in the other person's mind, and whether we have tried to make that picture accurate or to make it false. Words are the means; understanding is the end.

Which brings us to the second kind of deception, and to the people who practise it. There are those who pride themselves on never telling an outright falsehood. They choose their words with great care, so that every sentence can be defended as literally correct, and they use those sentences to lead others to believe what is not so. They answer a question that was not asked. They let a wrong assumption stand. They say something true in such a way that it will be misread, and then, when challenged, point out that they said nothing false.

Such people think of themselves as scrupulous. In fact they understand truth poorly. They have mistaken its outward form for its substance, and kept the letter of honesty while abandoning its purpose. The embellishing storyteller, who bends the words to serve an accurate picture, is closer to the truth than they are, for they bend an accurate set of words to serve a false one.

## Draft B

There are liars and liars, and not all of them tell lies. Consider, first, the born story-teller--the man who cannot recount the smallest incident of his day without improving it a little. The fish grows longer, the insult grows sharper, the narrow escape grows narrower with every telling. Strictly speaking, he is not telling the truth; and the strict moralist, who perhaps has never told a good story in his life, will say at once that he is lying. Yet it often happens that his story, embellished as it is, gives a more accurate picture of what really occurred than a sober and literal account would give. He has exaggerated the details in order to convey the effect, and the effect is true. His hearers know, as a rule, what he is about, and they make allowances for it as they would make allowances for a painter who has heightened the colours of a sunset in order that we might see it. Nobody is deceived.

The point is that honesty concerns meaning, not merely the literal wording. A statement is true or false according to the impression it is meant to leave in the mind of the person who hears it; and by that test the embellisher may be a far more honest man than he looks. The test might, however, cut the other way, too.

For there is another kind of person, much more common and much less amusing, who prides himself on never having told a lie in his life. He never has. He is scrupulous about every word; he would not, for the world, say anything that was not literally and technically correct. But he is perfectly willing to arrange his correct statements so that they mislead. He will tell you the truth, and nothing but the truth, and he will take very good care not to tell you the whole of it. He will let you draw the wrong conclusion and then point out, if you reproach him, that he never said so. Such a man, I fancy, understands truth very poorly indeed. He has mistaken the letter for the spirit, and he has kept the letter so carefully only because it allows him to betray the spirit the more safely, and with a rather better conscience than the honest liar can ever hope to enjoy. Of the two, give me the story-teller. He may lie in his words; he does not lie in his intentions, and it is the intention that counts.

## Draft C

There are two kinds of lying which are seldom called by that name, and which seem to me worth a moment's attention. The first is exaggeration; the second is the deception that keeps carefully within the letter of the truth. They are commonly lumped together, as though they were equally venial or equally wicked. I think they are nothing of the sort.

Take the born story-teller. He cannot recount the smallest adventure without improving it. The dog that barked at him becomes a dog that very nearly had him by the throat; the dull dinner becomes a dinner of positively heroic dullness. [example needed] He is, in the strict sense, a liar; he says a great many things that did not happen. And yet, oddly enough, his hearers very often come away with a truer notion of the occasion than they would have had from a sober witness. He has heightened the colours, but he has not changed the picture. The dinner really was dull; the dog really was alarming. What he conveys, in the end, is accurate--more accurate, perhaps, than the bare record.

For honesty, after all, is a matter of meaning, not merely of words. We do not tell the truth by stringing together sentences each of which can be verified; we tell the truth by leaving in the mind of the listener an impression that corresponds to the facts. That is the whole test. A man may use nothing but true statements and yet be a thorough liar; and a man may use a good many false ones and yet, in the larger sense, be honest.

Which brings me to the second kind--and I confess I have much less patience with it. There are people (it is always the most scrupulous-looking people!) who would not, for the world, utter a downright falsehood. They never say the thing that is not. But they arrange the things that are so cunningly that you come away believing something entirely false; and they know, perfectly well, that you will. They pride themselves on their truthfulness; I cannot see why. They have kept the letter and broken the spirit, which is precisely the opposite of what the story-teller does; and of the two, I would a great deal rather dine with the story-teller. Such people have, I think, a very poor understanding of what truth is. They have mistaken it for a form of words--and truth was never that.
