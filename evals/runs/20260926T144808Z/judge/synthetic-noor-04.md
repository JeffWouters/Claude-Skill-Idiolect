# Judge packet synthetic-noor-04

## The passage (by the author)

Every October the heating comes back on. And every October, the radiators knock.

It starts in the night. A tick, then a knock, then a long gurgle like a drain. The one in the back bedroom is the worst. It sounds like someone walking in the wall.

So on the first cold Saturday I bleed them. All nine. It takes about an hour.

The tool is a small brass key. It costs a few euros and it is the one tool I always lose. I keep three now. One on a hook in the boiler cupboard. One in the kitchen drawer. One in the toolbox. I can usually find one of the three.

First I turn the heating on and let it run for 20 minutes. The radiators get hot. Then I feel each one with my hand. Flat palm, top to bottom. A radiator with air in it is hot at the bottom and cold at the top. You can feel the line where the heat stops. On the back bedroom one, the line is halfway down.

Then I turn the heating off. This is the step people skip. Bleed a radiator with the pump running and you pull in more air. Or you get a jet of hot black water in the face. I have had both.

I wait ten minutes for things to settle. I make a coffee.

So then I start at the bottom of the house and work up. Downstairs first, then the landing, then the bedrooms. Air rises. If you start at the top, the air from below comes up after you.

At each radiator, the same steps.

Put an old cloth under the valve. The valve is a small square nut at one top corner. Fit the key. Hold a second cloth round the key. Then turn it a quarter turn anticlockwise. Slowly.

## Draft A

The pipes start to knock every autumn. Not all at once. A tick here, a gurgle there. Then a real bang in the night.

That is the signal. There is air in the radiators. So once a year, I bleed them.

Here is how I go about it.

First the test. I wait until the heating has been on for a while. Then I go round the house with my hand flat on each radiator. Bottom first. Then the top. A radiator with air in it is warm at the bottom and cool at the top. You can feel the line where the warm stops. If the top is cool, there is air. If it is warm all over, leave it.

I make a list as I go. [number needed, how many radiators need it most years].

Then the switch. I turn the heating off. Not down. Off. And I let it cool. Hot water under pressure is not something to open a valve on. This is the step I am most tempted to skip. I do not skip it.

Then the order. Ground floor first. Then upstairs. I start with the radiator on the list that is lowest in the house and work up from there. [example needed, why this order works in this house].

At each radiator I hold a cloth under the small valve at the top corner. I fit the key. Then I open it. Gently. A quarter turn is plenty. Not a full turn. If you open it hard, it spits. You want a slow hiss. Then you wait.

The hiss is the air leaving. It can take a little while. I stand there with the cloth and listen.

So that is the first half of the job. Knock, feel, switch off, and work up the house one valve at a time.

Tonight the house is quiet. The heating is off. There is a cloth on the landing and the key is in my pocket.

## Draft B

Every autumn, when the heating comes back on after the summer, the pipes start to make noise. Gurgling, knocking, the occasional trickling sound behind the walls. By now I recognise it as the signal that it is time for an annual job: releasing the air that has become trapped in the radiators.

Air in a radiator gets in the way of the hot water. It collects at the top, and the result is a radiator that is only partly doing its job. The easiest way to detect it is by touch. With the heating running, feel along each radiator from bottom to top. If the lower part is warm but the upper sections are noticeably cooler, there is air trapped inside. Going round the house this way tells me which radiators need attention and which can be left alone.

Before opening anything, I switch the system off. This is important. If the pump is still running, it keeps moving water and air around, which makes the job less effective, and a hot system means hot water coming out of the valve. It is better to let everything settle first. [example needed: how long to wait after switching off]

Then I work through the house in a set order, starting on the ground floor and moving upward. Beginning at the bottom makes sense because air rises through the system, so doing the upper floors last gives it the best chance to be caught where it ends up.

At each radiator I open the bleed valve gently. There is no need to force it or open it wide. A small turn is enough to let the air start escaping with a hiss. Going slowly keeps things under control, and it means I can react quickly when the hissing changes, which is the sign that the air is nearly out. I keep a cloth close by to catch any drips. Radiator by radiator, floor by floor, the house gets a little quieter as I go.

## Draft C

Every autumn the pipes start to talk. The heating comes on for the first time in months and the whole house knocks and gurgles. A trickling sound in the walls. A tick in the radiator under the window. That is air. It has crept into the system over the summer and now it is in the way.

So once a year I bleed the radiators. It is a small job. It is also easy to do badly.

First find out which ones need it. Put the heating on and let it warm up. Then go round and feel each radiator. Run your hand along the top, then lower down. A radiator full of water is warm all over. A radiator with air in it is warm at the bottom and cool at the top. Sometimes the top strip is stone cold. That is the one you want. Make a note. I go round with a scrap of paper. [example needed]

Then switch the system off. This matters. If the pump is running it pushes water round and can pull more air in. It can also push hot water out of the valve at you. Let it cool down a while. Hot radiators and bare hands are a bad mix.

You need a radiator key. A small brass one. Keep it somewhere you will find it next year. I did not, once, and spent an evening looking. And a cloth. And something to catch drips.

Start on the ground floor. Work upward. Air rises, so the upper rooms are where it collects last. If you start at the top you only have to go back and do it again.

At each radiator the valve is at one end, at the top. Fit the key. Hold the cloth under it. Then turn it slowly. Just a little. You will hear a hiss. That is the air coming out. Do not open it wide. It does not come out any faster and you risk losing the little screw. Just let it hiss.
