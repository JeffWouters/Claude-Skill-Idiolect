# Judge packet synthetic-noor-10

## The passage (by the author)

I have a pair of wool socks that are 12 years old. They have been darned nine times. I can see every repair.

They were grey when I bought them. Now they are grey with patches of green, brown and one of bright red. The red was all I had that night. I do not regret it.

Darning is not hard. It is slow. That is different.

You need three things. A darning needle, which is long with a big eye. Wool, close to the weight of the sock. And something round to hold the sock tight while you work. There are wooden darning mushrooms made for this. I use a light bulb. An old one, dead, the big round kind. It fits inside the heel just right.

So here is how I do it.

First I turn the sock the right way out and find the hole. Heels go first on my socks. Then the ball of the foot. Then the big toe. The hole is rarely one clean hole. There is a hole, and around it a wide ring of thin, worn wool. You have to darn the thin part as well. Or next month the hole is back, one ring wider.

I slip the bulb in and pull the sock tight over it. Then I run a line of small stitches round the thin part, about one centimetre out from the edge. This marks the patch. It also stops it from spreading.

Then I lay the warp. That is a set of long stitches going one way, top to bottom, across the hole. Side by side. Close together. Each one picks up a little of the good wool at the top and bottom. On a hole the size of a coin, that is about 20 lines.

It looks like the strings of a small harp.

## Draft A

I mend my old woollen socks by hand. It is a small skill and does not need much, but it does need the right things.

The tools are simple. First, a suitable needle, one with an eye large enough for yarn and a point that goes between the stitches without splitting them. Second, matching yarn, ideally close to the original in both colour and thickness, so the repair wears the same way as the rest of the sock. Third, a rounded support to stretch the fabric over while you work. Anything with a smooth, rounded surface will do. A spent light bulb works well. [example needed: what I actually use as a support] The support keeps the fabric in its natural shape so you do not accidentally sew it shut.

The most important lesson is to repair the weakened area around the hole, not just the opening itself. By the time a hole appears, the fabric around it is usually thin and worn too. If you only close the opening, the next hole will appear right next to your repair, often within a few wears. So I look carefully at the area, feeling for the thin spots, and include all of it in the darn.

Once I know how far the weakness goes, I outline the area. I run a line of small stitches around it, a little way into the sound fabric, to mark the boundary of the repair. That outline gives me a clear shape to work within and helps anchor everything that follows.

Then I lay parallel lengthwise threads across the area. Starting at one side of the outline, I take the yarn across to the other side, pick up a little of the fabric, and come back again, row after row, keeping the threads parallel and evenly spaced. They should lie across the hole without being pulled tight. These threads form the foundation. Once they are in place, the next step is to weave crosswise through them, and slowly the patch will take shape.

## Draft B

Most of my socks are wool. Old ones, some of them. They wear through at the heel and the ball of the foot. I used to throw them out. Now I mend them.

You need three things. A needle first. A darning needle, with a blunt point and a big eye. A sharp one splits the wool. Then yarn. Match it to the sock as best you can. Wool with wool, and about the same thickness. Too thick and you get a lump. Too thin and it wears through again fast. Colour matters less. Then something round to stretch the sock over. There are wooden darning mushrooms made for it. An old light bulb works just as well. A dead one, of course. It fits inside the heel and holds the fabric smooth and taut. [example needed]

Push the bulb in so the hole sits on top. Look at it.

The hole is not the problem. The hole is only the worst of it. Around it the wool has gone thin. You can see light through it. If you just close the hole, the thin part goes next week. Then you have a new hole right next to the mend. So you mend the whole weak patch. Out to where the wool feels strong again.

I mark that area first. Not with a pen. I run a line of small running stitches all round it, a little way into the good wool. A loose box around the damage. That is the edge. Everything inside it gets rebuilt.

Then the lengthwise threads. Start at one side of the box. Take the yarn from the top edge to the bottom edge, down the length of the sock. Catch a stitch in the good wool at each end. Then come back up, a thread's width along. Parallel lines, close together, side by side. Across the hole they float in the air. Across the thin part they lie on top.

Keep going until the box is full.
