# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-01

## The passage (by the author)

And all time past, was it all for this? Times unforgotten, and treasures of things?

What is the use of throwing great museums open to the people, if you tell them at the same time that to possess the contents of the museums would not make a private person happier? Why should there be cordons bleus in the world, if we ought to live on bread and milk? Above all, why have we praised, through the centuries, all the slow processes, the tardy consummations, of perfection, if raw material, either in art or life, is really best? I recall at this instant a friend of mine who expresses her democracy in her footwear. Her frocks are as charming as money can induce Paquin to make them; but if her frocks are an insult to the poor, her boots are an insult to the rich. I have seen her walk to a garden-party, in real lace, and out at heel. She fancied, I think, that her inadequate boots obliterated the deplorable social distinction between herself and her cook. In point of fact, her cook would not have condescended to them; would not have considered herself a “lady” if she had.

I have other friends who feel strongly the ignominy of personal service: who agree with many ignorant young women that it is more dignified to be a bullied, insulted, underpaid shop-girl with a rhinestone sunburst, than a well-paid, highly-respected parlor-maid in a uniform. Accordingly, they conscientiously deprive themselves of the parlor-maid, and spend her wages in trying to get a vote for the shop-girl. I do not understand their distinctions in liberty, or their definition of degradation. The parlor-maid at least can choose the mistress, but the shop-girl cannot choose the floor-walker.

I am, myself, essentially an undomestic woman, and I dislike the parlor-maid’s tasks to the point of feeling excessive irritation at having, occasionally, in this mad world, to perform them. But, seriously speaking, apart from the temperamental quirk, I would don her clothes and follow officially her career, rather than that parlor-maids in uniforms should pass wholly from the world. It is as if these people said, “Since those who are parlor-maids themselves cannot very well employ parlor-maids, then let no one have a parlor-maid.” Their factitious altruism, with all its peril, might be forgiven them; but the misguided creatures (who are human beings and egotists, after all, and as such must “save their face”) go on to say that it is really much nicer not to have parlor-maids. And that lie is unpardonable, for it strikes at the root of human experience. Parlor-maids would never have become a convention if they had not been found desirable.

## Draft A

I know a woman--a very rich woman, as these things go--who has taken to wearing shoes that are frankly worn out. She does not wear them because she likes them, or because she cannot afford others; she wears them, so far as I can make out, as a sort of public confession of faith. They are meant to say that she is no better than her neighbours, and that she has renounced, for the sake of equality, the vanities of her class. I have yet to meet anyone who was deceived by them. The shop-keeper is not deceived, nor the neighbour, nor (I strongly suspect) the woman herself. Everybody knows perfectly well what is in her bank-book; and the shoes merely add a touch of the theatrical to a situation which was, before, only mildly embarrassing. Equality is not served by a shabby heel.

Now it is the same with the larger renunciations. We are told, in many quarters, that the self-respecting modern household ought to do without servants; that to be waited on is to degrade the person who waits. Let us admit, by all means, that some forms of service have been degrading, and that some mistresses have been intolerable. But there, I think, the concession ends. Is domestic service, in itself, more humiliating than standing all day behind a counter, at the mercy of every customer who chooses to be rude? I cannot see that it is. The girl in a decent household has, very often, more privacy, more consideration, and more say in the ordering of her own work than the girl in the shop; she may leave when she likes, and she may pick her place. She has, in short, a choice--and choice, I take it, is the beginning of dignity. The shop-girl (it is always the shop-girl who is held up to us as emancipated!) has frequently a great deal less of it. [example needed]

But the most curious thing about the whole business is the insistence that life is actually pleasanter without such help. People who have given up their cooks and their refinements assure us, with a certain glassy brightness, that they have never been so happy. I do not believe them; and I do not believe that, in their hearts, they believe themselves. Experience has shown, for a good many generations, that leisure and order and a well-served table make for a more civilised existence, not a less civilised one. To deny that is not humility; it is merely a refusal to look at the evidence. One may give up one's comforts, if one must. One need not pretend, into the bargain, that the loss is a gain.

## Draft B

I know a woman of considerable fortune who has lately taken to wearing worn-out shoes. She does it on principle; she wishes the world to understand that she holds herself no better than her neighbours, and that her money is, so to speak, an accident she would rather not mention. The world understands nothing of the kind. Her cook sees the shoes, and so does her chauffeur, and so, certainly, does the girl who comes in by the day to do the mending. Not one of them is deceived. They know perfectly well what her income is, and they know that the shoes are a costume, assumed for their benefit and quite possibly resented by them. One does not become a sister to the poor by dressing up as a poor relation. Nobody is fooled.

There is a notion abroad that to keep servants at all is a sin against democracy. Granted, the old relation of mistress and maid had its abuses; but it is not clear to me that the girl behind a shop counter is any better off. She stands all day at the pleasure of strangers who do not know her name (and do not wish to know it!), and she has no more security than her employer chooses to give her. The girl in a decent household is in another case. She has a recognised place; she has a mistress who knows her, and a skill that is valued, respected, and paid for as a skill. She has some choice, too, for a good servant can leave one house and walk into another. I do not say that domestic service is paradise. My point is only this: it can offer more dignity--and more genuine freedom of choice--than the work the reformers are so eager to push her into. The shop is not a promotion.

The last word of these people is always that we should be happier without help. We should be simpler, they say, and freer, and nearer to the fundamental realities of existence. Well: a good many of us have tried it, whether we chose to or not. We have cooked our own dinners and swept our own floors, and we have discovered that the dinners were worse, the floors no cleaner, and the temper of the household considerably shorter. And we have discovered, besides, that there was no time left for the reading and the conversation and the quiet thinking which were, after all, the whole point of being civilised. That is what experience has shown. To say that life is better without such help is not humility; it is merely a refusal to look at the evidence. I fancy most of the people who say it have never tried. But the refinements we are asked to surrender are not luxuries in the vulgar sense; they are the conditions on which a certain kind of life is possible--and the shabby shoes, however conscientiously worn, do nothing whatever to change that.

## Draft C

There is a fashion, among people who can well afford otherwise, for giving up the small refinements of life and the household help that makes them possible, and for calling this sacrifice a service to equality. I think the fashion is mistaken, and I think most of the people who follow it know, somewhere below their principles, that it is.

Consider a wealthy acquaintance of mine who has taken to wearing worn-out shoes. He wears them deliberately, as other men wear a club tie, to show which side he is on. The trouble is that nobody is fooled. The shopkeeper knows who he is, the porter knows who he is, and the people he means to stand beside know better than anyone. A rich man in shabby shoes is still a rich man; he has merely added a small theatrical falsehood to his wardrobe. His shoes help no one, and they tell a story that nobody believes.

The same confusion runs through the argument against domestic service. We are told that to employ someone in the house is to humble them, and that a decent society would let such work die out. But compare it honestly with the work that is supposed to replace it. A clerk behind a shop counter stands for long hours, answers to a manager she rarely sees, repeats the same few phrases to strangers who do not look at her, and goes home with nothing made and nothing mastered. A capable cook or housekeeper in a good household has a craft, a measure of authority over her own domain, a known employer who depends on her, and often a good deal more say over how her day is arranged. There is dignity in being needed for what you do well. There is choice, too, when a skilled servant can leave one house for another that values her more. I do not say every household is a good one; many are not. I say only that the counter is not self-evidently the nobler place, and that those who close the one door rarely ask what lies behind the other. [example needed]

Then there is the claim, made with a kind of cheerful defiance, that life is actually better without such help: simpler, cleaner, more honest. This is the part I find hardest to accept, because it asks us to deny what experience has plainly shown. Every generation that has had the chance to hand off drudgery has taken it, and has used the time for something else: reading, talking, raising children, making things. To pretend that scrubbing and fetching are secretly the good life is not humility. It is a refusal to learn from the evidence of our own households.

Equality is a serious aim. It deserves serious means, not old shoes and a cold kitchen.
