# Judge packet synthetic-noor-03

## The passage (by the author)

There is a list on my fridge. There has been a list on my fridge for 15 years. Not the same list. But always one.

It is held up by a magnet shaped like a lemon. The paper is A5, cut from an old pad. I write in pencil. Pencil can be rubbed out. And pencil does not run when the fridge door drips.

The list has three parts. I drew the lines with a ruler.

The top part is food. What we are out of. Milk, eggs, bread, the usual. Anyone in the house can add to it. The rule is: if you finish it, you write it.

The middle part is jobs. Small ones. Things that take less than an hour. Fix the loose hinge on the bathroom cupboard. Change the bulb on the stairs. Oil the back gate. Clean the filter in the washing machine.

The bottom part is big jobs. Things that need a day, or money, or help. Paint the hall. Clear the loft. Get the chimney swept.

So that is the system. It is not clever. But it works, and I have tried a lot of systems.

I have tried apps. Three of them. Each one worked for a month. Then I stopped opening it. An app is out of sight. A list on the fridge is not. You see it every time you want a drink of water. Eight or ten times a day. It nags you without saying a word.

I have tried a notebook. Better. But the notebook lived in a drawer. Same problem.

I have tried a whiteboard. It was good for a year. Then the pens dried out. And someone wiped it by leaning on it. I lost a whole week of jobs to the back of a coat.

So now it is paper and pencil.

## Draft B

Our household to-do system is not clever. It is a sheet of paper on the refrigerator, and it works better than anything else we have tried.

The sheet is split into three parts. The first is groceries: whatever runs out gets written down, by whoever notices it. The second is quick chores, the small jobs that take a few minutes but somehow never get done unless someone writes them down. The third is larger projects, the things that take an afternoon, a weekend, or several attempts. Keeping those three apart matters. When everything sits in one long list, the quick jobs get buried under the big ones, and the big ones start to feel like a permanent reproach. Split up, each section has its own pace.

What makes it succeed is visibility. The refrigerator is the one place in the house everyone passes several times a day. You cannot open it without seeing the list. That is exactly where the phone apps we tried failed. They were perfectly capable, with reminders, shared lists and all the rest, but they lived inside a phone, and a phone is full of other things demanding attention. Out of sight really did mean out of mind. A task in an app was only seen when someone decided to open the app, and mostly nobody did.

Before settling on the paper sheet, we tried other physical options too. Notebooks came first. The trouble was that a notebook gets closed, put in a drawer or carried off to another room, and then it is as invisible as the app. After that came a whiteboard. That seemed promising, since it was always visible, but it had its own problems. [example needed: why the whiteboard was abandoned] In the end we abandoned both.

So now it is paper and a magnet. Someone writes, someone crosses off, and when the sheet is full, we start a new one. It is not sophisticated, but it gets looked at, and a list that gets looked at is a list that gets done.

## Draft C

The list lives on the fridge. One sheet of paper, held up by a magnet. That is the whole system.

It is split into three parts. Groceries at the top. Quick chores in the middle. Larger projects at the bottom. Groceries are things we have run out of, or are about to. Quick chores are things that take a few minutes. Change the bulb on the landing. Post the letter. Wipe the shelf in the fridge. Larger projects are the ones that take a weekend, or a trip to the shop, or a phone call I keep putting off. Anyone can add to any part. Anyone can cross things off.

It works because you cannot miss it. You open the fridge a dozen times a day. The list is there every time. You see what is left. You see that nobody has bought milk. You see that the gutter job has been sitting at the bottom for weeks. It nags without saying anything.

We tried phone apps first. Several. They were clever. They synced and sent reminders. Nobody opened them. A list you have to go and look for is a list you forget. The reminders came at the wrong moment and got swiped away. The apps are still on my phone, I think. Empty.

Then a notebook. That was my idea. A proper one, kept in the kitchen drawer. It lasted [duration needed]. The drawer was the problem. Out of sight, out of mind. And the notebook wandered. It ended up in a bag, or under the post, or in the car.

Then a whiteboard. That was better. It was on the wall and it was big. But the pens dried out. Then someone wiped half of it by leaning on it. Then we stopped trusting it. We took it down.

So, paper. Cheap, plain, and always in front of us. When a sheet is full I start a new one. It is the dullest system in the house. It is the only one that has lasted.
