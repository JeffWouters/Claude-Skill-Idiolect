# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-01

## The passage (by the author)

This is a free country, and the cynic must be allowed his fling. But if he has license to speak his mind in regard to the simple-hearted people who believe in Peace, we must be privileged to say what we think of him. The truth is that we think him to be a rather shallow-pated fellow who has been educated above his deserts. For all his knowing ways he has had but little knowledge of the world. He has seen the things which are obvious, the things that are shown to every outsider. He prides himself on his familiarity with accomplished facts, not realizing that these belong to the world that is passing away. The interesting things to see are those which belong to the world that is in process of becoming. These are not visible from the seat of the scornful.

The sweeping accusation of hypocrisy against men or nations whenever an incongruity is perceived between a professed purpose and an actual achievement is an indication of too great simplicity of mind. It is the simplicity that is characteristic of one without experience in the work of creation.

The cynic, perceiving the shortcomings of those who “profess and call themselves Christians,” greets their professions with a bitter laugh. He cannot tolerate their pretensions, and he urges them to return to a frank profession of the paganism which their deeds proclaim. Now it is eminently desirable that all who profess and call themselves Christians should be Christians,--but that takes time. The profession is the first step; that puts a whip into the hand of conscience. Not only do a man’s friends, but particularly his enemies, insist that he shall live up to his name. It is a wholesome discipline. In a new country two or three houses set down in a howling wilderness are denominated a city. It is a mere name at first, but if all goes well other metropolitan features are added in due time. I remember a most interesting visit which I once made to a university in a new commonwealth. The university consisted of a board of regents, an unfenced bit of prairie for a “campus,” a president (who was also professor of the Arts and Sciences), a janitor, and two unfinished buildings. A number of the village children took courses which, if persisted in for a number of years, might lead to what is usually termed the Higher Education. One student from out of town dwelt in solitary state in the dormitory. The president met me with great cordiality, and after showing me “the plant” introduced me to the student. It was evident that they were on terms of great intimacy, and that discipline in the university was an easy matter, owing to the fact that the student body was homogeneous.

## Draft A

There is a certain type of critic who takes a peculiar delight in the believer in peace. He points to the armies and the navies, to treaties broken and wars declared, and invites us to observe that the nations which talk most eloquently of brotherhood are precisely the nations most ready to fight. The believer, he concludes, is either a fool or a fraud. Since he is evidently not a fool, he must be a fraud.

I have no wish to be unjust to the scoffer. He has a firm grasp of the facts, and the facts are on his side. He knows what has been done and what is being done, and he can recite the military budgets of the great powers with the accuracy of a clerk who has never been asked what the figures are for. What he does not know is what is going to be done. His knowledge is complete, but it is complete only because it stops at the precise point where the interesting part begins.

But consider the charge of fraud a little more closely. It rests upon the discovery that there is a gap between what men profess and what they practise, and the scoffer announces this discovery as though it were his own invention. Every man who has attempted to build anything, whether a house or a business or a character, made it long before him and made it every morning. There is always a plan, and there is always a structure that falls short of the plan. To call the difference a fraud is merely to reveal that one has never built anything at all. The architect is not a liar because the scaffolding is still up.

The truth is that an ideal, once it has been publicly declared, does not remain idle. It begins to work upon the conscience of the man who declared it, and it continues working long after he would gladly have dismissed it. He does not live up to it to-day, nor to-morrow, and possibly he never will; but he cannot entirely forget it, and it has an embarrassing habit of appearing at inconvenient moments to inquire what he means by his conduct. Conscience is a patient creditor.

I am reminded of the colleges that were founded on the frontier in the early days, when a few earnest men would erect a building, give it an imposing name, and open its doors to learning and to whatever pupil might wander in. Sometimes there was only one. The scoffer of that period could easily have ridiculed the institution. Here was a college, and where were the scholars? Yet the founders were not impostors. They were declaring what they intended to have, and eventually they had it.

So it is with the ideal of peace. It has at present only one solitary pupil in each nation, and he is not a particularly promising one. But the college is open, and the name is over the door. The scoffer may keep his facts; the believer is content with the future.

## Draft B

There is a certain kind of critic who enjoys nothing so much as catching a believer in peace with blood on the nation's hands. He points to the armies, the treaties broken, the wars fought by people who sing hymns about brotherhood on Sunday, and he declares the whole business a fraud. The ideal, he says, is a mask. Take it off and you find the same old appetite for conquest underneath.

It is worth answering him, because he is not stupid. He sees clearly what has already happened. He can list the battles and the broken promises, and he is usually right about them. What he cannot see is what is still happening. He knows the settled facts and misses the ones still forming. He reads a nation as though it were a finished book, when it is a manuscript with most of its pages still blank.

His real mistake is about how anything gets built. He treats the gap between what people say they believe and what they actually do as proof of bad faith. But that gap is simply what building looks like. Every house has a stage when the plans are drawn and the ground is still bare. Every reform has a long season when the law says one thing and the street does another. To call that gap a fraud is to show that you have never tried to make anything yourself. Anyone who has started a business, raised a child or learned a trade knows that intention always runs ahead of performance. That is not hypocrisy. It is the order in which things happen.

A declared ideal does real work even while it is being betrayed. Once people have said out loud that peace is what they want, the saying stays with them. It becomes a standard they can be measured against, and more to the point, a standard they begin to measure themselves against. The first time a nation goes to war after declaring for peace, few may feel uneasy. The next time, more will. Conscience is slow, but it is stirred by words people cannot take back.

Think of the college founded on the frontier with a grand name, a grand charter and exactly one pupil. The scoffer would laugh at it, and in a narrow sense he would be right: one student is not a university. But the founders were not lying. They were declaring what they meant to build and committing themselves to building it. The charter came first because it had to. The buildings, the teachers and the crowds of students came later, if they came at all, and they came because someone had named the thing before it existed. [example needed]

The ideal of peace is in the same position. It has its charter and its handful of pupils. Mocking it for being small is easy. Seeing what it may become takes more patience than the scoffer usually has.

## Draft C

There is a certain kind of critic who takes great delight in exposing the believers in peace. He has caught them, he thinks, in the very act. They talk of brotherhood and they pay their taxes for battleships. They preach good will on one day of the week and read the war news with lively interest on the other six. What is this but fraud? And he goes about with the air of a man who has looked behind the scenes and found the painted canvas and the ropes.

I do not quarrel with his facts. He is an excellent observer of whatever has already come to pass. Show him a thing that is finished, with its corners squared and its accounts closed, and he will tell you exactly what it is. His trouble is with the things that are not finished. He has no eye for what is still in the making. A half-built house is to him merely a house without a roof, and he is quite ready to prove that no one can live in it.

Now it must be confessed that there is always a gap between what a man says he believes and what he does. There is a gap between the plan of a bridge and the bridge. If we are to call every such gap a fraud, we shall have to put the whole race of builders in the dock, and the architects first of all. The critic who is so shocked to find that the ideal is not yet the fact shows, not his shrewdness, but a curious innocence. He has never tried to build anything. He does not know that every enterprise begins with a declaration which the performance lags far behind.

What is the use, then, of declaring an ideal before we are ready to live up to it? The use is that it will not let us alone. Once a principle has been publicly announced, it takes up its lodging in the conscience and begins to make itself disagreeable. It asks awkward questions. It compares. It remembers what we said last year. Little by little it works upon us, till we are ashamed to fall so far short, and then, being ashamed, we do a little better.

I think of those colleges that were founded on the frontier when the frontier was very new. There was a charter and a board of trustees and a president, and a name that promised learning to a whole commonwealth. There was also, in the first term, a single pupil. A scoffer might have made merry with it. A university with one student! Yet the founders were not deceiving anybody. They were stating an intention and giving it a place to grow. The pupil came first; the others came afterward, because somebody had been bold enough to open the door before the crowd arrived.

The believers in peace are in much the same case. Their college has few students as yet. But the charter is written, and the door is open.
