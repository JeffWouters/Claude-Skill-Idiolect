# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-02

## The passage (by the author)

There was also last summer an apartment to let to a "respectable man" or, the announcement said, it "might do for friends." One of the reasons why many people are bored by hunting lodgings is that they are not humble in spirit. They seek proud lodgings.

As to apartment houses, which are a very different matter: the newspapers publish at various seasons of the year copious Apartment-House Directories, with innumerable half-tone illustrations of these more or less sumptious places. And these directories are competent commentaries on their subject. George Moore remarked, "With business I have nothing to do--my concern is with art." Except that I live in one, with apartment houses I have nothing to do--my concern is with lodgings.

There is only one philosophical observation to be made upon apartment houses. And that is this: How can all these people afford to live in them? When you go to look at apartments you are shown a place that you don't like particularly. You don't think, Oh, how I'd just love to live here if I could only afford it! But you ask the rental as a matter of form. And you learn that this apartment rents for a sum greater (in all likelihood) than your entire salary. And yet, there are miles and miles of apartment houses even better than that. And goodness knows how many thousand people live in them! People whose names you never see in the newspapers as ones important in business, in society, art, literature, or anything else. Obscure people! Very ordinary people! Now where do they get all that money? But about lodgings:

I one time went to look at lodgings in Patchin Place. I had heard that Patchin Place was America's Latin Quarter. I thought it would be well to examine it. Patchin Place is a cul-de-sac behind Jefferson Market. A bizarre female person admitted me to the house there. It was not unreasonable to suppose that she had a certain failing. She slip-slod before me along a remarkably dark, rough-floored and dusty hall, and up a rickety stair. The lodging which she had to let was interesting but not attractive. The tenant, it seemed, who had just moved away had many faults trying to his landlady. He was very delinquent, for one thing, in the payment of his rent. And he was somewhat addicted to drink. This unfortunate propensity led him to keep very late hours, and caused him habitually to fall upstairs.

## Draft A

There are two ways of looking for a place to live in New York. One is to look at apartments; the other is to look at rooms. The apartment is the more imposing, and I began, naturally, with that.

The great apartment blocks stand along the avenues like so many hotels that have decided to stay. They have names. They have awnings that go out to the curb, and a person in uniform under the awning, and another person in uniform inside, behind a desk, and a third person in uniform in the elevator. The rent of a flat in one of these establishments, I found, is considerably more than a man with an ordinary salary earns. I do not say this bitterly; I merely set down the fact. And yet these buildings are full. They are, I was assured, always full. Crowds of people live in them, stacked one upon another from the street to the roof. Who these people are, and what they do, and where it all comes from, I have not been able to learn. I have looked at them going in and coming out. They look much like anybody else.

So I turned to rooms. Rooms are another world, and a far more interesting one. In the course of this turning I found myself one afternoon in an alley in Greenwich Village, a little crooked place that seemed to have wandered in off the street and lost its way. There was a door, and a bell that did not ring, and presently the landlady.

She showed me the rooms. They were rooms. But it was not the rooms she wished to talk about; it was the gentleman who had had them before. He had been, it appeared, a "nice enough" gentleman in his way. But he drank. She did not say that he drank a little, or that he drank now and then. She said that he drank. And she said it several times, going from the window to the mantel and back to the window, as though the rooms themselves still smelt of it. Also, he had not paid. "Not a cent," she said, "for [period needed]." Then he had gone, one night, and taken his trunk with him, which she seemed to regard as the most unkind thing of all.

I said that I did not drink, much. She looked at me for a long moment, doubtfully, as a woman who has heard that before.

## Draft B

There are, broadly speaking, two ways of finding a place to live in New York. One is to take an apartment in one of the enormous new buildings which rise, floor upon floor and window upon window, along the avenues and the cross streets of the upper part of the town. The other is to hunt for rooms. I have considered the first and practised the second, and I am not certain that I understand either.

The apartment buildings are a mystery to me. The rent of a moderately handsome flat in one of these establishments is, so far as I can make out, more than an ordinary man earns in wages; and yet the buildings are full, crowded from cellar to roof, with curtains at every window, a name at every bell and a load of tenants in every elevator, going up and coming down at all hours of the day and night. Who are all these people? Where does the money come from? I do not know. I have asked, and nobody seems to know either. Perhaps they live, as it were, upon one another's rent.

So I went to look at rooms. The search led me to Greenwich Village, and down an alley there which I had somehow never noticed, though I must have passed its entrance repeatedly in the course of my ordinary errands. It was a narrow, crooked, agreeable alley, with a row of little old houses leaning together as if for company. The landlady met me at the door. She had the air of a woman who has been interrupted in something important, and who fully expects, in the nature of things, to be interrupted again before the afternoon is out.

She showed me the rooms without much enthusiasm, and while I looked about she talked, not of the rooms, but of the previous tenant. He drank. He had drunk, it appeared, steadily and without interruption for the whole of his residence; she had found the evidence, she said, everywhere, in places where it would never have occurred to a respectable person to look. He had also not paid. "Not one cent," she said, "for [number needed: how many] weeks." She told me this twice, and then a third time, with additions, and I gathered from the telling that she did not intend to be deceived again, and that she was studying me, meanwhile, for the symptoms.

I said I would think it over. I am thinking it over still.

## Draft C

Anyone who goes looking for a place to live in New York soon learns that the city offers two very different answers to the question, and that neither of them is quite what he had in mind.

The first answer is the big apartment block. These rise along the avenues, floor upon floor of them, with uniformed men at the doors and lifts that hum you up to corridors carpeted from end to end. The rents are astonishing. A flat in one of the better buildings costs more in a year than an ordinary working man earns in the same time, and sometimes a good deal more. And yet the buildings are full. Somebody is paying, and paying in crowds. I never solved the puzzle of who all these people were or how they managed it, and after a few afternoons of being shown bright, identical rooms by agents who seemed to measure my coat before they measured my interest, I stopped trying.

The second answer is the rented room, and it is here that the search becomes interesting. I remember in particular a visit to some rooms in an alley in Greenwich Village. The alley was narrow and a little crooked, the sort of place you would walk past a dozen times without noticing, and the house at the end of it had a door that stuck and a hallway that smelled of [detail needed]. The rooms themselves were not bad. They were low and oddly shaped, with windows that looked out on other people's walls, but they had a certain character that the great blocks could never buy.

The landlady showed me round. She was not interested in selling the rooms so much as in unburdening herself about the man who had last lived in them. He had drunk, she said. Not now and then, but steadily, and with a kind of dedication she seemed almost to respect. She pointed out marks on the floor and the table as evidence. Worse than the drinking, from her point of view, was that he had not paid. Week after week the rent had been promised and week after week it had failed to appear, until at last he had gone, leaving behind a debt and, she implied, a reputation for the house that it did not deserve.

I listened, and I looked at the marks, and I understood that I was being examined as a possible successor. Whatever else I might be, I was expected to be sober and to pay on time. It seemed a modest enough standard, and after the avenues, a refreshingly human one.
