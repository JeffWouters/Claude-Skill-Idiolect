# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-01

## The passage (by the author)

Succeeding visits caused us to suspect that Mr. James's ideas of the machinery of business are somewhat naive. He seemed to regard us as, so to say, the whole works. It entered our head that maybe Mr. James thought we received and answered all manner of correspondence, editorial as well as that connected with the retail business, opened up in the morning, read, accepted, and rejected manuscript, nailed up boxes for shipment, swept out the shop, and were acquainted perfectly with all confidential matters of the House. "I wrote you" (us), "you know," he said. And he referred by the way, apparently upon the assumption that the matter had been laid before us, to business of which we could not possibly have cognizance. And then he desired to send some books. Fumbling in his breast pocket, he produced a letter, from which he read aloud a list of his own works apparently requested of him. Carefully replacing his letter, he said: "I should like to send these books to my sister-in-law." With that he started out.

Now, it was not a difficult problem to assume that this could be no other than Mrs. William James, still, it is customary for purchasers to state the name of the person to whom goods are to go, and many people are sceptical that the salesman has it down right even then. "Your sister-in-law, Mr. James, is------?" we suggested. "Oh, yes, of course--of course; Mrs. William James; of course--of course," Mr. James said. Now, certainly, he supposed (it was evident) he had got finally settled a difficult and complicated piece of business. Mrs. William James's regular address we might reasonably infer. Still it might be that she was at the moment somewhere else, on a visit. It were better to have Mr. James give his order in the regular way. "And the address?" we mentioned. "Oh, yes--oh, yes; of course--of course," Mr. James said apologetically. Then, pausing a moment to see if there was anything more in this bewildering labyrinth of details to such a complex transaction, he departed, taking, as he drew away, his hat, as Mrs. Nickleby says, "completely off."

## Draft A

He came in out of the street rather quickly, as a man does who has determined to get a disagreeable business over with, and he came straight to my counter. He wished, he said, to order a book. He named it. I wrote it down. So far the transaction was of the ordinary character that repeats itself a great many times a day in any bookstore, and I was not, I confess, prepared for anything remarkable.

But it presently appeared that in his view I was the entire establishment. I was the stock room and the shipping department; I was the bookkeeper, the cashier and the person who ties the parcels; I was, as it were, the firm. Would I see that the book went off immediately? Would I attend to the wrapping personally? Would I make certain that it was charged correctly? I said that I would. It seemed simpler. There is no profit in explaining the division of labour in a large commercial house to a distinguished man of letters who has come in to purchase one book.

Then we arrived at the recipient. "Now, the name," I said, my pencil waiting.

He gave a name. Then he withdrew it. There was, it seemed, a question of initials. He looked at the ceiling (a thing customers frequently do in a bookstore, as though the ceiling were a directory) and he looked at the floor. He drummed a little with his fingers upon the counter. The address came harder still. He had it in his head, he explained, in a general way; it was the street he was uncertain of, and the number, and to some extent the town. We laboured at it together, slowly, patiently, earnestly, as two gentlemen might labour over a puzzle in the Sunday newspaper. In the end there was something written on my slip that I believed the post office would understand.

He thanked me. He thanked me very courteously indeed, and he evidently meant it. He drew on his gloves with the air of a man whose afternoon has been relieved of a considerable weight, so to speak, and he went out into the street again. I have observed people leaving a dentist's chair looking much the same.

The book, I believe, arrived.

## Draft B

He came in one afternoon, and I knew him at once from the portraits, though he seemed smaller and more hesitant than any photograph had led me to expect. He wanted to send a book to someone, he said, and he wanted it done properly.

What struck me first was that he took me for the whole firm. I was a young man behind a counter, responsible for finding volumes on shelves and writing out slips, but he spoke to me as though I kept the ledgers, packed the parcels, paid the postage and could vouch personally for the reliability of the carriers. Would I see that it was wrapped carefully? Would I make sure it went by the right post? Would I be sure the bill came to him and not to the recipient? I said yes to everything. It seemed unkind to explain the division of labour to a man who had so plainly never had to think about it.

Then came the matter of the address. I took up my pencil and asked for the name of the person the book was for. He began it confidently and stopped halfway. He tried again, with a different spelling, and then apologised and said that it was a lady he knew quite well, really, but that the name was one of those that never settled in the mind. The address was worse. He knew the street, or thought he did. He knew the house was near [landmark needed]. He was fairly sure of the town. He patted his pockets for a letter that might have it, found several letters that did not, and at last dictated something that he admitted was probably close enough for the post office to finish the job.

I wrote it all down as neatly as I could and read it back to him. He listened with great seriousness, nodded, and said that it sounded right, or at any rate that it sounded like her.

When it was settled he thanked me with real courtesy, more than the errand deserved, and shook my hand. He put on his hat at the door and went out into the street with the look of a man who has just got through something difficult and would not have to do it again for a while.

## Draft C

He wished, it appeared, to send a book. That was all. But it is one thing to wish to send a book and quite another thing to send it, as anybody who has stood behind a counter can tell you. The great man approached us with the air of one who has come to the right person at last. In his view, we gathered, we were the firm. We took the order, we wrapped the parcel, we kept the books, we carried the thing through the streets, and very likely we had written it. He addressed us upon each of these departments in turn, earnestly, patiently, a little anxiously, as a man speaks to a general manager. We did not undeceive him. One does not correct [novelist] upon the organisation of a retail business.

The book was chosen easily enough. It was when we came to the name of the person to whom it was to go that the difficulty began. "It is for--" he said, and stopped. He looked at the ceiling. He looked at us, as though we might know. He produced a name, withdrew it, and produced another, very like the first but spelled, he thought, rather differently. We wrote it down, both ways. Then there was the address. He was sure of the town. He was fairly sure of the street. The number of the house had gone wherever such numbers go. "You will find it," he said, with great confidence, "I am quite certain you will find it." We said we were quite certain, too. It is a remarkable thing, how much of the great world rests upon the belief that somebody in a shop will find it.

Here, now, was a man who could carry in his head the whole of a family through three generations, their motives, their silences, the exact shade of their hesitations over a teacup; and he could not carry the number of a house. Just so. Perhaps there is only so much room.

When at last it was all written down, more or less, he drew himself up, and a look of deep relief came over his face, the look of a man who has been to the dentist and found that it was only a cleaning. He thanked us, very courteously, at some length. He bowed. He went out into the street lightly, an errand done, a burden off his mind; and on to ours.
