# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-03

## The passage (by the author)

Most lies of the decenter sort are social. “The admixture of a lie doth ever give pleasure,” said the moralist Bacon. There is certainly very little defence for the lie that does not give pleasure. It is to save other people’s feelings, not our own, that we tell lies. Let me put a case quite bluntly. How, without lying, is a man to thank his small niece properly for the necktie which she has selected for his Christmas present? No one wants merely to be thanked for one’s trouble; every one wants to be told that his taste has been perfect. Now that the late Phillips Brooks’s handsome evasion of fact has become historic, who ever dares not to praise a baby explicitly? I confess that it goes against the grain with me to say that I have enjoyed something which I have detested; and I have frequently accepted invitations (especially over the telephone) because my tongue would not twist itself round the phrase “another engagement” when the other engagement was non-existent. But I have never had the slightest compunction about saying that I was sorry I had another engagement, when I did have another engagement and was not sorry.

I know only one person whom I could count on not to indulge herself in these conventional falsehoods, and she has never been able, so far as I know, to keep a friend. The habit of literal truth-telling, frankly, is self-indulgence of the worst. Nothing could be more delightful, in an evil sense, than telling certain people that their Christmas presents, their babies, and their hospitalities are all horrors which defy description; especially if one could count it a virtue to one’s self to say those things starkly. But one cannot keep that weapon only for one’s foes: the only excuse for saying inexcusable things is that one always says them. Roughly speaking, one’s friends are the people of whom one thinks, habitually, pleasant things. But even friends can be annoying, or unbeautiful, or dull. And it is of the essence of those manners which are morals not to tell them so if one can help it. “Faithful are the wounds of a friend”--and must sometimes be dealt. But no stabbing over non-essentials! And above all, no stabbing when it is a pleasure to stab. Sometimes these truth-tellers congratulate themselves that their praise is immensely enhanced by its rarity. There, I fancy, they are mistaken: for in the first place, praise that is too long on the way loses its savor; and in the second, they acquire, I have noticed, a censorious habit of mind that prevents them from praising at all.

## Draft A

There is a kind of lie which nobody, so far as I know, has ever been hanged for, and which a great many excellent people tell every day of their lives. I mean the small, polite untruth that is told to spare somebody's feelings. Let us not pretend that we are above it. Who among us has not written to an aunt to thank her for a gift that went, the same afternoon, to the back of the highest shelf? Who has not bent over a perambulator and declared that the infant within was the loveliest thing he had ever seen--when it was, frankly, rather like every other infant, and not a very handsome one at that? I, for one, cannot manage them without fibbing; and I have long since ceased to be ashamed of it.

There are, of course, people who will not fib. They make a point of it. They tell you that your new hat is unbecoming, that your aunt's gift was hideous, and that the baby looks like a boiled lobster; and they expect you to admire them for their candour. I am sorry, but I do not admire them. Their bluntness seems to me the most selfish of indulgences. It costs them nothing, and it costs other people a good deal; for the pleasure of feeling honest, they are quite willing to hurt, and they are quite willing, too, to lose their friends. They do lose them, in the end. One cannot go on forever being told the truth about one's hats.

But there is a subtler danger in the habit, and it is this. The person who will not praise unless praise is absolutely and literally deserved soon finds that he does not praise at all. He has grown so watchful against flattery that he has forgotten how to be generous. He notices, instead, what is wrong; and noticing what is wrong becomes, with him, a second nature. He is not a liar, certainly; but he has become something almost as tiresome--a habitual critic, who cannot look at a dinner or a drawing-room or a friend without mentally marking it down.

And even when he does bring himself to a compliment, it is usually too late. He waits until he is quite sure; and by that time the moment has passed. A kind word, like a cup of tea, is best when it is hot. Offered a week afterward, with all the proper reservations, it is worth very little to anybody. I would rather have a warm fib at the right moment than a chilly truth at the wrong one; and I suspect that most of us, if we are honest, would say the same.

## Draft B

There is a kind of lie that nobody, I fancy, has ever been punished for telling, and that most of us tell, week in and week out, without the smallest qualm. It is the small polite untruth that spares another person's feelings. You thank your aunt for the ornament she has sent you, which you did not want and would never have bought, and you say it is exactly what you wanted; you do not say that it will go straight into a drawer and stay there. You are shown a new infant, red and wrinkled and entirely without features, and you declare that it is beautiful. Every one does it. The aunt is pleased, the mother is pleased, and nobody is a penny the worse--except, perhaps, the conscience of the very scrupulous, who ought to know better than to be troubled.

For there are people who insist upon blunt honesty in these matters, and who are rather proud of it. They will tell you that they "cannot pretend," that they "must say what they think." Granted; but why must they? Nobody has asked them for a verdict on the ornament. The truth, in such a case, serves no purpose whatever except to gratify the person who tells it. It is a selfish indulgence, and like most selfish indulgences it comes expensive in the end. The blunt people lose their friends, one by one, and they generally lose them without in the least understanding why. They have been honest--and honesty, they suppose, is its own excuse, and needs no apology to anybody. It is not.

There is another thing to be said. People who seldom praise anything are apt to become habitually critical; the faculty of admiration, like any other faculty, withers when it is not used. They begin by withholding a compliment out of scruple, and they end by being unable to see what is good in anything at all. And a compliment that is too long delayed loses most of its value. The friend who waits until she is perfectly sure, and who then weighs every word before she speaks, may be scrupulously accurate; but by the time her praise arrives, nobody wants it. My point is only this: a little generous exaggeration, offered promptly and without calculation, is worth more than a great deal of careful truth offered too late. We are not on oath at the dinner table. Heaven forbid that we should be!

## Draft C

Most of us tell small untruths every week of our lives, and most of us would be offended to be called liars. The untruths I mean are the polite ones: the words we say not to gain anything for ourselves but to spare someone else a moment of hurt. I want to say a word in their defence.

Think of the gift that arrives from an aunt at the holidays. It is the wrong colour, the wrong size, and entirely the wrong idea, and she chose it with care and is waiting to hear how you like it. Or think of the new infant held up for your admiration, red and creased and indistinguishable from every other infant, while its parents watch your face. In both cases the honest answer is available, and in both cases almost nobody gives it. We thank the aunt warmly. We say the baby is beautiful. We fib, and we are right to.

There are people who will not. They pride themselves on plain speaking, on never saying what they do not mean, and they describe this as a kind of integrity. I think it is closer to a selfish indulgence. The blunt truth-teller keeps his own conscience spotless at the cost of other people's feelings, and he pays for it in friendships. People do not return, for long, to a house where every gift is judged and every child appraised. The satisfaction of being candid is his; the bruise belongs to someone else. [example needed]

There is a second cost, less obvious. Those who rarely praise, who hold back a kind word until they are sure it is fully deserved, tend to grow habitually critical. The muscle that notices what is good weakens from disuse, and the one that notices faults grows strong. After a while such a person no longer sees much to admire, not because the world has changed but because he has trained himself out of looking.

And praise, like most good things, does not keep. A compliment given at the moment it is felt is worth a great deal: it lands when the effort is still fresh and the doubt still near. The same compliment offered a month later, after careful consideration, is a small thing, faintly dutiful, and often faintly suspicious. If we mean to be generous at all, we had better be generous promptly, and accept that promptness will sometimes run a little ahead of strict accuracy.

None of this is an argument for lying in matters of weight. It is an argument for proportion: for recognising that a small kindness is sometimes worth more than a small fact, and that the people who refuse ever to make that trade are not as virtuous as they think.
