## The passage (by the author)

Why is it that we continue to ask for estimates when so few of us believe in them? The question is not entirely rhetorical, since the practice of estimation survives in almost every software organisation despite a long and well-documented history of disappointment, and the persistence of a practice that consistently fails usually suggests that it is doing something other than what it claims to do. Arguably the estimate is not a prediction at all; it is a negotiation conducted in the vocabulary of prediction, a way of reconciling what the business wants with what the engineers fear, in which both parties agree to pretend that the resulting figure describes the future. I suspect most experienced engineers understand this intuitively, even if they would not articulate it in quite those terms. The difficulty arises when the pretence is forgotten — when the negotiated figure hardens into a commitment, and the commitment becomes a measure by which people are judged.

It may be helpful to consider what we are actually doing when we produce an estimate for a piece of software work. We are imagining a sequence of activities that has not yet happened, in a system we only partially understand, subject to interruptions we cannot foresee, and we are compressing all of that uncertainty into a single figure that must fit neatly into a planning document. The act is, in some sense, heroic; it asks the imagination to perform a task for which it is poorly equipped, and it rewards confidence over accuracy. Is it any wonder that the results are unreliable? What is more surprising, perhaps, is how rarely the unreliability is acknowledged in the planning process itself, as though each new estimate were an entirely fresh attempt rather than the latest entry in a long series of similar misjudgements.

## Draft A

Why do software teams keep producing estimates that so few people, including the teams themselves, actually trust? It may be that the estimate was never meant to be a prediction in the first place. In practice it works more like a negotiation, a meeting point between what the business hopes can be delivered and what the engineers fear might go wrong, and the number that emerges records the balance of that negotiation rather than any careful measurement of the work.

There is nothing especially wrong with this, as long as everyone understands what the number is. The trouble begins at the moment the figure stops being a starting point for discussion and turns into a binding promise, repeated in plans and presentations until it acquires an authority it was never given. Those who offered it with reservations find that the reservations have been forgotten; those who received it as a commitment find, in due course, that it was not one; both sides are left with the sense that the other has behaved badly. I suspect this sequence is familiar to anyone who has spent time on either side of it.

It seems to me that the more serious failure lies in what happens afterwards. Planning seldom learns from its own history, and the same kinds of estimate miss by the same kinds of margin, project after project, without anyone asking why the pattern repeats. Arguably the misses themselves contain the most useful information an organisation could have about its own work, if it were willing to look at them rather than explain them away. Perhaps the problem is not that our estimates are wrong, but that we keep forgetting how they were made.

## Draft B

Why do software teams continue to produce estimates that so few people believe? The question is worth asking seriously, because the practice persists with remarkable stubbornness despite a long history of disappointment on all sides. One plausible answer is that estimates are not really forecasts at all, or not only forecasts. They function as a kind of negotiation — a meeting point between what the business would like to happen and what the engineers fear might happen instead. The business brings its wishes, the engineers bring their worries, and the figure that emerges is less a prediction than a settlement, a number that both sides can tolerate for the time being.

Understood in this way, the estimate is not in itself a problem. Negotiation is a normal and often healthy part of working together, and a settled figure can give everyone something to plan around. The difficulty begins when the settlement is mistaken for a commitment — when a number offered tentatively, under conditions of considerable uncertainty, hardens into a binding promise against which the team will later be judged. At that point the engineers learn to pad their figures defensively, the business learns to discount them accordingly, and the estimate loses whatever informational value it once had.

What seems most striking is how little the planning process tends to learn from its own history. The same kinds of work are underestimated in the same ways, cycle after cycle, and the misses are treated each time as individual accidents rather than as evidence of a pattern. A team that looked honestly at its past estimates might discover a good deal about where its optimism is misplaced; an organisation that did the same might discover how its own expectations shape the numbers it receives. That such reflection is rare suggests, perhaps, that the estimates are serving some purpose other than accuracy.

## Draft C

Software teams spend a surprising amount of time producing estimates that almost nobody fully trusts. The people who give them hedge in private, the people who receive them add their own mental buffer, and everyone expects the real answer to be different. Yet the ritual continues, sprint after sprint and project after project.

One reason is that estimates are doing a different job from the one they claim. On paper, they are predictions. In practice, they are often a form of bargaining. The business side arrives with what it wants and when it would like it. Engineering arrives with its worries about complexity, risk and everything that could go wrong. The estimate is where those two positions meet, and the number that comes out reflects the negotiation as much as the work.

That on its own is not a disaster. A negotiated figure can still be a useful starting point for a conversation about scope and priorities. The trouble starts when the figure turns into a binding promise. Once an estimate is written into a plan, shared upwards and treated as a commitment, everyone involved changes how they behave. Engineers pad future estimates to protect themselves. Managers push back on those padded numbers because they expect padding. The bargaining gets harder and the numbers mean less.

Meanwhile, the planning process rarely learns from its own history. Teams miss estimates in similar ways again and again, yet each new round of planning starts as if those misses never happened. The same optimism about integration work, the same underestimate of testing, the same surprise at how long reviews take. [example needed] The data needed to improve is often sitting right there in past projects; it is just not being used.

Estimates would earn more trust if they were treated honestly as what they are: a best current guess, agreed through negotiation, that should be checked against how similar guesses turned out before.
