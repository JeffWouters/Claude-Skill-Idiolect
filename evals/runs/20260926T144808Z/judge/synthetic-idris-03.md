## The passage (by the author)

There is a curious psychology to all this. A deadline that is widely understood to be arbitrary can nevertheless exert enormous force, because once it has been announced it acquires a reputational dimension; missing it would mean admitting that the original plan was wrong, and the people who made the plan may be more invested in its correctness than in its outcome. You have perhaps seen this dynamic play out, in which a date that everyone privately believes is unrealistic is publicly defended until the last possible moment, at which point it is quietly moved and the whole exercise begins again. The consequence is a kind of institutional theatre, in which the deadline functions less as a planning tool than as a performance of confidence. I suspect that most participants in this theatre understand its nature perfectly well; they simply feel unable to be the one who stops the performance.

Is there a better way to relate to deadlines? It seems to me that the most important step is simply to be honest about their origins — to ask, whenever a date is proposed, what would actually happen if it were missed, and to allow the answer to shape how seriously the date is treated. A deadline tied to an unmovable external event deserves one kind of response; a deadline chosen because it seemed like a reasonable target deserves another, and conflating the two does a disservice to everyone involved. Perhaps we might also learn to renegotiate dates earlier and more openly, treating a revised deadline not as a failure of planning but as the natural result of learning more about the work. None of this is technically difficult, and none of it requires new tools or elaborate methods. What makes it difficult is the reluctance to disappoint, which is, in some sense, a reluctance to be honest.

## Draft A

How does a date that almost nobody believes in manage to survive for so long? Most people who have worked on a large project will recognise the pattern, in which a target is announced, doubts about it begin to circulate quietly among the people doing the work, and yet the date itself remains untouched on every plan and in every update. It may be that the date has stopped being a forecast at all. Once it has been announced, it becomes a matter of reputation, and those who announced it defend it long after the reasons for doing so have gone.

There is also a question that is rarely asked in these situations, which is what missing the date would actually cost. Sometimes the answer is considerable, because something outside the organisation depends on it; more often, I suspect, the cost is smaller than the anxiety surrounding it, and consists mostly of an awkward conversation with the people who were promised it. Is it any wonder that teams keep working towards a date they privately doubt, when the alternative is to be the one who says so first?

It seems to me that the most useful thing an organisation can do is to renegotiate early, while there is still room to change scope or sequence, rather than late, when the only options left are overwork or a public retreat. This is not primarily a problem of planning technique. The real barrier is the fear of letting people down, which makes each postponement of the conversation feel like a small kindness when it is, arguably, the opposite. Perhaps the date we should worry about is not the one on the plan, but the date on which someone first knew it could not be met.

## Draft B

There is a curious transformation that a target date undergoes once it has been announced. Before it is spoken aloud, it is a guess, and everyone involved understands it as such; afterwards, it becomes something closer to a promise, and before long it has become a matter of reputation. The people who named it feel their credibility bound up with it, the people who heard it begin to plan around it, and the date acquires a weight out of all proportion to the reasoning that produced it. What follows is often a kind of quiet defence, in which evidence that the date cannot be met is explained away, deferred, or simply not raised, long past the point at which anyone privately believes in it.

It may help to ask a question that is rarely put directly: what would it actually cost to miss this date? Sometimes the answer is considerable — a contractual obligation, a dependent team, a commitment made to others outside the organisation. Often, though, the honest answer is much smaller than the anxiety surrounding it would suggest, and the principal cost turns out to be embarrassment rather than any tangible harm. Knowing which kind of date one is dealing with changes the conversation entirely, since it separates the dates that genuinely must hold from those that are held largely out of pride.

Where a date is in doubt, the most useful thing a team can do is to renegotiate it early, while there is still room to adjust scope or expectations. This sounds straightforward, and in procedural terms it is. The real obstacle, I think, lies elsewhere: in the reluctance to disappoint people who were counting on us, and in the feeling that raising the matter amounts to admitting that we were wrong. That reluctance is entirely human. Yet the disappointment is rarely avoided by postponing it; it is merely concentrated into a later and more painful moment, when fewer options remain.

## Draft C

Most people who have worked on a large project have watched a target date that nobody quite believes in any more being defended as if it were still realistic. The plan says one thing, the work says another, and yet the date stays on the slide, meeting after meeting.

Part of the reason is that an announced date stops being a planning figure and becomes a matter of reputation. Once a manager has told their leadership, or a team has told its customers, the date carries the name of whoever said it. Moving it feels like admitting a mistake in public. So it gets defended long past the point where the evidence supports it, and the defence itself consumes effort that could have gone into the work.

A useful way to break that spell is to ask a plain question: what would missing this date actually cost? Sometimes the answer is serious, because something outside the team depends on it and cannot move. Often, though, the honest answer is that a few people would be disappointed and a plan would need to be redrawn. [example needed] Putting the real cost next to the growing cost of pretending tends to change the conversation.

The earlier that conversation happens, the better. A date renegotiated while there is still room to adjust scope, staffing or sequence is a normal piece of planning. A date abandoned in the final week is a failure that everyone saw coming. The information that the date was at risk usually existed long before; what was missing was the willingness to say it.

That willingness is the real barrier, and it is rarely about process. It is fear of letting people down: the manager who promised, the stakeholders who were promised to, the team who worked hard towards it. Organisations that want honest dates have to make it safe to say early that a date is in doubt, and to treat that as good news about visibility rather than bad news about the people.
