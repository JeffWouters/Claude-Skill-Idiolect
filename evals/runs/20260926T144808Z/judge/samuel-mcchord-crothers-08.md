# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-08

## The passage (by the author)

Lowell describes the rough humor of the frontier, with the free and easy manners which characterize

this brown-fisted rough, this shirt-sleeved Cid, This back-woods Charlemagne of empires new, Whose blundering heel instinctively finds out The goutier foot of speechless dignities, Who, meeting Cæsar’s self, would slap his back, Call him ‘Old Horse’ and challenge to a drink.

He had in mind the backwoodsmen whom sturdy apostles like Peter Cartwright labored with to such good purpose. But the Honest Miner, though a pioneer, is not a backwoodsman. His humor is of a different quality. He would not think of slapping Cæsar on the back and calling him “Old Horse.” It would seem more amusing to him to address some one who might properly be called “Old Horse” with titles of honor.

“Truthful James” delights in euphemism. He does not object to calling a spade a spade, but he refuses to do so in such a way as to give offense.

Which it is not my style To produce needless pain By statements that rile Or that go ’gin the grain.

He is no “brown-fisted rough” who delights in swagger. There is roughness enough all about him, and it pleases him to cultivate the amenities. His gentlemanliness is often carried to excess.

The most characteristic humor of the Honest Miner consists, not in grotesque exaggeration, but in delicate understatement. What can be more considerate than the notice posted by the side of an open shaft: “Gentlemen will please not fall down this shaft, for there are men at work below.”

A Nevada minister once described to me the action of a brother minister in the early days. The minister went to a certain town where he offended the lawless element, and was threatened with physical violence if he persisted in his intention of preaching. My friend described the method by which the liberty of prophesying was asserted. “He went into the pulpit, laid his revolver on the Bible--and then he preached extempore.”

## Draft A

The humour of the prospector is often confused with that of the backwoodsman, but the two are of quite different breeds. Lowell has given us the frontier figure in his most characteristic form. He is brash and boastful. He likes to hear himself talk and he likes to have you know that he can whip any man in the county. His fun is loud, and it is at somebody's expense.

The miner was not of this temper. He had a fondness for courtesy which would have surprised those who knew him only by report. He liked to address his neighbour by a title, and the grander the better. He would no more have called a neighbour by his bare surname than he would have gone to a funeral in his shirt sleeves. [example needed] It was not flattery. It was simply that in a rough place he preferred smooth manners, as a man in a cold country prefers a warm coat.

His wit was of the same gentle kind. It lay chiefly in understatement. Where the backwoodsman would have shouted, the miner murmured. I remember a sign that stood beside a deep open pit on the edge of a camp. It did not say Danger in large letters. It suggested, in the politest terms, that the passer-by might find it advisable to keep to the path. [example needed] The mildness of the warning was the whole joke, and the pit made the point better than any capital letters could have done.

There is a story of a preacher who came into one of these camps and was told that his sermon had better be short, or he might not live to finish it. He mounted the platform, took out his revolver, and laid it carefully upon the open Bible. Then he began to speak, without notes. I do not know what text he chose, but I am told that he held the attention of his hearers to the end. The miners appreciated the gesture. It was courteous, it was understated, and it made everything perfectly clear.

## Draft B

The humour of the prospector is not the humour of the backwoodsman, though the two are often confused by those who have met neither. Lowell's frontier figure is a brash fellow who boasts and swaggers and expects you to be amused by the sheer size of his claims. His wit is the wit of exaggeration, and it is always louder than the occasion requires.

The miner has another manner altogether. He is, in his own fashion, a courteous man, and he has a great fondness for grand titles; every acquaintance is promoted to some high rank, and the humblest shanty on the hillside is given a name that would not disgrace a palace. There is nothing brash about this. It is the instinct of a man who has very little else to give and therefore gives honour freely.

His wit, accordingly, lies in understatement. Where the backwoodsman would shout, the miner murmurs, and the murmur is the more effective of the two. I have in mind the kind of sign he would set up beside an open pit, which did not warn the traveller in capital letters of the danger of death but observed very politely that the ground was uneven at this point and that gentlemen would do well to take care. [example needed: the wording of such a sign] The traveller who reads it smiles. The traveller who ignores it does not smile for long.

The same mildness appears in the story of a preacher who had been threatened by some of the rougher members of his congregation. He came into the pulpit and took out his gun and laid it deliberately upon the open Bible. Then, leaving the text of the sermon to take care of itself, he preached without any manuscript at all. It was, perhaps, an unusually attentive audience.

## Draft C

The humour of the prospector is often lumped together with the humour of the backwoodsman, as though every joke told west of the settled country came from the same source. It does not. The two men are funny in quite different ways.

Lowell's frontier figure, the backwoodsman as he appears in Lowell's work, is brash. He boasts, he exaggerates, he shoulders his way into a conversation and fills it. His jokes are loud and his manners are rough, and much of the comedy comes from how little he cares who hears him.

The miner is another sort altogether. He prefers courtesy. He addresses rough men by grand titles and treats the most ordinary exchange with a formality that seems borrowed from a drawing room. [example of such a title needed] Where the backwoodsman shouts, the miner bows.

His wit follows from that. It lies in mild understatement, in saying far less than the situation calls for and saying it politely. The classic instance is the warning sign beside an open pit. Another man would write DANGER in large letters. The miner puts up a courteous little notice, something closer to a gentle suggestion than a warning, as if the chief risk of falling in were a small social embarrassment. [wording of the sign needed] The joke is in the gap between the size of the hole and the mildness of the note.

The same spirit shows in a story about a preacher who came to a camp and was threatened. [details of the threat needed] He did not back down, and he did not bluster. He took out his gun and set it down on top of the scripture in front of him. Then, with the book held shut under the weapon, he spoke to the congregation without notes, unscripted, saying whatever he had to say. Nobody needed the gesture explained. It was polite, it was quiet, and it made its point completely.

That is the miner's humour at its best. It never raises its voice. It lets the facts, and a little courtesy, do the work.
