## The passage (by the author)

It would be easy to conclude from this that recurring meetings should simply be abolished, and there are teams that have tried precisely that experiment with a certain evangelical enthusiasm. I am not convinced that the conclusion follows. What the meeting provides, even in its degraded form, is a guaranteed occasion for contact — a moment when you can raise something half-formed without having to justify interrupting anyone, when the quieter members of a team can be seen and heard, when small frictions surface before they harden into grievances. Perhaps the ritual persists precisely because it performs this function, however inefficiently, and because nobody has designed a better substitute. In some sense the meeting is less a tool for making decisions than a piece of social infrastructure; its value is not visible in any particular session but in the accumulated familiarity it produces over months. Remove it without replacing that function, and we may discover that the team has become efficient in a way that is also, subtly, lonelier.

What, then, might we do differently? One possibility is to treat each recurring meeting as something that must periodically earn its renewal, rather than as a permanent fixture whose continuation requires no argument. A team might pause every so often and ask, with genuine openness, whether the original purpose still holds, whether a different purpose has replaced it, and whether that new purpose is worth the collective attention it consumes. The conversation is often uncomfortable, since it touches on questions of status and inclusion that are rarely discussed openly; the person who organised the meeting may feel that their judgement is being questioned, and the people who value it most may be the least inclined to say so. It is nevertheless the kind of discomfort that tends to be productive, because it forces the team to articulate what it actually wants from its time together. I suspect that many meetings would survive such scrutiny in a modified form — shorter, less frequent, or reframed around a clearer question — and that a few would be quietly and gratefully retired.

## Draft A

Every so often someone suggests scrapping the standing team meetings altogether. The weekly sync, the Monday check-in, the Thursday review: they fill the calendar, they eat into focus time, and on a bad week it is hard to point to a single decision any of them produced. The case for clearing them out sounds obvious.

I think it is only half right. Judging a standing meeting purely by the decisions it produces misses what many of them are actually for. A regular slot where the same people see each other, hear what the others are working on and notice who seems stretched is a kind of social glue. It keeps contact going between people who would otherwise only meet when something has gone wrong. That contact is hard to measure and easy to underrate, right up until it is gone and a team discovers it has become a set of individuals who share a ticket queue.

That does not mean every standing meeting deserves to live forever. The problem with recurring meetings is that they recur by default. Nobody has to argue for them again once they are on the calendar, so they outlast the reasons they were created. The fix is not to abolish them but to make them earn their place. Every so often, a team should put each standing meeting back on the table and ask plainly: what is this for now, who needs to be here, and would we create it today if it did not exist?

Asked honestly, that question produces three kinds of answer. Some meetings would end, and should, because the problem they were set up to handle is solved or has moved elsewhere. Many more would survive in a different shape: shorter, with fewer people, with a sharper focus, or explicitly relabelled as a chance to stay in touch rather than a decision forum pretending to be one. A few would carry on exactly as they are, and the team would at least know why.

So before scrapping the lot, audit them. Keep the glue, cut the habit, and give every meeting a regular moment where it has to justify itself again. [example needed]

## Draft B

Few things attract such reliable complaint as the standing team meeting, and few are defended so rarely. The case against it is familiar enough: an hour each week, or a quarter of an hour each morning, in which little is decided and much is repeated, and which could, we are told, be replaced by a short written update that nobody would need to attend. There is some truth in this. Yet the complaint rests on a particular assumption about what meetings are for, namely that their purpose is to produce decisions, and that a meeting which decides nothing has therefore failed. I am not convinced the assumption holds. A good part of what a standing meeting provides is not decision at all but contact — the regular occasion on which people who might otherwise work in parallel for weeks are obliged to see one another, to notice who seems stretched or uncertain, and to maintain the loose familiarity on which later cooperation depends. That kind of social glue is difficult to measure, which is perhaps why it is so easily dismissed.

None of this means that every standing meeting deserves to survive. The more modest proposal is that a team should, at regular intervals, ask each of its recurring meetings to justify itself afresh, as though it were being proposed for the first time. What is this meeting actually for? Would we create it today if it did not already exist? Who needs to be in the room, and who attends only out of habit or a vague fear of missing something? Questions of this kind are uncomfortable, because they imply that the arrangement we have been tolerating may not have been worth tolerating, but they are also the only honest way of telling a meeting that serves the team from one that merely occupies it.

My expectation is that most meetings would come through such a review in altered form rather than disappearing altogether: shorter, perhaps, or given a narrower focus, or held less often. A few would end, and their ending would be a relief to everyone involved. What seems worth resisting is the all-or-nothing framing, in which the only choices are to keep every meeting as it is or to abolish the lot. Neither honours what the meetings were meant to do.

## Draft C

What, in the end, is the weekly team meeting actually for? The question is usually asked with some irritation, by people who have just sat through another hour in which nothing was decided, and the answer they expect is that it serves no purpose at all. It may be that this answer comes too quickly, since it assumes that a meeting exists only to produce decisions and then judges it, rather harshly, by that standard alone. I suspect most standing meetings do something else, something harder to name and considerably harder to measure.

There is also the matter of what a regular gathering provides simply by recurring. It gives people who might otherwise work in isolation a predictable occasion to see one another, to notice who seems stretched and to hear about problems before they grow; it gives newer members of the team a place to learn how the group talks; it gives the group itself a rhythm that, in some sense, holds it together. None of this appears in the notes taken afterwards, and that is arguably why it is so easily dismissed whenever someone proposes to clear the calendar. You may have felt the loss of such contact only once the meeting that provided it was gone.

It seems to me that the better response is neither to defend every standing meeting nor to abolish them wholesale, but to ask each one to justify itself again at intervals. A team might pause, from time to time, to consider whether a given meeting still earns its place, what it would lose if the meeting stopped, and whether that loss would be felt by anyone at all. Many meetings would survive this kind of scrutiny in a trimmed or refocused form, shorter perhaps, or with a clearer purpose than before; some would come to a quiet end, and few people would mourn them. The exercise matters less for its verdicts than for the attention it brings to habits we rarely examine. Perhaps the question is not whether we should meet, but whether we still remember why we do.
