# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-02

## The passage (by the author)

He loves to tell of the ups and downs of his own fortune. There is no bitterness in his memory of his failures. They relieve the record from the monotony that belongs to assured success. His successes are not less gratifying because, like all things earthly, they have had a speedy ending. A dozen times he has “struck it rich.” He has thrown away his pick and shovel and gone below to bask in the smiles of fortune. He has indulged in vague dreams of going to Europe, of looking up his family tree, and of cultivating grammar and other fine arts. Fortune continued to smile, but after a while her smile became sardonic, and with a wink she said, “Time’s up!” Then the Honest Miner would take up his pick and shovel and return to his work, neither a sadder nor a wiser man,--in fact, exactly the same kind of man he was before. That Experience is a teacher is a pedantic theory which he rejects with scorn. Experience is not a schoolmaster, Experience is a chum who likes to play practical jokes upon him. Just now he has given him a tumble and got the laugh on him. But just wait awhile! And he chuckles to himself as he thinks how he will outwit Experience.

All the traditions of the mining country confirm him in his point of view: Listen to what Experience says, and then do just the opposite. It is the unexpected that happens. The richest diggings bear the most lugubrious names. The Montanian delights to tell of the riches taken out of Last Chance Gulch. The Arizonian for years boasted of the gayety of Tombstone and the amazing prosperity of the Total Wreck Mine.

Certain physiologists are now telling us that the poetic praise of wine is based upon a mistake. Alcohol, they say, is not a stimulant, but a depressant. It does not stimulate the imagination so much as it depresses the critical faculty so that dullness may easily pass for wit. An idea will occur to a sober man as being rather bright, but before he has time to express it he sees that it is not so. Under the inhibition of good sense he holds his tongue and saves his reputation. But in a convivial company the inhibition is removed. Everybody says whatever is uppermost in his mind. The mice play, not because they are more lively than before, but only because the cat is away.

## Draft A

The prospector had a way of taking his misfortunes which would have puzzled a moralist. The moralist expects a man who has been knocked down to get up a wiser man. He is to reflect upon his error, to trace it to its cause, and to resolve never to repeat it. The prospector did none of these things. When the vein pinched out, or the town he had helped to build was left to the jack rabbits, he did not regard it as a judgment. He regarded it as a joke, and not a joke upon himself so much as a joke which Fortune had played upon everybody, and which it would be churlish not to enjoy. He had been fooled, to be sure; but it had been a good prank, well carried out, and he bore no grudge against the prankster.

That he learned nothing from the experience was, I think, a source of quiet satisfaction to him. Learning from experience is a dreary business. It narrows a man's hopes to the size of his past. The prospector preferred to keep his hopes a size or two larger, and he went off to the next excitement with the same light heart as before.

He had, moreover, the whole weight of mining tradition on his side. The lore of the camps was full of stories in which the expected thing did not happen. The experts had condemned the ground, and the greenhorn had found the ledge. It was a favourite notion that the richest claims had been given the most dismal names, as if the men who staked them had been so sure of failure that they wished to have the laugh on themselves in advance. [example needed] Whether or not this was so, it was believed, and the belief did much to keep up the spirits of the camp. A man whose prospects looked hopeless could reflect that hopelessness was the very sign of a bonanza.

I must add a word about drink, since it was so constant a companion of these adventures. It was commonly supposed that strong drink made a man witty. I have learned, however, that the men of science are of a different opinion. They tell us that liquor does not quicken the wits at all. What it does is to put to sleep the inner critic who ordinarily sits in judgment upon our sayings. The drinker is not cleverer than before; he has merely ceased to notice that he is not. This explains a good deal of the humour that was admired in the saloons, and also why it seldom survived until the next morning.

## Draft B

The frontier prospector has a way of taking bad luck that would break most people. He sinks everything into a claim, the claim comes to nothing, and he laughs. He finds a promising vein, it pinches out after a week, and he laughs again. He treats each setback as a prank the mountain has played on him, a joke at his expense that he is happy to be in on. What he does not do is learn from it. The next claim gets the same hope, the same money and the same cheerful certainty that this time it will pay. He is not foolish about it, exactly. He simply seems to have decided that learning would spoil the fun.

There is some reason in this. Mining lore is full of stories that reward the man who ignores the obvious. The expert says there is nothing in a hillside, and somebody who knows no better digs there and strikes it rich. The claim everyone passed over turns out to be the best in the district. Even the names tell the story. Prospectors gave their claims gloomy, mocking names, the sort of name you give a hole you expect to abandon, and some of those very claims hid great wealth. [example needed] A man raised on such stories has little use for caution. Experience teaches him that experience is unreliable, and he takes the lesson to heart.

So boom and bust are not, to him, a cycle to be managed. They are the weather. When times are good he spends freely, and when times are bad he waits for them to turn, confident that they will. Neither state seems to change his temper much. The same easy good humour carries him through both.

A word on drink, since it keeps company with this kind of life. It is commonly believed that liquor loosens the tongue and sharpens the wit, that a man who has had a few is funnier and bolder than he is sober. Scientists who have looked into the matter say otherwise. What drink does, they report, is dull a man's capacity for criticising himself. He is not actually wittier. He has merely stopped noticing when he is not. The jokes are the same jokes; the difference is that the teller has lost the part of his mind that would have kept the weaker ones to himself.

That may explain some of what passes for high spirits in a mining camp. It does not explain the prospector's cheerfulness, which he carries sober just as well.

## Draft C

The prospector has a philosophy of his own, and it is a cheerful one. He has lived through the boom and he has lived through the bust, and he has come to regard both with the same good-humoured tolerance. When the town that was to have been a metropolis dwindles to a saloon and a signpost, he does not complain of the injustice of Providence. He treats the whole affair as a practical joke of which he happens to be the victim, and he enjoys it almost as much as if he had played it on somebody else.

What he does not do is learn anything from it. This is the part of his philosophy that most disturbs the moralist. Experience, we are told, is a hard teacher, but the prospector has discovered that it is a teacher whose lessons need not be taken too seriously. He goes out to the next excitement with precisely the same confidence with which he went out to the last. He has been fooled before, and he fully expects to be fooled again. But he would not for the world miss the pleasure of being fooled.

Nor is he altogether wrong. The lore of the mining camps is full of stories in which the expected thing did not happen and the unexpected thing did. The claim that the experts condemned turned out to be the richest in the district. The men who named their holdings in a spirit of gloomy irony, as though to forestall disappointment, found that the most dismal names concealed the greatest wealth. [example needed: a gloomy claim name that proved rich] In such a country a reasonable man is at a disadvantage. Prudence is a virtue in a settled community, but on the frontier it is merely a way of missing the next strike.

A word should be said about the prospector's drink, since he himself would say a good deal about it. He believes that liquor quickens his wit, and in the evening his conversation certainly seems to him more brilliant than in the morning. But the men of science take a less generous view. They tell us that alcohol does not sharpen the wit at all; it only dulls the faculty of self-criticism, so that a man ceases to notice how dull he has become. The improvement, in other words, is entirely in the audience, and the audience is himself.
