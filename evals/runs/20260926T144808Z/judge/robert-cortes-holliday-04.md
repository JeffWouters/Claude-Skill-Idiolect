# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-04

## The passage (by the author)

We have now to record an extraordinary adventure. Our later education was derived in some considerable measure from the writings of Mr. Henry James. This to explain our emotion. We had never expected to behold himself, the illustrious expatriate who had so far enlightened an unkempt mind. But the night before we had been talking of him. Indeed, it is impossible for us to fail to perceive here something of the supernatural.

But hold! "William Edwards," says a newspaper notice, "who used to drive a post stage between New York and Albany, died on Saturday at his home. He was born in Albany," and so and so, "and many were the stories he had to tell of incidents connected with the famous men who were his passengers." Even so. We were ourselves a clerk. That is, for a number of years we waited on customers in a celebrated book shop. This is one of the stories we have to tell of the personages who were, so to say, our passengers. Or perhaps we are more in the nature of those unscrupulous English footmen to high society, of whom we have heard, who "sell out" their observation and information to the society press.

Anyhow, we are of a loquacious, gossipy turn; and we were booksellers, so to speak, to crowned heads. We have recently heard, too, of another precedent to our garrulous performance, the publication in Rome of the memoirs of an old waiter, who carefully set down the relative liberality of prominent persons whom he served. After having served Cardinals Rampolla and Merry del Val, this excellent memoirist entered opposite their names, "Both no good." With this we drop the defensive.

We noticed Mr. Wharton sitting down, legs crossed, smoking a cigar. Awaiting, we presumed, his wife. A not unpicturesque figure, tall, rather dashing in effect, ruddy visage, dragoon moustache, and habited in a light, smartly-cut sack suit of rather arresting checks, conspicuous grey spats; a gentleman manifesting no interest whatever in his surroundings.

## Draft A

I ought to say at the outset that I owe a great part of whatever reading education I possess to a certain novelist. I read him before I ever saw him; I read him, indeed, with the uncritical devotion of a young man who has discovered that books may be about people very like the people he knows. Later, behind the counter of a bookstore, I met him. That is, I sold him books. It is not quite the same thing as meeting a man, but it is, in its way, a fairly intimate acquaintance.

I am aware that there is something a little undignified in the position of one who sets down his recollections of the celebrated persons he has served. The coachman who drove the great statesman has his memories. So has the footman, and the lady's maid, and the whole chattering company of servants below stairs, who know, or believe they know, precisely what went on above. There is even, I am told, a memoir by a waiter of ancient Rome, who observed the notable men of his day across the dishes he carried. I do not claim to be better than these. I claim only to be one of them; and I have noticed that the reading public is seldom unwilling to listen to the coachman.

Well, then. Let me begin with a man waiting.

He stood near the front of the store, a little apart from the counters. He was extremely well dressed: a coat of excellent cut, gloves of the proper shade, a stick held at the proper angle, the whole effect expensive, quiet, and faintly severe. He was not looking at the books. He was looking at the door. Now and then he consulted his watch, put it away, and resumed his contemplation of the door. Customers passed and repassed him; he did not seem to notice them. Who he was I did not then know.

## Draft B

For some years I sold books, and in that time a number of well-known people came through the door of the shop where I worked. I have been asked more than once to set down what I remember of them, and I have hesitated, because I am not sure that a salesman's memories have any real claim on a reader's time.

I will admit one debt at the start. Much of whatever reading education I have, I owe to a novelist whose books I went through long before I ever imagined I would meet him. I read him as a young man reads, greedily and without much judgement, and he taught me more about what a book could do than any schoolroom did. Later he became one of the customers, and I served him across the counter. That is a strange thing to happen to anyone, and I have never quite got used to it.

I know what sort of company I am putting myself in by writing this. Coachmen have told what they overheard from the box. Servants have gossiped about the households they worked in. There is even, I am told, a memoir by a waiter in ancient Rome recalling the great men he fed. I have no wish to add to the list of those who make a living from other people's private moments, and I hope I shall not. What I saw was mostly ordinary, and I mean to report it that way.

Let me begin with a picture rather than a principle. A man is standing in the shop, waiting. He is well dressed, not showily but with care, and he holds himself with the patience of someone who is used to being attended to eventually. He is not looking at the books. He is looking at the door, or at the clock, or at nothing in particular. [detail needed] I did not know then who he was.

## Draft C

I was for some years a clerk in a bookstore. I am not so now. But a good part of whatever reading I have done, and nearly all of the direction of it, I owe to a certain novelist, [novelist], whose books I had read long before I had any notion that I should one day wait upon him across a counter. That I should later meet him seemed at the time, and seems still, a curious turn of things.

What follows, then, is a bookseller's recollections of the famous persons who came in. I am aware how this sort of thing looks. It is the kind of thing that coachmen might write, if coachmen wrote; that the servants in a great house do, in fact, deliver, in the kitchen, of an evening. "And then says she to him--." There is a long tradition of it. Somewhere, I believe, there is the memoir of a waiter in ancient Rome, who set down what the great men of his day said and ate and how they tipped. I claim no better standing than his. I served the dishes, so to speak, and I watched the diners. A man behind a counter sees his customers, as the waiter sees his, at a peculiarly unguarded angle.

Consider, as a sort of first exhibit, a gentleman waiting in the store. He is very well dressed. He is dressed, you would say, with care, but with a care so perfect that it has ceased to be visible and become merely a quality of the man. His gloves are in one hand. His stick is under his arm. He stands a little apart from the tables, not touching anything, looking, not at the books, but at the door, with an air of calm and considerable patience, as one who has been kept waiting before by better people than whoever it is he is waiting for now. Somebody will come for him, presently. Meanwhile he waits, and we look at him, and he knows we look at him, and does not mind.

He is, you understand, only a beginning.
