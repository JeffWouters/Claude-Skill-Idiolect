## The passage (by the author)

There is also a subtler distortion at work. Status updates tend to describe activity rather than understanding; they report what has been done, which is easy to enumerate, rather than what has been learned, which is often more important but harder to articulate. A team may complete a great many tasks while its grasp of the underlying problem remains as uncertain as it was at the outset, and the update will faithfully record the tasks while saying almost nothing about the uncertainty. Conversely, a team may spend a period apparently producing little, while in fact resolving a fundamental question that will make everything subsequent far easier; the update, which has no category for such progress, will make that period look like a failure. You might say that the format rewards visible motion over invisible insight, and in doing so it shapes, perhaps more than we realise, what teams choose to work on.

What might a more truthful kind of update look like? It seems to me that it would make room for confidence as well as progress — for the reporter to say, in effect, how sure they are about what they are reporting, and what would change their mind. It would distinguish between the parts of the work that are well understood and the parts that remain obscure; it would treat an emerging concern as legitimate information rather than as an alarm that must be justified before it can be raised. Such updates are harder to write and harder to read, since they resist the comforting simplicity of a single colour. They also, I suspect, give the people who receive them a far more accurate sense of where things stand, which is presumably what the update was meant to provide in the first place.

Perhaps the difficulty of reporting status honestly is a symptom of something larger: the discomfort that institutions feel with uncertainty, and the corresponding pressure on individuals to present their work as more settled than it is. The update becomes a small performance of control, repeated at regular intervals, in which everyone understands that the performance is partly fictional but nobody wishes to be the first to abandon it. To change this is not a matter of redesigning a template but of cultivating a culture in which saying "I am not yet sure" is regarded as a sign of honesty rather than of weakness. We might discover, if we tried, that the most useful thing a status update can communicate is not how far we have come, but how clearly we can see the road ahead.

## Draft A

Most progress reports are, when you look at them closely, lists of things that have been done. This task was completed, that ticket was closed, a milestone was reached or narrowly missed. Such lists have the virtue of being easy to produce and easy to verify, and they satisfy a reasonable desire on the part of those who read them to know that work is happening. What they tend not to convey is whether anyone has learned anything. A team may complete a great many tasks while its understanding of the problem remains exactly where it was, or it may complete very few while discovering that the problem is quite different from the one it set out to solve. The second situation is arguably far more important to know about, and it is precisely the one that the conventional report is least equipped to express.

What would a better report look like? It seems to me that it would begin by stating, as plainly as possible, how confident the team is in its current direction and in its sense of what remains. It would identify the areas that are still unclear — the parts of the work where assumptions have not yet been tested, where dependencies are poorly understood, or where the team simply does not know what it will find. It might even distinguish between the kind of uncertainty that further work is likely to resolve and the kind that will persist until something outside the team's control has happened. None of this requires an elaborate template. It requires only that the report be treated as a vehicle for understanding rather than a record of activity.

Here, though, lies the difficulty. Changing the form of a report is the easy part, and organisations are rather fond of doing it, since a new form has the satisfying appearance of reform. But a field labelled "confidence" will be filled in optimistically if those filling it in have reason to believe that low confidence will be held against them. A section for open questions will remain conspicuously empty if raising questions has, in the past, invited scrutiny rather than help. The form can only carry what the culture permits it to carry.

The real work, then, is to make the admission of doubt acceptable — to ensure that a report which says "we are not sure" is received as useful information rather than as a warning sign about the people who wrote it. That is a matter of how leaders respond, repeatedly and visibly, to the honest reports they receive. It cannot be designed into a form; it can only be demonstrated, one response at a time.

## Draft B

What does a progress report actually tell the person who reads it? On the surface it tells them a great deal, since it lists the tasks completed, the tasks in progress and the tasks still to come, often in careful detail. It may be that this detail is precisely what conceals the more important information, which is what the team has learned since the last report and how that learning has changed its view of the work ahead. A list of finished tasks describes activity; it says very little about understanding.

You may have written reports of this kind yourself, and noticed how naturally they fall into the shape of a ledger. The form asks what has been done, and so that is what gets written, while the discoveries that might matter more (that a component is less stable than assumed, or that a requirement means something different from what everyone thought) find no obvious place to go. I suspect most readers sense the gap without being able to name it, and compensate by asking for more reports, which only produces more lists.

There is also a quieter cost to reporting in this way. When the only acceptable content is completed work, the areas that remain unclear tend to disappear from view until they can no longer be ignored, and by then the options for dealing with them have narrowed considerably. A report that stated, alongside its list, how confident the team felt about the remaining work and which parts were still poorly understood would arguably be shorter and more useful at once; it would direct attention to where it is needed rather than spreading it evenly across everything.

It seems to me, however, that changing the template will achieve rather little on its own. People already know how to express doubt; what they lack is any assurance that doing so will be received as useful rather than as a sign of weakness. An organisation that adds a field for confidence to its reports, while continuing to treat every admission of uncertainty as bad news, will simply receive a field filled with optimism. The harder work lies in how leaders respond when someone does write that a part of the project is unclear, whether they ask what would help or ask why it was not already solved.

The progress report is, in the end, one of the few occasions in working life on which a team formally tells others what it knows. Perhaps its value depends less on what it lists than on what it is safe to admit.

## Draft C

Progress reports are supposed to tell the people reading them how a piece of work is really going. Most of them do something narrower. They list what has been done: tickets closed, features merged, milestones ticked off, meetings held. It is a record of activity, and activity is easy to count.

What they rarely capture is what the team has learned. Yet on most projects, and especially on uncertain ones, that is the more important kind of progress. Discovering that an integration is harder than expected, that a key assumption was wrong, or that a whole area of the plan is poorly understood can matter far more than another five completed tasks. Those discoveries tend to go missing from reports, because there is no column for them and because they often look like bad news.

The result is a familiar pattern. Reports look steadily positive for weeks, full of completed items, while the real picture of the work quietly shifts underneath them. When the gap finally surfaces, it arrives as a surprise to everyone except the people who were doing the work.

Better reports would do two things that most current ones do not. First, they would state confidence. Not just "we finished these items" but "we are fairly sure about this part, less sure about that one, and here is why". A simple statement of how confident the team is in the remaining plan says more than a long list of completed tasks. Second, they would flag the areas that are still unclear: the parts nobody has looked at properly yet, the dependencies that have not been confirmed, the questions without answers. Naming those areas early gives everyone a chance to act on them while there is still room to do so. [example needed]

It is tempting to treat this as a template problem and to fix it by adding a confidence field and an "open questions" section to the report form. That helps a little, but not much. People already know how to write down doubt. What stops them is what happens next. If admitting uncertainty leads to extra scrutiny, sharper questions from above or an implied judgement that the team is not in control, people will learn to leave those fields blank or fill them with reassurance.

So the real change has to be in how organisations respond. Readers of reports need to treat an honest statement of doubt as useful information, and to reward the people who give it rather than question their competence. Leaders can set the tone by sharing their own uncertainty openly and by thanking teams that raise concerns early. Once admitting doubt is safe, the forms will start telling the truth almost on their own.
