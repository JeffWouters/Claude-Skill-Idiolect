# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-07

## The passage (by the author)

One can usually either begin or end with Mr. Chesterton, though one can seldom do both. “It is simpler to eat caviare on impulse than to eat grape-nuts on principle,” he says, in one of his intervals of pure lucidity. I should like to make a Chestertonian transposition, and pronounce that it is better (I do not say simpler) to eat caviare on principle than to eat grape-nuts on impulse. The fact is that the modern fad of simplicity for its own sake has ceased to be merely ridiculous; it has become dangerous. May not some of us lift our voices against it?

I have no right, I suppose, to ally, in my own mind, socialists and vegetarians. But I nearly always find, when I ask a vegetarian if he is a socialist, or a socialist if he is a vegetarian, that the answer is in the affirmative. I am sure that they, on their side, confuse snobs with meat-eaters. One could forgive them, were they more bitterly logical. For my own part, I should be quite willing to go the length of all Hinduism and say that rice itself has a soul. I can even see myself joining a “movement” for giving the vote to violets and disfranchising orchids. This, however, is not their desire. They do not wish to make even the ox a citizen--only a brother; and I have never discovered that vegetarians--even when they were “hygienic,” not “sentimental,” ones--were anxious to reproduce the history of the rice-fed peoples. But let their logic take care of itself. My point is really that socialists and vegetarians are banded together to fight for the simplifying of life. Socialism, of course, organizes as furiously as Capital itself; and I leave it to any one if a nut-cutlet is not complicated to the point of mendacity. But ostensibly both sects are on the side of Procrustes against human vagaries. Both would surely consider caviare immoral; either because no one ought to eat it, or because every one cannot. It does not much matter, I fancy, which point you make against the dried roe of the sturgeon. My own plea for caviare rests precisely on the fact that it is not, and cannot be, thrust into every one’s mouth. It is not simple, no. The only really “simple” food-stuff is manna. Imagine, for example, calling anything simple that has to be shot out of a cannon by way of preparation. In point of fact, very few people eat caviare save on impulse,--otherwise, they find it too nasty. But it is an impulse worthy of being dogmatized; of becoming a principle.

## Draft A

I should like to say a word for caviar. It is not, I admit, a subject that seems to call for defence; but the fashion for plain living has gone so far of late that even caviar needs a champion. Not long ago the cult of simplicity was merely silly, and one could afford to smile at it, go home, and eat a proper supper with a clear conscience. It has become something worse than silly. It has become a doctrine, with its apostles and its martyrs, and it is beginning to do real harm.

It is curious how often the apostles of simplicity turn out to be socialists as well, and how often the socialists turn out to be vegetarians. The overlap is not an accident. Both creeds, at bottom, have the same end in view: they wish to level every one down to a common standard, and they are quite content that the standard should be a low one. The vegetarian would have us all eat lentils because some of us cannot get beef; the socialist would have us all do without because some of us cannot get enough. Neither of them, so far as I can discover, proposes to raise anybody up. Their equality, in short, is the equality of the bread-line.

Now caviar is, frankly, an unequal thing. It is costly, it is rare, and it is not, and in the nature of things cannot be, available to all. And part of its value--I will not pretend otherwise--lies precisely in that fact. Granted that this sounds shocking; but consider. A pleasure that every one, rich or poor, clever or stupid, can have at any moment of the day is hardly a pleasure at all, and nobody values it. The savour of a rare thing is partly the savour of its rarity, and to abolish the rarity would be to abolish the savour with it. You would not make the world richer by distributing caviar to the multitude. You would merely make caviar common.

There is more to it than that. Nobody, I fancy, likes caviar the first time he tastes it. It is an acquired taste, and like all acquired tastes, whether for olives, for poetry, or for the society of intelligent people, it represents a certain effort of education. The palate that has learned to relish it has been trained, as the ear is trained to relish a fugue. Such acquired indulgences are not vices to be apologised for. They are the small victories of cultivation over crude appetite, and they deserve to be raised into principles. The simple-lifers would throw them all away. I would rather keep them, and keep the caviar too.

## Draft B

I wish to say a word for caviar. It is not that caviar is in any danger of disappearing; it is that the people who dislike it are becoming, in my opinion, a public menace. The craze for simplicity has gone a good deal further than anyone expected. Not so very long ago it was merely silly--a matter of brown bread and sandals and earnest conversation. It is now something worse. It has become a doctrine; and a doctrine, unlike a fad, does harm.

I have noticed--and I do not think I am alone in noticing--that the socialist and the vegetarian are very often the same person. It is not an accident. They have, at bottom, the same temper of mind. The one wishes that nobody should have more money than anybody else; the other wishes that nobody should eat anything more interesting than a lentil. Both of them, in the name of justice or of health, are engaged in the same enterprise: they wish to level us all down. They would have us all live on the same diet, in the same kind of house, with the same thoughts in our heads; and they would call the result equality. I call it a very dull dinner.

Now caviar is the perfect answer to them both. It is not wholesome, in the vegetarian's sense; and it is not democratic, in the socialist's. It cannot be had by everybody. Let us admit that frankly, for there is no use pretending otherwise. But I am prepared to go further, and to say that its not being had by everybody is part of what makes it worth having. A delicacy that everyone ate every day would cease, by that very fact, to be a delicacy. Its value lies partly in its rarity; and to complain of its rarity is to complain of the thing itself.

Nor is caviar a taste that one is born with. Nobody, I suppose, likes it the first time. It is an acquired taste--which is only to say that it is a taste one has taken trouble over. And that, it seems to me, is exactly why it deserves respect. The things we have learned to enjoy, by patience and by effort, are the things that are most truly ours. Our acquired indulgences are not weaknesses to be apologised for; they are the beginnings of a discrimination, and discrimination, once it is firmly rooted, ought to harden into principle. I do not ask anyone to eat caviar. I only ask that those of us who have learned to like it should not be made to feel ashamed. We have earned it. And we have earned, too, the right to say so.

## Draft C

For some years now the fashion for plain living has been growing, and I had been content to regard it as merely silly. I am no longer content. What began as a harmless affectation has become something that does real damage, because it teaches people to be ashamed of pleasure and to mistake narrowness for virtue.

Let me take caviar as my text, since it is the very emblem of what the simplifiers dislike. It is costly, it is rare, it is not needed by anyone, and a great many people do not care for it on first acquaintance. On every count it is the sort of thing that the prophets of simplicity would have us give up. I propose to defend it.

It is worth noticing who the prophets are. Socialists and vegetarians are not the same people, but they overlap more often than chance would explain, and they share a temperament. Both look at the table and wish to clear it. Both are suspicious of any pleasure that not everyone can share, and both would rather see everyone reduced to the same plain fare than see a few enjoy what the many cannot. Their programme, whatever they call it, is to level down. [example needed]

Now it must be admitted that part of caviar's value lies precisely in its not being available to all. The simplifier treats this as a confession of guilt. I treat it as a plain fact about how pleasure works. A delicacy that everyone ate every day would cease to be a delicacy; it would become a staple, and we should need to find some other thing to be delighted by. Rarity is not the whole of the charm, but it is part of it, and there is nothing shameful in saying so. The same is true of fine wine, good books in good bindings, and every other refinement that must be sought out rather than simply handed over.

There is a further point. Caviar is an acquired taste. No one loves it at once; one learns to love it, through a little effort and a little patience. That makes it, in a small way, an achievement. And acquired indulgences of this kind deserve better than to be treated as weaknesses. They deserve to become principles: settled convictions that the world contains things worth the trouble of learning to enjoy, and that a life which refuses them all is not purer but poorer.

The simple life asks us to give up our pleasures one by one and to call the result a virtue. I would rather keep my caviar, and keep, along with it, the conviction that taste is worth cultivating and that not everything good must be shared equally to be good at all.
