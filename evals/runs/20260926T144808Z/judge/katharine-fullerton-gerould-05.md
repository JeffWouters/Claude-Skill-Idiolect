# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-05

## The passage (by the author)

Materialism in all classes.

The idolatry of science.

Only one of these is purely intellectual; two might almost be called political. In point of fact, all four are interwoven.

I should be insultingly trite if I proceeded here to expound the fallacy of the historic statement that all men are born free and equal. We have all known for a long time that individual freedom and individual equality cannot co-exist. I dare say no one since Thomas Jefferson (and may I express my doubts even of that inspired charlatan?) has really believed it. No one could believe it at the present day except the people who are flattered by it; and of people who are flattered by it, it is obviously not true. The democracy of the present day--like the aristocracy of another day--is fostered by the people whom it advantages; and the people whom it advantages are adding themselves, at the rate of a million a year, to our census lists. When even democracy has to reckon with the fact that its premises are all wrong, and that men are not born equal--that hierarchies are inherent in human kind regardless of birth or opportunity--it proceeds to do its utmost to equalize artificially; it becomes Procrustes. But will any one contend that Procrustes left people free?

Now, what has this to do with culture? Simply this: that culture is not a democratic achievement, because culture is inherently snobbish. Contact with “the best that has been thought and said in the world” makes people intellectually exclusive, and makes them draw distinctions. Those distinctions, seriously speaking, are not founded on social origins or great possessions; they are founded on states of mind. So long as democracy is simply a political matter, culture is left free to select its groups and proclaim its hierarchies. But it is characteristic of our democracy that political equality has not sufficed to it; the “I am as good as you are” formula has been flung out to every horizon. The people with whom it has become a mania insist that their equality with every one else in their range of vision is a moral, an intellectual, a social, as well as a political, equality. Let that formula prevail, and culture, with its eternal distinction-drawing, will naturally die. For contact with the best that has been thought and said in the world induces a mighty humility--and a mighty scorn of those who do not know enough to be humble before the Masters. They are an impersonal humility and an impersonal scorn--attitudes of the mind, both, not of the heart. But humility and scorn are both ruled, theoretically, out of the democratic court.

## Draft A

There is one idea, more than any other, which stands in the way of cultivated learning in this country; and that is the idea that men are naturally equal. I know it is not a popular thing to say. But it seems to me that the idea is simply false, and that everybody who has ever watched a nursery or a schoolroom knows it to be false. Children are not equal; grown people are not equal; and no amount of legislation will make them so. Wherever human beings are gathered together, some of them will rise and some of them will sink, and a hierarchy of one sort or another will appear. We may abolish one kind of rank. We shall only find another springing up in its place.

Now culture is, by its very nature, exclusive. I do not mean that it is exclusive in the way that a fashionable club is exclusive. It does not care who your grandfather was, and it does not care what is in your purse. The poorest boy may enter it, and the richest may be turned away at the door. But it ranks. It ranks minds--it says, quite frankly, that this mind is finer than that, and this taste truer, and this judgment sounder. That is what it is for. A culture which refused to make such distinctions would not be a culture at all; it would be merely an accumulation of information, and information (it is always information that we are offered, nowadays!) is a very different thing.

Let us admit, by all means, that political equality is a good thing. I do not propose to quarrel with the ballot. But we are no longer content with equality in politics. We demand it in every sphere--in manners, in speech, in education, in taste. We are made uneasy by anyone who seems to think himself better informed than his neighbour, and we call him a snob; and we are made still more uneasy by anyone who admits that he knows less, and we call him a fool for admitting it. [example needed]

And there, I think, is the heart of the matter. For culture breeds two things which the thoroughgoing egalitarian cannot abide. It breeds humility, because the cultivated man knows how much finer minds than his own have gone before him. And it breeds disdain--a decent, quiet disdain for the shoddy and the second-rate. The two go together; one cannot have the first without the second. But in a world where everyone must be as good as everyone else, there is no room for either. We must not bow, and we must not scorn. And so, very naturally, we cease to be cultivated.

## Draft B

Of all the forces that work against cultivated learning, none is more steady or more thorough than the ideology of equality. I do not mean equality before the law, or at the ballot box, which is a sound principle and a hard-won one. I mean the broader belief that people are, by nature, equal, and that any arrangement which ranks them is a wrong to be put right.

That belief is simply false. People differ in strength, in patience, in memory, in taste, in the quickness and depth of their minds. No amount of good will alters this, and no society has ever managed to organise itself without some form of hierarchy. Abolish one ranking and another appears in its place, often cruder than the one it replaced. The question is never whether there will be distinctions among people, but which distinctions we will honour. [example needed]

Culture is, by its nature, one of those distinctions. It is exclusive, and there is no use pretending otherwise. But it is worth being precise about what it excludes. It does not rank people by wealth, since a rich man may be entirely uncultivated and a poor one deeply read. It does not rank them by birth, since no one inherits a trained mind. It ranks minds: their range, their discipline, their capacity to recognise what is fine and to tell it from what is merely popular. That is an aristocracy anyone may enter, but only by the long road of effort, and many will not take it.

The egalitarian cannot rest content with political equality. He wants equality in every sphere, in taste and judgment and learning as well as in rights, and so he comes to regard any claim of superiority in these things as an offence. A person who says that one book is better than another, or one mind better furnished than another, is accused of snobbery. The very act of judging becomes suspect.

This leaves culture with nowhere to stand, because culture depends on judgment and on the feelings that judgment breeds. It breeds humility, in the person who measures himself against the great and finds himself small. It also breeds disdain, a quiet and necessary disdain for the shoddy, the facile and the second-rate. Both are unwelcome in a society that insists all things are equally worthy. Humility looks like self-abasement, and disdain looks like arrogance, and so both are discouraged, and with them the whole habit of mind that made learning worth pursuing.

We can keep our political equality and keep our culture too. What we cannot do is extend equality into every sphere and still expect cultivation to survive there.

## Draft C

The trouble with cultivated learning in a democracy is not that people dislike it. They are, for the most part, quite willing to admire it from a distance. The trouble is that it rests on an assumption which the democratic temper cannot bring itself to accept: the assumption that some minds are better than others. We have been taught, from the nursery up, that men are naturally equal. It is a noble sentiment, and in its proper place a useful fiction; but it is not true, and nobody who has spent an hour in a schoolroom believes that it is. Men are not equal in strength, in beauty, in industry, or in wit. Wherever they are gathered together, a hierarchy appears, whether we like it or not. We may abolish titles and forbid privileges; the hierarchy will simply reassert itself in another shape.

Culture is, by its very nature, exclusive. It ranks people; that is what it is for. But--and this is the point that its enemies always miss--it does not rank them by wealth or by birth. The son of a millionaire may be a barbarian, and the son of a village cobbler may be the most finished scholar of his generation. Culture asks only one question: what can your mind do? It is the least snobbish of all aristocracies, and it is an aristocracy none the less. That is precisely why the egalitarian distrusts it.

Now I have no quarrel with political equality. Let every one vote, by all means, and let every one stand equal before the law. The mischief begins when equality is demanded, not in politics only, but in every sphere of life--in taste, in learning, in the very estimate we form of one another's minds. For culture breeds two things which such a demand cannot tolerate. It breeds humility, because the cultivated person is always aware of how much greater minds have gone before him; and it breeds a certain disdain, because he cannot pretend that the second-rate is as good as the first. Neither humility nor disdain has any place in a world where every opinion is held to be as good as every other. The one is thought servile and the other insolent. And so, between the two, culture is squeezed out, and we are left with a comfortable, levelled mediocrity which calls itself enlightenment. I fancy it is nothing of the sort.
