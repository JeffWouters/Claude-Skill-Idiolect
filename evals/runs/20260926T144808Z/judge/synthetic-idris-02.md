## The passage (by the author)

There is a considerable literature on techniques that promise to improve the situation — relative sizing, probabilistic forecasting, the breaking down of work into ever smaller pieces — and I do not wish to dismiss any of them, since each captures something true about the problem. Yet I am struck by how often these techniques are adopted as a kind of procedural reassurance rather than as a genuine change in how uncertainty is discussed. A team may replace its hours with points, or its points with ranges, while the conversation surrounding the estimate remains exactly as it was: somebody asks when the work will be finished, somebody else provides an answer they suspect is optimistic, and everyone proceeds as though the answer were reliable. The technique changes; the institutional relationship to uncertainty does not. It may be that no method of estimation can compensate for an organisation that is unwilling to hear the word "unknown".

What would it look like to take uncertainty seriously? You might begin by treating every estimate as a statement about the present state of our knowledge rather than about the future, and by expecting it to change as that knowledge changes — which is to say, by treating revision as a sign of learning rather than as a failure of discipline. You might also distinguish, more carefully than we usually do, between the parts of a project that resemble work we have done before and the parts that are genuinely novel, since the former can often be estimated with reasonable confidence while the latter resist prediction almost entirely. None of this is especially original; it is, arguably, what thoughtful engineers have been saying for a very long time. The obstacle has never been a shortage of ideas but a reluctance, on all sides, to relinquish the comfort that a precise-sounding figure provides.

## Draft A

Every few years a new method of estimation arrives in software teams with the promise that, this time, forecasting will become reliable. Hours give way to ideal days, ideal days to story points, story points to T-shirt sizes or to the refusal to estimate at all, and each change is accompanied by a brief period of optimism before the familiar disappointments return. It is tempting to conclude that we simply have not yet found the right unit. I suspect the more accurate conclusion is that the unit was never the problem. What these methods tend to leave untouched is the attitude a team and its organisation hold towards uncertainty — the expectation that a number, once spoken, should remain fixed, and that any later change to it is a kind of failure. Replacing hours with points does nothing to that expectation; it merely gives it a new vocabulary.

A more useful starting point, it seems to me, is to treat an estimate as a statement about what we currently know rather than a prediction we are bound to honour. Understood this way, an estimate ought to move as knowledge grows, and a team that revises its figures as it learns is not being unreliable but is doing exactly what an honest forecaster should. The discomfort arises only when the organisation receiving the estimate has not been invited to share this understanding, and so reads every revision as a broken promise.

There is also a distinction worth drawing within the work itself. Some of what a team is asked to do closely resembles things it has done before, and for this work past experience offers a reasonable guide; other work is genuinely new, and experience offers much less. Predictions tend to go wrong when the two are blended into a single figure, as though the familiar and the unknown carried the same kind of risk. Separating them, and being candid about which is which, would not make forecasting certain. It might, however, make it truthful.

## Draft B

Every few years a new estimation method arrives with the promise that this time software forecasting will work. Hours give way to story points, story points to T-shirt sizes, T-shirt sizes to throughput counts. Teams adopt the new unit, run a few planning sessions with it, and within a year the forecasts are about as unreliable as they were before.

The reason is that the unit was never the problem. What undermines most forecasts is the attitude to doubt that sits underneath them. If an organisation treats an estimate as a commitment the moment it is spoken, people will pad it, defend it and hide the uncertainty in it, whatever it is measured in. Swapping hours for points changes the vocabulary of the conversation, not its dynamics. The same reluctance to say "we don't know yet" survives every change of method.

A healthier starting point is to accept that an estimate is a statement about what we know today. It should be expected to move as that knowledge grows. An estimate that never changes over the life of a piece of work is not a sign of accuracy; it is usually a sign that nobody is updating it. Revising a forecast when you learn something new is the method working, not failing.

It also helps to stop treating all work as the same kind of thing. Some of what a team does is familiar: it has built something similar before, it knows the systems involved and it can lean on its own history to predict how long it will take. Other work is genuinely new, and history has little to say about it. Mixing the two into a single number hides the difference. Forecast the familiar parts with confidence, and be explicit that the new parts carry wide uncertainty until the team has explored them.

## Draft C

Why is it that each new method of estimation, however promising it looks at first, leaves software forecasts roughly as unreliable as they were before? A team exchanges hours for points, or points for sizes, or sizes for some other unit that has lately come into fashion, and for a while the change feels like progress. It may be that the unit was never the problem. What stays the same through every such exchange is the attitude to doubt, which is to say the habit of treating uncertainty as an embarrassment to be hidden, rather than as information to be shared.

An estimate, in the end, is only a statement of what we know at the moment we make it. We tend to treat it as a fixed quantity, to be defended or apologised for, when it would be more honest to expect it to move as our knowledge of the work grows; a forecast that never changes is arguably less trustworthy than one that does, since it suggests that nobody has been learning. I suspect many teams sense this already, but find it difficult to say aloud to the people waiting on the date.

It seems to me that the more useful step is to separate, before any number is given, the work that resembles things the team has done before from the work that is genuinely new. The first can be predicted with some confidence, because there is experience to draw on; the second can only be bounded, and perhaps revisited once the first uncertainties have been explored. Treating the two alike, in whatever unit, produces a figure that is too confident about the unknown and too cautious about the familiar. Perhaps what needs to change is not how we count, but how willing we are to admit what we cannot yet know.
