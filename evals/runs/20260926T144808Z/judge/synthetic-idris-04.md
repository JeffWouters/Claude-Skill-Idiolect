# Judge packet synthetic-idris-04

## The passage (by the author)

What does it feel like to become a beginner again after a long period of competence? Most of us in technical work will face the question repeatedly, since the tools and languages and platforms we depend upon change with a regularity that makes permanent expertise almost impossible; the skills that made us valuable not long ago may now be of diminishing relevance, and the new skills we need must be acquired while we continue to be expected to perform. It is an uncomfortable position, arguably more uncomfortable for experienced practitioners than for newcomers, since the experienced have grown accustomed to the feeling of knowing what they are doing and have perhaps forgotten what it is like not to. I suspect that a great deal of resistance to new technology, which is usually described in terms of technical merit, is really a resistance to that feeling of incompetence. We defend the familiar tool not because it is better but because we are better at it, and because the alternative would require us to be seen struggling.

There is also the question of where the learning is supposed to happen. Organisations speak warmly about continuous learning and professional development, and some of them genuinely mean it; yet the actual structure of most working weeks leaves remarkably little room for the slow, inefficient, exploratory activity that real learning requires. You are expected to acquire the new skill somehow, perhaps in the margins of the day or in your own time, while delivering at the level you delivered before. Is it surprising that people tend to learn only as much as is immediately necessary, picking up fragments of a new technology in the course of solving particular problems without ever developing a coherent understanding of how it works? The result is a kind of patchwork competence, adequate for the tasks at hand but brittle whenever something unexpected occurs.

## Draft A

There comes a point in many engineering careers when the technology one has mastered is quietly superseded, and a person who has spent years being the one others consult finds themselves, once again, a beginner. The experience is rarely discussed in these terms. We tend instead to talk about resistance to change, about engineers who dismiss a new framework or cling to familiar tooling, as though the objection were purely technical. Sometimes it is. More often, I suspect, the pushback conceals something less comfortable to name: the discomfort of feeling unskilled after a long period of feeling competent, and the fear that colleagues will notice. Criticising a tool is considerably easier than admitting that one does not yet understand it.

Organisations do not make this easier. Almost every workplace speaks warmly about professional development, and many publish statements about continuous learning and growth. Yet the calendar tells a different story. The time in which learning is supposed to happen is seldom set aside; it is assumed to occur somewhere between deliverables, in the evenings, or in the brief lulls that appear between one urgent task and the next. The message received by the experienced engineer is therefore a mixed one: learn the new thing, certainly, but do not let it slow anything down.

The consequence is a particular kind of knowledge — acquired in fragments, never quite consolidated, adequate for the task at hand but liable to give way under pressure. Learning squeezed into the gaps tends to be shallow, because depth requires sustained attention, and fragile, because what has not been properly understood cannot easily be adapted when circumstances change. It would be worth asking whether an organisation that wants its senior people to master new technology is prepared to accept the temporary loss of productivity that genuine learning demands. If it is not, it should perhaps be less surprised when the learning does not take.

## Draft B

What happens to an experienced engineer when the technology they know best is replaced by something they do not know at all? The usual answer is that they resist it, and the resistance is often described as conservatism or a reluctance to change. It may be that the objections raised against a new tool are sometimes a more respectable way of expressing something harder to admit, which is the discomfort of feeling unskilled again after years of being the person others consulted.

Consider what the change asks of such a person. You may have spent many years building an instinct for how a particular system behaves, and that instinct is suddenly of limited use; the questions that once took a moment now take an afternoon, and the colleagues who once came to you for answers now watch you search for them. It is hardly surprising that the tool becomes the target, since criticising the tool is considerably easier than confessing the loss.

There is also the matter of how little room workplaces actually make for learning. Organisations speak warmly of development, and arguably they mean it, yet the calendar rarely contains any time in which development could take place. Learning is squeezed into the gaps between deliverables, in half-hours stolen from other work, and what is learned in this way tends to be shallow and fragile, enough to get a task done but not enough to understand why it worked. I suspect this is where much of the resistance comes from, rather than from any attachment to the old tools.

It seems to me that the remedy lies less in persuading people to embrace the new than in giving them protected time, and permission, to be beginners for a while without apology. Perhaps the real question is not whether experienced engineers can start over, but whether we are willing to let them do it properly.

## Draft C

There is a particular kind of discomfort that comes with being very good at something and then having to start over. Experienced engineers meet it whenever the technology underneath their work changes: a new platform, a new language, a new way of building and shipping. Years of hard-won fluency suddenly count for less, and they find themselves asking the same basic questions as someone in their first job.

A lot of the pushback against new tools comes from that place. The objections are usually framed in technical terms, and some of them are fair. But underneath many of them is something less comfortable to say out loud: nobody enjoys feeling unskilled at work, especially when their standing rests on being the person who knows. Resistance can be a way of protecting that standing. Recognising this is not about dismissing the objections; it is about hearing what is actually being said.

Workplaces do not make it easier. Most of them talk warmly about development and continuous learning, then fill every week with delivery work and leave no real time for either. Learning is expected to happen somewhere in the gaps: an evening, a slow Friday afternoon, the half hour between meetings. The message people receive is that growth matters, as long as it costs nothing.

Learning squeezed into those gaps tends to be shallow. People pick up enough to get a task done, copy a pattern that seems to work and move on before they understand why it works. That knowledge is fragile. It holds up until something unusual happens, and then it breaks, often at the worst possible moment. [example needed]

If organisations want experienced people to embrace new technology rather than resist it, they need to take both problems seriously. Make it acceptable to be a beginner again, visibly and without loss of standing. And treat learning time as real work, planned and protected, rather than something people are trusted to find for themselves.
