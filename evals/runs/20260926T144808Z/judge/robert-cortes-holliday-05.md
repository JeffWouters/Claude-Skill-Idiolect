# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-05

## The passage (by the author)

Well, I told her, by way of making talk, that I believed I was held to be a reasonably honest person, and that I was frequently sober.

"Oh," she said, "I can see that you are a gentleman--in your way," she added, in a murmur.

So, you see, in hunting lodgings you not only see how others live, but how you seem to others.

It is certainly curious, the places in which to dwell which one is shown in hunting lodgings. Once I was given to view a room in which was a strange table-like affair constructed of metal. "You wouldn't mind, I suppose," said the lady of the lodging, "if this remained in the room?"

"Oh, not at all," I replied. "But what is it?"

"Why, it's an operating table," she explained. "Of course, you know," she added, "that I'm a physician. And," she continued, "of course I should want to make use of it now and then, but not regularly, not every day."

To a lady with a patch over her eye with lodgings to let in Broome Street I one time stated, by way of being communicative, that I was often in my room a good deal doing some work there. Ah! With many ogles and grimaces, she whispered hoarsely, with an effort at a sly effect, that "that was all right here. She understood," she said. Perfectly "safe place for that," it was. "The gentlemen who had the room before were something of the same kind."

As you know, "references" frequently are demanded of one hunting lodgings. To get into a really nice place one must really be a very nice person. "You know, I have a daughter," sighs the really nice landlady.

To obtain lodgings in Kensington one must be very well-to-do, particularly if one would be on the "drawing room floor." "I like these rooms very much," I said to a prim person there, and I hesitated.

## Draft A

Looking at rooms to rent is one of the few ways a stranger can walk into another person's house and be judged on the spot, and the judging is often the most entertaining part.

Landladies have their own methods. One will look at your boots before she looks at your face, as though the state of the leather told her everything about your habits. Another will ask what you do for a living and then listen not to the answer but to the way you give it. I have been approved of and doubted for reasons I could only guess at. [example needed] None of them ever explained their reasoning, and I came to suspect that most of them could not have done so. They simply knew, or believed they knew, within the first minute, and the rest of the visit was a formality.

The strangest room I was ever offered held a surgical table. It stood in the middle of the floor, solid and unmistakable, and the landlady showed me round it as if it were a sideboard. She mentioned the view, the light and the rent. She did not mention the table. I did not quite like to ask, and so I never learned whether it had belonged to a previous tenant, whether it was meant to stay, or what exactly I was expected to do with it. I thanked her and left, and I have wondered about it since.

At the other end of the scale are the upscale places, where the judging is done not by instinct but by paperwork. Here you do not charm anyone. You are asked for references, and then for more references, and it is made clear that the rooms are intended for people of means. The questions are polite and the answers are checked. There is no surgical table, and no one cares about your boots, because the matter has been settled well before you arrive by the size of your income.

I preferred the landladies. They were unpredictable and sometimes unfair, but at least they were looking at me.

## Draft B

The landlady, when she opens the door to you, is not showing you a room. She is looking you over. The room comes afterward, if at all.

Each has her own method. One studies your shoes. Another your hat. A third asks you, before you are fairly in the hall, what you do; and when you tell her, she repeats it, slowly, as though turning it over to see whether it has been mended. One, I remember, asked me whether I was "a quiet gentleman." I said that I believed I was. She said that they all said that. Another decided, after a very little conversation, that I was a teacher, and nothing I could say afterward would move her from this. She was kind to me, as one is kind to teachers.

In one house I was shown up two flights of stairs to a large back room, well lighted, reasonably clean, and furnished, I observed, chiefly with a surgical table. It stood in the middle of the floor. It was not explained. The landlady walked around it, pointing out the closet, the view, the gas. She did not mention the table, and neither, somehow, did I. I have since wondered a good deal about the previous occupant. I did not take the room.

The upscale places are another matter. There, one is not so much looked over as examined. There are no signs in the window; there is a hall, and a person in the hall, and a card is taken from you and carried away. You are asked for references. You are asked, in effect, though never in words, what you are worth. Whom do you know? Where have you lived? What are your means? You give your answers, and they are received politely, and written down, and you are told that you will hear.

You do not hear.

## Draft C

It is a curious thing, when you go about looking at rooms to rent, to discover that you are the one being looked at. You had supposed that you were inspecting the premises. Not at all. The premises, in the person of the landlady, are inspecting you; and they are forming, as you stand in the hall with your hat in your hand, a very definite opinion of your character.

The methods by which they arrive at this opinion are various and, I think, rather remarkable. Each landlady has her own system, and no two systems are alike. [example needed: one landlady's way of judging the visitor] [example needed: a second, contrasting way] What the systems have in common is their confidence, which is absolute, and their reasoning, which is never disclosed. You come away from such interviews with the strange sensation of having been weighed, measured, and entered in some ledger that you will never be permitted to read.

Then there was the room with the surgical table. The landlady showed it to me with perfect composure. It was, in other respects, an ordinary room, such as one might inhabit without remark; and there in the middle of it, solid and unmistakable, stood a surgical table. She did not explain it. I did not ask. We stood and regarded it together for a moment, in a silence that seemed to me, at any rate, considerably longer than it was, and then she went on to describe the advantages of the neighbourhood.

The more fashionable establishments are another matter altogether. There nobody troubles to divine your character; they ask for your references. They wish to know who you are, whom you know, and (though they are much too well bred to put it so) how much you possess. You are received, as it were, at the frontier, and your papers are examined with the courteous thoroughness of a customs official. I did not get past the frontier.
