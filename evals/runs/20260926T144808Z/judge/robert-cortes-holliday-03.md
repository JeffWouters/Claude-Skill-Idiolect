# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-03

## The passage (by the author)

Then there are the remarkable people who "keep up" with the flood of fiction; who say, "Oh, I've read that," in a tone which implies that they are not so far behind as that! "Have you no new novels?" they inquire. Novels get "old," one might suppose, like eggs, in a couple of days. The quest of these seekers of books suggests the story of the lady at a public library who, upon being told that seven new novels had come in that morning, said, "Give me, please, the one that came in last." There are, too, those singular folks who appear regularly every year just before Christmas, buy a great quantity of books for presents, and disappear again until the next year just before the holiday season. What, we have wondered, do they do about books the rest of the time? Ministers are always very trying characters to book clerks. "Beware of the gallery," says a fellow serf to us, "there's a minister browsing around up there." The official servants of the Lord fall, in the book clerk's mind, into that class technically described by him as "stickers." All gentlemen wearing high hats also belong to this classification. Deaf customers are embarrassing, for the reason that one always addresses one's next customer as though he were deaf, too. Foreigners are invariably very polite to clerks. They bow when they enter and take off their hats upon leaving. Very respectful people. "There," said a fellow thrall, "come two old women in at the door. Now, if I were my ancestor, I'd dance around that table with a stone club and brain them." As it is, they ask, "Have you Hopkinson Smith's 'Gondola Days'?" He says, "I think so." A lady, very rich and important looking, wants a book "without an unpleasant ending." "I wonder how this is" (looking at the last page). "No" (closing the book with a thump), "that won't do." A gentleman orders two sets of the Prayer Book and Hymnal, to be marked upon the cover with his name, the words Grace Church and his pew number. He informs us that every year while he is away in the summer his set of these books is stolen.

## Draft A

The bookseller's customer is not, as you might suppose, a person who wants a book. He is a great many other things first.

There is, to begin with, the reader who wants only what is new. Not new this season; new this morning. If a novel has been out a fortnight it has, for him, gone stale, like bread. He comes in with a certain eagerness, looks along the table, and asks, "Is this the latest?" If you say that it is, he takes it. If you say that there is another, he takes that. What is in either of them does not greatly concern him. He is buying the date.

Then there is the customer who comes once a year. You know him by his manner at the door, a little lost, a little defiant, as a man enters a church he does not attend. It is the holidays. He must have something for somebody. He does not know what, and he does not know for whom, precisely, and he would like you to know both.

There are the clergy. They are, as a class, excellent company. They stand at the shelves for a long time. They take down a volume and read in it, standing, with every appearance of enjoyment, and put it back, and take down another. And there is the gentleman in the silk hat. He is very well turned out indeed. He, too, lingers. He lingers a good deal. He examines, he considers, he raises the eyebrows over a price, and he goes away, with great dignity, having bought nothing whatever. One comes to feel that the silk hat and the not buying go together somehow.

And there are the requests. A lady would like "a story, you know, that ends happily." She is not particular about anything else. She will not tell you what she has read, nor what she liked; only that it must end happily, and that she would like to be quite sure of this before she pays. Someone else would like a church book, engraved; a prayer book with a name upon the cover in letters of gold. This, you learn, is quite as much a part of literature as anything else in the store.

It is all, of course, human nature. Only it is human nature buying books.

## Draft B

A person who stands behind the counter of a bookstore for any considerable length of time comes to know certain varieties of customer as a naturalist comes to know the birds of his neighbourhood. He recognises them at a distance. He can tell, almost before the door has closed behind them, what they will inquire for and what, in the end, they will carry away.

There is, first, the reader who wants only the newest novel. Not a new novel; the newest. A book which appeared last month is, in his estimation, already somewhat faded, and a book of last season is simply not to be discussed. He does not greatly care who wrote it or what it is about, provided it is still, figuratively, damp from the printing press. And there is the customer whom one sees once a year, in the weeks preceding the holidays, and never again. He enters with a list, a worried expression and an armful of packages from other establishments; he wants assistance, and he wants it immediately.

Then there are the clergy. They come in quietly, they take down a volume here and a volume there, they read a page, they replace it. They are very pleasant. They seldom purchase. There is also a certain species of gentleman in a top hat (it is invariably a top hat) who drifts in during the afternoon and remains for a long, long time among the shelves, examining the recent publications with a kind of dignified melancholy, and who departs, at last, with the same serene expression and nothing whatever under his arm. One does not quite know what he came for. Perhaps he does not know either.

And then there are the requests. A lady wishes a story with a happy ending; she is indifferent as to which story, so long as the conclusion is happy, and she wants to be assured of it beforehand. Another customer desires church books, engraved, and is not at all satisfied until the question of the engraving has been gone into thoroughly and exhaustively.

One learns to accommodate them all, or, as the case may be, to explain gently why one cannot.

## Draft C

Anyone who works behind the counter of a bookshop for long enough comes to know the customers by type before they have said a word, and some of the types are very odd indeed.

There are the readers who want only what is newest. They are not interested in a novel that came out last season, however good it may be, and they regard one from last year as practically an antique. They come in asking what has arrived this week, and if the answer is nothing, they look at you as though you had personally failed them. What the book is about matters less than the date on it. They would rather read a poor story that is fresh than a fine one that everyone has already discussed.

Then there are the people who appear once a year, at the holidays, and never otherwise. They come in with a list and a look of mild panic. They do not know where anything is kept, they do not know what anyone on the list would like, and they want it all settled quickly so that they can escape. For the rest of the year you forget they exist, and then they arrive again, exactly as bewildered as before.

A quieter kind of customer is the one who lingers without buying. Clergymen are especially given to this. They drift along the shelves, take down a volume, read a few pages with every sign of enjoyment, and put it back. Men in top hats do the same, standing for a long time in one spot as if the shop were a reading room kept for their convenience. They are perfectly polite. They simply never reach the counter.

And then there are the requests. One customer wanted a story, any story, provided it had a happy ending, and was willing to let me choose so long as I could guarantee that part. Another wanted church books with engraving on the covers, and was particular about the lettering in a way that suggested it mattered far more than the contents. [example needed]

None of them was unpleasant. But after a while you realise that a bookshop sells a great many things besides books, and that most of them are harder to find on the shelves.
