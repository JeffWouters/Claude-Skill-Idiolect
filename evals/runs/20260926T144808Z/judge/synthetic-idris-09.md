## The passage (by the author)

It may be that the most valuable thing a team can do is to make learning visible — to treat it as a legitimate activity that people can talk about openly rather than as a private deficiency to be remedied in secret. This means, among other things, allowing people to say that they do not understand something without that admission being counted against them; it means asking questions in shared channels rather than in private messages; it means senior engineers occasionally demonstrating their own uncertainty, so that others feel permitted to acknowledge theirs. The effect of such practices is considerable, though difficult to measure. A team in which learning happens in public tends to learn faster, since each person's confusion becomes an occasion for collective clarification, and the questions asked by one person often turn out to be the questions that several others were too embarrassed to raise.

Why, then, is this kind of openness so rare? Part of the answer, I suspect, lies in the way expertise functions as a form of status within technical organisations. To know something that others do not is to possess a kind of currency, and admitting ignorance can feel like spending that currency carelessly; the more senior one becomes, the higher the perceived cost of saying "I don't know". There is also, perhaps, a cultural inheritance from the way many of us were educated, in which not knowing the answer was associated with failure rather than with the beginning of inquiry. These attitudes are deeply ingrained and rarely examined, even by people who would disavow them if asked. They persist not because anyone defends them explicitly but because they are woven into countless small interactions, each of which reinforces the sense that competence must be performed even when it is not felt.

Perhaps what we need is a more generous understanding of what professional competence actually consists of. It is not the possession of a fixed body of knowledge, which will in any case become obsolete; it is, arguably, the capacity to keep learning, to tolerate the discomfort of not yet understanding, and to help others through that discomfort as well. Seen in this light, the moment of becoming a beginner again is not a threat to our expertise but an expression of it. The engineers I have most admired have rarely been those who knew the most; they have been those who remained curious, who asked the obvious questions without embarrassment, and who seemed to regard their own ignorance as an interesting place to begin rather than as something to be hidden.

## Draft A

In most engineering teams, there is a quiet cost to saying "I don't know". People pay it in small ways: a pause before asking a question in a meeting, a search through old messages rather than asking a colleague, a nod at a term they have never heard before. Each of those moments is minor. Added up across a team, they slow down how quickly everyone learns.

The alternative is to make not knowing something people can say openly. When one person admits a gap in public, a few things happen at once. They get an answer faster than they would have alone. Others who shared the same gap but did not want to say so get the answer too. And the person who explains it often discovers that their own understanding was less complete than they thought. A single honest question can move the whole group forward. [example needed]

So why is it so hard? Part of the answer lies in how knowledge works as status. In many technical teams, being the person who knows is a large part of how respect is earned. Seniority, credibility and influence all attach to expertise. Admitting a gap can feel like giving some of that away, particularly for people whose position depends on being seen as the expert. The more a team rewards knowing, the more it quietly punishes not knowing.

Another part of the answer goes back much further. Most people spent years in schooling where not knowing the answer meant losing marks. Questions were something the teacher asked and students answered, and the right answer was the one that counted. Those habits do not disappear at the office door. Many capable engineers still treat a question from a colleague as a test to pass rather than an invitation to think together.

Changing this starts with a different idea of what competence is. It is tempting to think of an expert as someone who has accumulated a fixed store of facts. In a field that changes as fast as engineering, that store goes out of date quickly. What actually marks a strong engineer is continuing curiosity: the habit of noticing what they do not understand, asking about it and following it until it makes sense. By that measure, the person who asks good questions in public is showing competence, not a lack of it.

Teams can support this deliberately. Senior people can model it by asking their own questions openly and admitting when something is new to them. Leads can thank people for raising gaps rather than moving past them. And teams can make space for learning together, so that finding out becomes a shared activity rather than a private struggle.

## Draft B

There is a small moment in the life of most engineering teams that reveals a great deal about their culture: the moment when someone is asked a question and does not know the answer. What happens next — whether they say so plainly, or deflect, or offer a confident guess and hope for the best — tells you more about how the team learns than any stated policy on training or growth. In teams where it is acceptable to say "I don't know", the admission tends to open a conversation, and the gap is filled quickly, often by someone who had been quietly wondering the same thing. Admitting a gap publicly, in other words, does not only help the person who admits it. It helps everyone who shared the gap without saying so, and it makes the team as a whole learn faster than it otherwise would.

Why, then, is the admission so difficult? Part of the answer lies in the way knowledge functions as a form of status in technical work. The engineer who knows the obscure corner of the system, who can answer the question others cannot, gains a kind of standing that is not easily surrendered, and saying "I don't know" can feel like giving some of it away. Another part lies further back, in habits formed long before anyone joined a team. Schooling, for most of us, rewarded the right answer and penalised its absence; the student who admitted ignorance was rarely praised for honesty. It would be surprising if those lessons simply vanished on entering professional life.

Changing this requires, I think, a different understanding of what competence actually is. We tend to picture it as a store of facts, accumulated over years and drawn upon when needed, so that a competent engineer is one whose store is large. Yet the store is always incomplete and always ageing, and much of what it contains will be obsolete within a few years. What distinguishes the genuinely capable engineer is not the size of the store but the disposition to keep adding to it — a continuing curiosity, a readiness to notice what one does not understand and to go and find out. Seen this way, the admission of ignorance is not a failure of competence but one of its clearest expressions.

A team that wishes to make learning open might therefore begin by examining how it responds to the words "I don't know", and particularly how its most senior members respond when they say them. ## Draft C

What would it take for an engineering team to treat learning as something done openly, in front of one another, rather than privately and slightly out of sight? The question sounds simple, and most teams would claim to value learning already. It may be that the claim and the practice have drifted apart, since in many teams the admission that you do not know something still carries a small social cost that everyone has learned to avoid.

The benefits of admitting a gap in public are easy to describe. When one person says that they do not understand how a part of the system works, others who were equally uncertain but silent can learn alongside them; the person who does understand is prompted to explain it, and may discover in explaining that their own understanding had gaps; the team as a whole gains a clearer picture of where its knowledge is thin. Is it any wonder that teams in which this happens tend to learn faster than those in which it does not?

There is also the matter of why it happens so rarely. In many workplaces knowledge functions as a form of status, and to reveal what you do not know is to give away a little of that status in front of the people whose regard you most want. I suspect the habit goes deeper than any particular workplace, reaching back to schooling in which not knowing the answer was something to be concealed from the teacher, and in which the reward went to whoever could supply the right response most quickly. Few of us, arguably, have entirely unlearned that lesson, even after many years of professional life.

It seems to me that the most useful change is to reconsider what we mean by competence in the first place. We tend to picture it as a store of facts, accumulated over a career and drawn upon when needed, and this picture makes every gap look like a deficiency. A more accurate picture, perhaps, is that competence is a continuing curiosity, a willingness to keep noticing what one does not yet understand and to do something about it; seen this way, the engineer who asks the most questions may well be the most competent person in the room. Leaders can help simply by asking their own questions aloud, and by responding to the questions of others with interest rather than surprise.

The daily work of a team is, in the end, one of the few settings in working life in which learning and doing happen side by side. Perhaps the measure of such a team is not how much it knows, but how comfortably it can say what it does not.
