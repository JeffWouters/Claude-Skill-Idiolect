# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-04

## The passage (by the author)

Simplicity is an acquired taste. Mankind, left free, instinctively complicates life. The hardest command to follow has always been that which bids us take no thought for the morrow. Perhaps that is what Mr. Chesterton means when he talks of the difficulty of eating grape-nuts on principle. The real drawback to “the simple life” is that it is not simple. If you are living it, you positively can do nothing else. There is not time. For the simple life demands virtually that there shall be no specialization. The Hausfrau who is living the simple life must, after all, sweep, scour, wash, and mend. She must also cook; from that, even Battle Creek cannot save her. She may dream sternly of Margaret Fuller, who read Plato while she pared apples; but in her secret heart she knows that either Plato or the apples suffered. And from what point of view is it simpler to have a maid-of-all-work than to indulge one’s self in liveried lackeys? Not, obviously, for the mistress; and it is surely simpler to be an adequate second footman than to be an adequate bonne-à-tout-faire. We should really simplify life by having more servants rather than fewer; more luxury instead of less. The smoothest machinery is the most complicated; and which of us wants to sink the Mauretania and go back to Robert Fulton’s steamboat? One would think that the decision would be made naturally for one by one’s income. But it is the triumph of the new paradox that this is not so. Thousands of people seem to be infected with the idea that by doing more themselves they bestow leisure on others; that by wearing shabby clothes they somehow make it possible for others to dress better--though they thus admit tacitly that leisure and elegance are not evil things. Or perhaps--though Heaven forbid they should be right!--they merely think that by refusing nightingales’ tongues, they make every one more content with porridge. Let us be gallant about the porridge that we must eat, but let us never forget that there are better things to eat than porridge.

## Draft A

We hear a great deal, nowadays, about the simple life. It is recommended to us as a cure for nerves, for extravagance, and for most of the other ills of modern civilisation; and those who recommend it always speak as though it were the easy way, the way of least resistance, which only our foolish habits prevent us from taking. I have never been able to see it so. The simple life, as far as I can make out, is the most complicated and exhausting life there is.

Consider what it means in practice. If you are to do every chore yourself--to cook, and scrub, and mend, and carry the coal--you will very soon find that you have no room for anything else. The day is filled from end to end with small necessary tasks, each of which somebody else could have done better and more quickly. That is not simplicity; it is merely inefficiency, dignified by a pleasant name. The division of labour was invented precisely because nobody can do everything well. A household that employs specialised help, and that permits itself a reasonable degree of comfort, is not making life harder for itself. It is making life possible--possible, that is, for the reading and the friendship and the leisurely thought which are the real business of a civilised existence.

Some one will object that the point of the thing is moral rather than practical: that by wearing shabby clothes and refusing luxuries we show our solidarity with those who cannot afford them. Granted that the motive is admirable; but whom, exactly, does it help? The poor are not one whit richer because I wear a threadbare coat. The shopkeeper who might have sold me a new one is, if anything, a little poorer. My renunciation feeds nobody and clothes nobody; it merely makes me uncomfortable, and makes me feel virtuous about being uncomfortable. I do not think that is a very good bargain. Comfort, sensibly used, sets us free for better things.

## Draft B

We hear a great deal, nowadays, about the simple life. It is recommended to us from the pulpit and the platform and the women's page; and it is always recommended as though it were easy. I wish, very much, that the people who recommend it would try it for a month. They would discover, I think, that there is nothing on earth so complicated.

For the simple life, as it is commonly preached, means doing everything oneself. One cooks, and one washes up; one sweeps, and one mends; one answers the door and the telephone and the tradesmen. Each of these things is simple enough, taken singly. It is when they are taken together that they become impossible. The woman who does them all has no time left for anything else--not for reading, not for thinking, not for her friends, and very little, I suspect, for her husband and her children. She has simplified her life, if you like, by reducing it to a single occupation; but it is not an occupation that she would have chosen, and it is not one that leaves her any the richer.

Now the plain truth is that specialised help makes living easier, not harder. The cook who knows her business will cook better, and faster, than the amateur; the laundress will do the laundry better; and between them they set free a great many hours for work that no one else can do. More comfort, too--better lamps, warmer rooms, softer chairs--does not complicate existence; it smooths it. I cannot see why we should be ashamed of wanting existence smoothed.

But the simple-lifers have another argument, and it is the one they are proudest of. They wear shabby clothes, and they refuse luxuries, because other people cannot have them. Let us grant, by all means, that it is a generous impulse. It is also a perfectly useless one. My shabby coat does not warm anybody else; my going without a thing does not put that thing into anyone's hands. [example needed] The only result is that there is one more uncomfortable person in the world--and the world, heaven knows, has enough of those already.

## Draft C

We are often urged to adopt the simple life, as though simplicity were a kind of rest. It is nothing of the sort. The so-called simple life is harder than the ordinary one, and a good deal less efficient.

Consider what it asks. If we are to do every chore ourselves, cooking, cleaning, mending, fetching and carrying, then those chores will take up the day, because that is what chores do when there is only one pair of hands to meet them. The person who lives simply in this sense does not have a quieter life; he has a narrower one. There is no room left for reading, for work that needs a clear head, for friends, or for idleness, which has its own uses. Everything else must wait until the dishes are done, and the dishes are never quite done.

The alternative is not decadence. It is the ordinary principle on which every other part of life already runs: that people who do one thing well should do it, and that the rest of us should be glad of them. A good cook, a capable housekeeper, a man who understands the furnace, these are specialists, and their help makes living easier rather than harder. So does a measure of plain comfort, a warm room, a decent chair, good light to read by. Such things do not soften us. They free us to spend our energy on what we choose rather than on the endless small labours of keeping going. [example needed]

The advocates of simplicity often reply that there is a moral point to it, that by wearing shabby things or refusing luxuries we stand closer to those who have less. But it is hard to see whom this helps. The worn coat on my back does not warm anyone else. The dinner I decline to eat is not sent to a hungrier table. If anything, the refusal takes work away from those who would have been paid to provide what I went without. Self-denial of this kind is a private exercise with a public face, and it should not be mistaken for charity.

Simplicity, properly understood, would mean making life easier. The simple life, as preached, makes it harder, and calls the difficulty a virtue.
