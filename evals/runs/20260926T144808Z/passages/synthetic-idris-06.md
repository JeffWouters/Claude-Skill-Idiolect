What is a meeting, really, once it has been placed on the calendar and allowed to recur indefinitely? It may be that the recurring meeting is the closest thing most organisations have to a ritual, in the anthropological sense of the word — a gathering whose original purpose has quietly dissolved while its form persists, maintained by a vague institutional reluctance to be the person who suggests cancelling it. We arrive, we exchange the customary pleasantries, and we wait for someone to articulate why we are there; perhaps someone does, and perhaps the explanation is convincing, but more often the conversation drifts toward whatever happens to be most visible that week. I suspect that very few of us would defend the meeting if asked to justify it from first principles, and yet we continue to attend with considerable loyalty. There is something in this that deserves more attention than the usual complaints about wasted time, because the persistence of the ritual tells us something about what teams actually need from one another.

Consider the way a meeting begins its life. Usually it is created in response to a genuine difficulty — a project that has lost its sense of direction, a dependency between teams that nobody quite owns, a decision that keeps being deferred — and in those first weeks it is arguably the most valuable hour anyone spends. The people in the room have a shared problem; they bring information that cannot easily be written down; they leave with a clearer sense of who is doing what. The difficulty is that the problem eventually resolves, or changes shape, while the invitation remains exactly as it was, reproducing itself week after week with the indifference of any automated process. Does anyone notice the moment at which the meeting stops serving its purpose? In my experience the transition is almost always invisible, since the attendees are still talking, the notes are still being taken, and the calendar still insists that this is where we ought to be.
