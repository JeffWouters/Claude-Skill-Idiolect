It would be easy to conclude from this that recurring meetings should simply be abolished, and there are teams that have tried precisely that experiment with a certain evangelical enthusiasm. I am not convinced that the conclusion follows. What the meeting provides, even in its degraded form, is a guaranteed occasion for contact — a moment when you can raise something half-formed without having to justify interrupting anyone, when the quieter members of a team can be seen and heard, when small frictions surface before they harden into grievances. Perhaps the ritual persists precisely because it performs this function, however inefficiently, and because nobody has designed a better substitute. In some sense the meeting is less a tool for making decisions than a piece of social infrastructure; its value is not visible in any particular session but in the accumulated familiarity it produces over months. Remove it without replacing that function, and we may discover that the team has become efficient in a way that is also, subtly, lonelier.

What, then, might we do differently? One possibility is to treat each recurring meeting as something that must periodically earn its renewal, rather than as a permanent fixture whose continuation requires no argument. A team might pause every so often and ask, with genuine openness, whether the original purpose still holds, whether a different purpose has replaced it, and whether that new purpose is worth the collective attention it consumes. The conversation is often uncomfortable, since it touches on questions of status and inclusion that are rarely discussed openly; the person who organised the meeting may feel that their judgement is being questioned, and the people who value it most may be the least inclined to say so. It is nevertheless the kind of discomfort that tends to be productive, because it forces the team to articulate what it actually wants from its time together. I suspect that many meetings would survive such scrutiny in a modified form — shorter, less frequent, or reframed around a clearer question — and that a few would be quietly and gratefully retired.
