# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-09

## The passage (by the author)

For one’s self alone, it is a question whether any lie could bring such luxury as that of telling the simple truth. To lie to save one’s self is the mark of the beast; to lie to save another person may make one distrust the cosmos, but at least it is a purer fault. For it seems to be agreed on by all codes that the unselfish motive is a mightily purging element. On the whole, I should say that the person who likes to lie should never, in any circumstances, be allowed to. Leave the lying to the people who hate it. You will not find them indulging often.

Perhaps the greatest conflict for Puritan youth has always come when it faced for the first time the unfamiliar shape of Honor. Honor and John Calvin have fought on many a strange battlefield for the young soul, and the young soul must often have wondered which was friend and which was foe.

Honour and wit, foredamned they sit,

sings Kipling in an atavistic moment. Which of us has not at some time or other shudderingly understood him? And yet it is only the fortuitous trappings of Honor which can so disturb. For the truest thing about Honor is that, like Charity, it “seeks not itself”; and Honor in the mediæval sense was the darling child of the Church. Honor does not break its word; it protects the weak against itself, and against others; it keeps its engagements. It is more immediately concerned with its duty to humanity than with its duty to God; which is doubtless why the Puritan mystic saw it as a foe. The code of honor is the etiquette-book of the Christian; and the people who have attacked it are the people who have considered that Christians needed no etiquette. By our ancestors who were bred in the cold and windy times of the Reformation it was held to deal chiefly with duelling, gaming, and illicit affairs. “The debt of honor,” “the affair of honor”--what do even these corrupted phrases mean except that the gentleman has found more ways to bind himself than the laws of the land afford? I do not know that Honor ever compelled a man to gamble or to provoke a quarrel; but if he has gambled or if he has quarrelled--if he has undertaken to play the lamentable game--he must not skulk behind a policeman, like a cry-baby or a sans-culotte, because things have not gone his way. If he has broken, he must pay.

## Draft A

Most of us lie now and then, and most of us have an unexamined sense of which lies are acceptable. It is worth examining, because the line we draw says a good deal about what we think honour requires.

The first distinction is between lies told for others and lies told for oneself. A person who lies to shield a friend, to spare someone pain, or to keep a confidence entrusted to him has done something that may still be wrong, but it is wrong in a generous direction. He has put another person's interest ahead of his own comfort, and even ahead of his own record for truthfulness. The self-serving lie is different in kind. The man who lies to escape blame, to gain an advantage, or to make himself look better has used the truth as a thing to be spent on his own behalf. We are right to judge him more harshly. The first sort of liar may be mistaken; the second is merely selfish.

This way of thinking has not always been welcome. Puritan religion, which shaped so much of the country's moral vocabulary, long regarded honour with suspicion, and at times as an outright enemy. Honour seemed to it a worldly standard, a code of pride and reputation that competed with the demands of conscience and of God. It preferred rules, and it wanted those rules to be the same for everyone. Anything that looked like a private code, especially one that might sometimes excuse a lie, was suspect on principle.

But honour is not the loose permission its critics feared. Properly understood, it is a stricter standard than the law, not a looser one. The law tells us what we may be compelled to do. Honour is what we bind ourselves to do when no one could compel us. A man of honour keeps promises that no court would enforce, meets obligations that no contract records, and holds himself to account in matters where he could easily escape account. Its simplest test is the payment of debts. The honourable man pays what he owes, including the debts that could never be collected from him, and he does not look for technical reasons to avoid paying.

Seen this way, the question of the permissible lie becomes clearer. Honour does not forbid every untruth, but it forbids using untruth to escape what one owes. A lie that protects another may be consistent with it. A lie that saves one from a debt, whether of money or of loyalty, never is.

## Draft B

Most of us will admit, I think, that there are lies and lies. The moralist who holds that every untruth is equally black has never, I suspect, had to protect anybody. There is all the difference in the world between the lie a man tells to save his own skin and the lie he tells to save some one else's. The first is a meanness; the second may be very nearly a virtue. I do not say it is a virtue. I say that it is forgivable, and that the other is not--or not easily.

The distinction seems to me plain enough. Yet it is a distinction our fathers were oddly unwilling to make; and I think I know why. The trouble goes back to the Puritans, who had a great deal to say about sin and very little to say about honour. They distrusted honour--and not without reason, from their point of view. Honour was a worldly thing, a Cavalier thing; it had to do with swords and pride and the opinion of one's equals, and it set up a standard of conduct that did not come out of the Book. To the Puritan mind it was not merely beside the point. It was an enemy. A man who looked to his honour was looking away from his soul.

We have inherited a good deal of that suspicion without inheriting the faith that justified it. And the result, frankly, is that a great many of us do not know what honour is. We think it means obeying the law, or not being found out. It means nothing of the kind.

Honour, as I understand it, begins exactly where the law leaves off. The law tells you what you must do; honour tells you what you have bound yourself to do, though no court on earth would hold you to it. It is the promise kept when breaking it would cost nothing. It is the word given to a man who is dead, or to a man who will never know. It is--above all, and most simply--the paying of one's debts: the debts one owes in money, and the debts one owes in kind, and the debts that were never written down at all.

A man of honour may, on occasion, tell a lie; he may tell it to shield a woman, or a friend, or a secret that is not his. But he will not tell it to escape a debt. That is the line, and I think it is the right one.

## Draft C

When is a lie permissible? The moralists have argued it for centuries, and I have no hope of settling it in a page. But I think one distinction may be drawn with some confidence. A lie told for the sake of another--to shield a friend, to spare a sick man, to keep a confidence--is a different thing from a lie told to save one's own skin or fatten one's own purse. The first may be wrong; the second is certainly base. Most people feel this without being taught it. It is odd how little of our formal ethics has troubled to say so.

The reason, I suspect, lies partly in our religious inheritance. The Puritan mind--and a good deal of American morality is Puritan still, whether it knows it or not--long looked upon honour as an enemy. Honour was worldly; it smelt of swords and of the aristocracy; it set a man's opinion of himself above his duty to God. [example needed: a Puritan authority condemning honour] So honour was put away, and conscience was put in its place, and conscience, being a private affair between a man and his Maker, has never been much concerned with the lies he tells to other men.

Yet honour is not the swaggering thing the Puritans feared. It is something far more exacting. The law tells a man what he must not do; honour tells him what he must do though no law obliges him. It is a bond he lays upon himself--a promise, not to a court, but to his own conception of what a gentleman owes. Some one will say that this is vanity dressed up as virtue. Granted: there is vanity in it. But it is a vanity that pays its debts, and that is more than can be said for a good many consciences.

For that, when all is said, is the test. The man of honour pays what he owes: his bills, his promises, his gratitude, his apologies. He pays them when nobody could compel him to, and when nobody would know if he didn't. The point is that a lie told for another may be consistent with honour; a debt left unpaid never is. The one is a fault. The other is a disgrace.
