## The passage (by the author)

There is also a subtler distortion at work. Status updates tend to describe activity rather than understanding; they report what has been done, which is easy to enumerate, rather than what has been learned, which is often more important but harder to articulate. A team may complete a great many tasks while its grasp of the underlying problem remains as uncertain as it was at the outset, and the update will faithfully record the tasks while saying almost nothing about the uncertainty. Conversely, a team may spend a period apparently producing little, while in fact resolving a fundamental question that will make everything subsequent far easier; the update, which has no category for such progress, will make that period look like a failure. You might say that the format rewards visible motion over invisible insight, and in doing so it shapes, perhaps more than we realise, what teams choose to work on.

What might a more truthful kind of update look like? It seems to me that it would make room for confidence as well as progress — for the reporter to say, in effect, how sure they are about what they are reporting, and what would change their mind. It would distinguish between the parts of the work that are well understood and the parts that remain obscure; it would treat an emerging concern as legitimate information rather than as an alarm that must be justified before it can be raised. Such updates are harder to write and harder to read, since they resist the comforting simplicity of a single colour. They also, I suspect, give the people who receive them a far more accurate sense of where things stand, which is presumably what the update was meant to provide in the first place.

Perhaps the difficulty of reporting status honestly is a symptom of something larger: the discomfort that institutions feel with uncertainty, and the corresponding pressure on individuals to present their work as more settled than it is. The update becomes a small performance of control, repeated at regular intervals, in which everyone understands that the performance is partly fictional but nobody wishes to be the first to abandon it. To change this is not a matter of redesigning a template but of cultivating a culture in which saying "I am not yet sure" is regarded as a sign of honesty rather than of weakness. We might discover, if we tried, that the most useful thing a status update can communicate is not how far we have come, but how clearly we can see the road ahead.

## Draft A

The progress report occupies an odd position in organisational life. It is written regularly, read occasionally and believed, I suspect, rather less often than anyone would care to admit. Its purpose is to tell others what a team knows about the state of its work, and yet the form it usually takes is remarkably poor at conveying exactly that. Most reports are, in essence, lists of completed tasks: this was finished, that was merged, the other was deployed. Such lists have the virtue of being easy to verify, but they say very little about what the team has actually learned, which is often the more important part of any week's work.

The consequences of this are not merely cosmetic. When reports reward the completion of tasks, teams come to prioritise the kind of work that can be completed and reported, and to neglect the kind that yields understanding without producing anything that can be ticked off. A week spent discovering that an approach will not work, or that a requirement was misunderstood, may be the most valuable week of the project, and yet in the report it looks like a week in which nothing happened. Over time, the format quietly shapes what people choose to do, pulling them towards visible activity and away from the slower business of finding out what is true.

What might a more honest report look like? It seems to me that it would say not only what has been done but how confident the team is in its current picture, and what questions remain open. It might distinguish between the parts of the work that are well understood and those that are still largely guesswork; it might name the assumptions that, if wrong, would change the plan; it might admit, plainly, that some things are not yet known. None of this is especially difficult to write. The difficulty lies in whether anyone is prepared to read it without alarm.

And that, I think, is where the real obstacle sits. Reports that express doubt are only useful in organisations that are willing to receive doubt as information rather than as a sign of weakness. Where every admission of uncertainty is met with escalation or disappointment, teams learn very quickly to present a picture of steady control, whatever the underlying reality. The report then becomes a small performance, staged for an audience that would rather be reassured than informed. Perhaps the most useful change an organisation could make is not to redesign the template at all but to make it safe to say, in writing, that we are not yet sure. The fake control would then have less work to do, and the reports might begin to tell us something.

## Draft B

Progress reports are meant to tell people where a piece of work stands. Most of them do something narrower. They list what has been completed: tickets closed, features shipped, milestones passed. That is useful information, but it is not the same as what the team actually knows. And the gap between the two shapes decisions more than most organisations realise.

Consider what a team learns in a typical stretch of work. It discovers that an assumption was wrong, that a dependency is shakier than expected, that the problem customers described is not quite the problem they have. These insights are often the most valuable output of the period. They change what should happen next. Yet they rarely appear in the report, because there is no line for them. A report built around completed tasks rewards completing tasks, so teams prioritise the work that produces visible items over the work that produces understanding. Exploration, investigation and the careful testing of ideas all look like a week with nothing to show.

The result is that leaders make decisions based on a picture that is tidy but incomplete. They see steady progress and assume the path ahead is clear. Meanwhile the team may be carrying real doubts that never made it onto the page. [example needed]

A better report would say more about knowledge and less about activity. It would state how confident the team is in its current plan, and why. It would list the open questions that still matter: the things nobody knows yet, and what it would take to find out. It might say which assumptions have been tested and which are still guesses. None of this needs to be long. A few honest lines about confidence and uncertainty would tell a reader more than a page of completed items.

This is where the real difficulty lies. Reports hide what teams know partly because the format encourages it, but mostly because the organisation around them rewards the appearance of control. A report that says "we are not sure yet" can feel like an admission of weakness. Teams learn quickly that confident, complete-looking updates are received better than candid ones, and they write accordingly.

Changing the format alone will not fix that. Organisations have to make it genuinely safe to admit doubt. That means responding to an honest statement of uncertainty with interest rather than alarm, and treating open questions as useful information rather than as signs that someone has lost the thread. When doubt is welcomed, it gets reported early, while there is still time to act on it. When it is punished, it stays hidden until it turns into a surprise, and false control is replaced by the real thing only when it is already too late.

## Draft C

What does a progress report actually tell the people who read it? It may be that it tells them a great deal about what a team has done and remarkably little about what the team has come to understand, which is arguably the more important of the two. The usual report is a list of completed tasks, tickets closed and milestones reached, and its format quietly assumes that progress is the same thing as activity.

The difficulty is that much of the most valuable work a team does leaves no trace in such a list. A week spent discovering that an approach will not work, or that a requirement was misunderstood, or that a dependency is far less reliable than everyone believed, may be the week in which the project became considerably more likely to succeed; in the report it appears as a week in which little was delivered. You might reasonably expect people to see through this, and some do, but a format that records only completion will over time teach a team to prefer the kind of work that can be completed and reported, and to postpone the kind that merely reduces uncertainty.

There is also the matter of what a better report would contain. It would, I suspect, say less about tasks and more about knowledge: how confident the team is in its current plan, which questions remain open, and what it has learned since the last report that changes its view of the work ahead. None of this is especially elaborate, and most teams could write it in the time they currently spend polishing lists of finished items. The difference is that it would give readers some sense of where the real risks lie, rather than an impression of steady movement towards a destination whose position is still, in some sense, unknown.

It seems to me, however, that the format is the easier part of the problem. A report that states its doubts plainly will only survive in an organisation that is willing to receive doubt without treating it as a failure, and many organisations, perhaps without intending to, reward the appearance of control over the admission of uncertainty. Until that changes, teams will go on writing reports that describe what they have done rather than what they know, because the first is safe and the second is not. Perhaps the most honest report is not the one that shows the most progress but the one that shows most clearly how much remains uncertain.
