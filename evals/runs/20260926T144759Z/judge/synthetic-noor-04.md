## The passage (by the author)

There is a hiss. Air coming out. It smells of metal and old water. The hiss can go on for a while. On the back bedroom one it went on for almost a minute.

And then the sound changes. The hiss goes wet. A spit. Then a bead of water. That is the moment. Turn the key back, clockwise, snug. Not tight. You can strip the little valve if you force it.

Wipe the water. Check the cloth. Move on.

Eight of the nine had some air. The one in the hall had none. It never does. I do not know why. It is the lowest one in the house and the closest to the boiler. Something to do with that.

When they are all done, I go to the boiler. There is a pressure gauge on the front. A round dial with a needle. It should sit between 1 and 1.5 bar when cold. After bleeding it drops, because you let water out. This year it was down to 0.6.

So I top it up. Under the boiler there is a short silver hose with two taps. The filling loop. I open both taps, slowly, and watch the needle climb. At 1.2 I close them. The pipes groan while the new water finds its way round.

Then the heating goes back on.

I walk round the house and feel each radiator again. Top to bottom. Hot all over. The back bedroom one too. It is the best part of the job. You put your hand flat on the metal and the heat is even from edge to edge.

That night the house was quiet. No knocks. No tick. Just the low hum of the pump in the cupboard and the click as the thermostat turned it off.

I will do the same next October. The air comes back. It always does. Small bubbles, a little at a time, over the year.

## Draft A

I bled the radiators today. It is a small job. It is also one that makes the whole house better.

Here is the order I do it in.

First the heating goes off, so the water is still. Then a radiator key, and a cloth. The key fits the square valve at the top corner of each radiator. I hold the cloth under it and turn the key slowly. Not far. A quarter turn is plenty.

Then you wait. The air comes out first with a hiss. It can take longer than you think. Then a spit of water, then a steady bead.

That is the moment to stop. Close the valve gently. Not hard. The valve is small and made of soft brass. If you force it, you can strip it or crack the seat. Then you have a leak where you had a cold spot. So I turn it until it stops, and no further.

Then the next radiator. And the next.

After that, the boiler. Every drop that came out of the valves came out of the system. So the pressure has dropped. The gauge on the front of the boiler shows it. Mine was down to [reading needed] bar.

So I refill it through the filling loop. That is the short pipe under the boiler with a tap at each end. Open one, then the other. Water runs in from the mains. Watch the needle. When it reaches about 1.2 bar, close both taps. Not more. That is the level I aim for.

Then the heating goes back on.

The difference shows within the hour. The heat spreads evenly now. Top to bottom, end to end. No cold band along the top of each radiator.

It will not last. Air gets back into the system over the year. It always does. So next autumn I will do it all again.

Tonight the radiators are warm all over. The key is back in the drawer.

## Draft B

If a radiator is warm at the bottom and cold at the top, there is almost certainly air trapped inside it. Bleeding it out is one of the simplest jobs in home maintenance, but there are two moments where it pays to slow down: closing the valve, and topping the pressure back up afterwards.

With the heating off and the radiator key fitted to the bleed valve, open the valve a little. You will hear air hissing out. Keep a cloth ready underneath, because the hiss is followed by water. The moment water appears, the air is out, and that is your signal to close the valve. Do it gently. The bleed valve is a small part, and it is surprisingly easy to overtighten it and damage the thread or the seat. Snug is enough. Wipe away any drips and move on to the next radiator.

Every bit of air you release is replaced by water from the system, and on a sealed system that means the pressure drops. Once all the radiators are done, check the pressure gauge on the boiler. If it has fallen, you need to refill the system through the filling loop. Open the loop slowly and watch the gauge rise. You are aiming for roughly 1.2 bar. Close the loop when you reach it, and check that both valves on the loop are properly shut so the pressure does not keep creeping up. Your boiler's manual is the final word here [example needed: where to find the recommended pressure for a given model].

Then turn the heating back on and give it some time. The difference is usually obvious: the radiators heat evenly from top to bottom, and rooms that felt cool in one corner feel the same all over. The gurgling noises tend to stop as well.

Do not expect it to last forever. Air finds its way back into the system gradually, and in my experience it has built up again after about a year. So this is not a one-time repair but a yearly routine, best done before the cold weather really sets in.

## Draft C

Put a cloth under the valve first. An old towel is fine. Then fit the key to the square pin at the top corner of the radiator and turn it slowly. A quarter turn. No more. You will hear a hiss. That is the air. Let it go.

Keep the cloth ready. The hiss will stop and there will be a spit. Then water. A little dribble at first, then a steady bead. That is the moment. Close the valve. Gently. Do not force it. The pin is small and it is usually brass, and brass is soft. Crank it shut and you can shear it, or crush the seat, and then it will weep for ever. Snug is enough. Wipe it dry, and check it again after a minute. If it is still dry, move on.

Do every radiator that needs it. Then go and look at the boiler.

Letting air out lets pressure out too. The gauge on the boiler will have dropped. Mine always does. So now you top it up. That is what the filling loop is for. It is the flexible hose under the boiler with a valve at each end. [description of loop depends on boiler model] Open one valve. Then open the other, slowly, and watch the needle on the gauge. It climbs. Stop at about 1.2 bar. Close both valves. Check the needle again. It should sit still.

Then turn the heating back on and wait.

The difference is easy to feel. Before, the radiators were hot at the bottom and cold at the top. After, the heat runs right across them. Top to bottom, end to end. The rooms warm up evenly. No more cold corner by the window.

It does not last. The air comes back. It comes back every year. I do not know exactly where it comes from, and it does not matter. So I do this every year. Same key, same cloth, same gauge. It is not a big job. It is just one that has to be done again.
