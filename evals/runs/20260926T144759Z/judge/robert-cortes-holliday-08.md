# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-08

## The passage (by the author)

Some people say that it is the most awful trial.

But it isn't so at all.

One of the most entertaining things that can be done in the world, so full of interesting things, is to go hunting lodgings. Also, it is one of the most enlightening things that can be done, for, pursued with intelligence and energy, it gives one an excellent view of humankind; that is, of a particularly human kind of humankind. It is a confoundly Christian thing to do--hunting lodgings--because it opens the heart to the queer ways, and speech, and customs of the world.

Now, I myself hunt lodgings as some men hunt wild game.

Nothing is better when one is out of sorts, somewhat run down, and peevish with the world generally than to go out one fine afternoon and hunt lodgings In some remote part of town.

When in a foreign city, especially, the first thing I myself do, as soon as I am comfortably settled somewhere--and after, of course, having looked up the celebrated sights of the place, the Abbey, the Louvre, Grant's Tomb---is to put in a day or so hunting lodgings.

Even to read in the papers of lodgings to let is refreshing and educational. All lodgings are "sunny"--in the papers. They are let mainly by "refined" persons, and are wonderfully "quiet." I remember last summer in London there was "a small sitting to let to a young lady." Lodgings, by the way, are usually "apartments" in England, as you know. Though, indeed, it is true that when a gentleman rents over there what we call a "furnished room" he is commonly said to "go into lodgings." A fine phrase, that; it is like to that fine old expression "commencing author." And that reminds me: the most fascinating lodgings to hunt, perhaps, anywhere, are called "chambers." These which I mean are in the old Inns of Court in London. And the most charming of these remaining is Staple Inn, off Holborn. I used frequently to hunt chambers in "the fayrest Inne of Chancerie." There are no "modern conveniences" there. You draw your own water at a pump in the venerable quadrangle, and you "find" your own light. But to return:

## Draft A

I do not at all agree with those who say that looking for rooms is a hardship. I think it one of the pleasantest things there is. There is no better way that I know of to see human nature. You go in at one door after another, and behind each door there is somebody, and each somebody is different from the last. You see how people live. You see what they think important, and what they do not. You come away, I think, a broader person than you went in; more tolerant, more sympathetic, more inclined to believe that there is a good deal to be said for everybody.

Even the advertisements are entertaining. I have read a great many of them. Every room in them is "large." Every room is "light" and "airy." Every house is "select," and every neighbourhood is "refined." Every landlady keeps "a private family." Every one of them offers "all home comforts." The remarkable thing is that there appear to be, in the whole of the city, no rooms that are small, or dark, or stuffy, and no house that is not select. It is a very comforting picture. I have never been able quite to reconcile it with the rooms. But that, I suppose, is the fault not of the rooms but of me.

The most charming rooms of all to look at, though, are the old chambers of the lawyers in London. I speak of the Inns. You go in out of the noise of the street through an archway, and at once it is quiet. There are old trees, and old grass, and old stone, and old brick gone soft in colour with the smoke of many years. There are staircases worn hollow in the middle. There are names painted up beside the doors, a whole column of them, most of them, I dare say, of men long gone. The rooms themselves are panelled, and rather dark, and have deep windows, and very likely a fireplace that smokes. They are not convenient. Nobody has ever said that they are convenient. But there is about them a kind of mellowness, of settled and dignified age, that I have met with nowhere else. One would not need, I think, to take them. It is enough to have looked at them.

## Draft B

Most people speak of looking for rooms as a chore, something to be got through as quickly as possible and forgotten. I have never been able to see it that way. To me it is one of the pleasures of life, and I am always a little sorry when I have found what I wanted and the search is over.

The reason is simple. There is no better way of seeing human nature at close quarters. In the course of a few afternoons one is let into a dozen houses, shown how other people live, and talked to by landladies, caretakers and departing tenants who would never speak so freely in any other circumstances. Each of them has a story, or at least a manner, and each of them adds something to one's sense of how various people are. I think it broadens one's sympathy. It is hard to go on judging people by a single standard after seeing how differently, and how reasonably, they arrange their lives.

Even the advertisements are a pleasure, if one reads them in the right spirit. They are written with a uniform enthusiasm that is almost touching. Every room is bright, every house is quiet, every neighbourhood is convenient, and every landlady is kind. [Example advertisement wording needed.] Read one by one, they mislead; read together, they have a cheerful sameness that amounts almost to a literary form, and I have spent many contented minutes over a column of them without any intention of answering a single one.

But of all the places I have gone to inspect, the most charming are the old legal chambers in London. They have a character nothing else quite matches. The staircases are old and worn, the rooms are panelled and a little dark, and there is a stillness about the courts outside them that seems to belong to another century. One feels, going up the stairs, that generations of lawyers have climbed them before, and that the rooms have outlasted a great many tenants and will outlast a great many more. The pleasure is in seeing them, and in imagining for half an hour what it would be like to live there.

That, finally, is the whole secret of enjoying the search. One must go to look, not only to find.

## Draft C

I have never been able to understand why looking for rooms should be regarded as a hardship. People speak of it as they speak of going to the dentist, or of making out an income-tax return: a thing to be got through with clenched teeth and forgotten as soon as possible. To me it has always been one of the pleasantest of occupations, and I have sometimes been tempted, when I had no need of rooms whatever, to go and look at a few, merely for the entertainment of it.

For consider what it offers. You are admitted, on the most respectable of pretexts, into the private arrangements of a great many of your fellow creatures. You see how they live, what they value, what they put up with and what they will not put up with; you observe their furniture, their pictures, their notions of comfort and their notions of a bargain. It is an education in human nature such as no library can furnish. And it is, I think, an education in charity as well. One comes away from a morning of it with a considerably enlarged sympathy for the odd, the obstinate, and the merely unfortunate, among whom, it may be, one has occasionally caught a glimpse of oneself.

Even the advertisements are a pleasure. I read them as other people read novels. What strikes one first about them is their extraordinary unanimity. Every room is "desirable." Every situation is "convenient." Every house is "select," every landlady "refined," every neighbourhood "quiet and exclusive." There is not, in the whole of the columns, a single room that is admitted to be dark, or a single street that is confessed to be noisy. One begins to wonder where the dark rooms and the noisy streets have gone. Nowhere, apparently. They do not advertise.

But of all the places I have inspected, the most charming, beyond any comparison, are the old legal chambers of London. There is about them something that no newer building can pretend to. [detail needed: what the chambers were like] I did not always take them. I did not always want them. But I never climbed one of those staircases without a sense that I was, so to say, being permitted a glimpse of something that had been going on quietly for a very long time, and would go on, whether I took the rooms or not.
