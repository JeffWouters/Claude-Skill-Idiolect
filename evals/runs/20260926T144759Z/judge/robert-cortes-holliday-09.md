# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-09

## The passage (by the author)

The panorama before his view is the human mind. He panders to its divers follies, consults its varied wisdom. He stands umbrellaless in the rain of all its idiosyncrasies. Why has he not lifted up his voice? He, the book clerk, that lives among countless volumes of confessions! Whose daily task is to wrestle hour by hour with a living Comedie Humaine! Has the constant spectacle of so many books been astringent in its effect upon any latent creative impulse? Or has he been dumb in the colloquial sense, forsooth; a figure like Mr. Whistler's guard in the British Museum? Sundry "lettered booksellers" of England have, indeed, given us some reminiscences of bookselling and its humours. But they were the old boys. They belonged to an old order and reflected another day. "As physicians are called 'The Faculty' and counsellors-at-law 'The Profession,'" writes Boswell, "the booksellers of London are called 'The Trade.'" Let us look into this Trade as it is to-day, we said. So for a space we played we were a book clerk.

There are two, decidedly contradictory, popular conceptions of the man whose business it is to sell books. One is the sentimental notion of an old gentleman in a "stovepipe hat," a dreamer and an idealist, who keeps a second-hand stall. The most delightful pictures of him are in the pages of Anatole France. He is a man of much erudition. And books are his wife and family, food and drink. Then there is the other idea. "Why is it," we report the remark of an important looking gentleman in a high hat, "that clerks in book stores never know anything about books?" (or anything else, was perhaps not far from his thought.) This gentleman, it was readily perceived, had an idea that he had said something rather good. But it was not new. This conception of the book clerk is one of the world's seven jokes--brother to that of the mother-in-law. The book clerk of this view is a familiar figure in the pages of humour, like the talkative barber or the comic Irishman of the vaudeville stage--a stock character. His illiteracy is classic; his ignorant sayings irresistable. He was sired by Charles Keene and damned by Punch. Phil May was his godfather; and every industrious humourist employs him periodically. These two ideas of the book business are perhaps reconciled by the popularly cherished sentiment that book sellers are not what they were. Newspapers from time to time print feature articles about the days "When Book Sellers Knew Books." If you ask a salesman in a modern book shop if he has "Praed," you of course expect him to reply, "I have, sir (or madam), but it doesn't seem to do any good."

## Draft A

It has often struck me as odd that so few bookshop assistants have ever written about their work. They are, after all, people who live among books, and one might expect a fair number of them to try their hand at making one. And they certainly have the material. No one sees more of the small peculiarities of human beings than the person behind a shop counter, and a bookshop draws a more varied and more peculiar public than most. Every day brings its quota of vanity, confusion, shyness, eccentricity and unexpected kindness, and the assistant sees all of it at close range. Yet almost none of it gets written down. The assistants watch, and remember, and say nothing.

Part of the reason, I suspect, is a sense that it would not be proper. The customers did not come in to be observed, and there is something that feels like a breach of confidence in recording what they did. Part of it may be simple tiredness; after a day of other people's questions, few want to spend the evening going over them again. [Further reason needed.] Whatever the cause, the silence has meant that the public has been left to form its own picture of the bookseller, and it has formed two, neither of them very accurate.

The first is the romantic picture. In it the bookseller is a learned, dreamy figure, grown old among his volumes, who knows the contents of every one of them and would rather talk about them than sell them. He lives a little apart from the world, gently absent-minded, and treats his shop as a kind of library with a till. It is an appealing picture, and I have met customers who came in expecting to find him and were visibly let down.

The second picture is the opposite, and less kind. In it the clerk is an ignorant young person who knows nothing about books except where they are shelved and what they cost, and whose ignorance is a fit subject for jokes. [Example of such a joke needed.] He cannot tell one author from another, recommends whatever is nearest, and has never read anything he sells. This picture is at least as common as the first, and it is repeated with a good deal of satisfaction by people who would not care to have their own work judged so quickly.

Alongside both runs a kind of nostalgia. People like to say that booksellers used to know their stock better, that in some earlier and better time the man behind the counter had read everything on his shelves and could lay his hand on any book without looking. Perhaps it was so. But the claim is always made about the past, never about any bookseller the speaker actually knew, and I have learned to treat it with some caution.

The truth, as usual, lies somewhere among the pictures and is more ordinary than any of them. It is also more interesting, and it is a pity that those best placed to describe it so seldom do.

## Draft B

It is a curious thing, when you come to think of it, that so little has been written by the people who sell books. Everybody else writes. The doctor writes his reminiscences; the actor writes his memoirs; the butler and the ship's steward, each in his turn, set down what they have seen of mankind from their particular corner of it. But the bookshop assistant very seldom says a word. Yet of all people he ought to be at home among books; and of all people he has the best opportunity of observing the curious creatures who buy them.

And he has seen a great deal. All day long the human procession passes before his counter: the vain and the timid, the learned and the pretentious, the decided and the hopelessly undecided. He sees their small vanities, their odd economies, their sudden and inexplicable enthusiasms. He hears what they say about authors, and what they say about one another. He could, if he chose, compile a natural history of the reading public that would be a good deal more entertaining than most of what the reading public reads. Why does he not? It may be that he is tired; it may be that he is discreet. Or having spent the day among so many books, he may feel that the world has enough of them already.

Meanwhile the public has made up its own mind about him, or rather has made up two minds, which do not agree. According to the first view the bookseller is a learned and gentle creature, dusty and absent-minded. He sits among his folios in a kind of perpetual twilight, reading; and he regards a customer, when one appears, as a not altogether welcome interruption. This is a charming figure. It exists chiefly in essays.

According to the second view the clerk in the bookshop is an ignorant young person who has never read anything. He cannot tell one author from another; he would sell you a cookery book in place of a volume of philosophy if the bindings happened to be the same colour. This figure exists chiefly in the conversation of people who have been kept waiting.

And then there is the nostalgic view, which is the most persistent of the three. In the old days, it is said, booksellers really knew their stock. You had only to name a book, and the man behind the counter could tell you not only whether he had it but where it stood, and what was wrong with the second edition, and why you would be better off with the first. Whether this golden age ever existed I cannot say. [example needed: evidence either way] It is always, I notice, a little further back than anybody can remember.

## Draft C

It has often seemed to me odd that the people who sell books so seldom write about it. Policemen write their recollections. Actors write theirs. Doctors, lawyers, hotel keepers, and gentlemen who have been in the diplomatic service all write theirs. But you do not often come across the memoirs of a bookshop assistant. And yet, if you think of it, who sees more? All day long the public passes before him, in every variety. He sees it when it is vain, and when it is foolish, and when it does not know its own mind, and when it is pretending to know a great deal more than it does. He sees its quirks and its crotchets and its little absurdities, by the hundred, by the thousand. And he says nothing. He wraps the parcel, and makes out the slip, and says "Thank you," and holds his peace.

Perhaps it is because the public has already made up its mind about him. It has, indeed, two minds about him, and they do not agree.

According to the one, the bookseller is a learned man. He is elderly, rather. He wears spectacles, and very probably a skull-cap. He sits at the back of a dim shop among piles of old folios, and he knows everything that is in all of them. He is a little absent. He is a dreamer. He would rather talk to you about an early edition than sell you anything, and when at last he does sell you something he does it absently, as if it were not quite the point.

According to the other, the clerk in a bookshop is an ignorant young person who has never read anything. You have only to ask him for a book to find that out. He has never heard of it. He does not know who wrote it. He goes away to ask somebody, and comes back and says that they have not got it. It is a favourite subject for humorous writers. You will find him, in this character, in the comic papers, pretty regularly.

And then there is the gentleman who remembers. He remembers the booksellers of his youth. They, he says, knew their stock. You could ask them for anything and they would put their hand upon it. They had read the books they sold. It is not so now. I do not know whether it is so now or not. I do not know whether it was so then. I notice only that the good old days, in this business as in every other, appear always to have been some time before one was in a position to check them.
