# Judge packet synthetic-noor-01

## The passage (by the author)

It came loose on the third try.

And there was the washer. Black rubber, once. Now it was grey and hard and split on one edge like a dry lip. No wonder. It could not seal against anything.

I took it to the shop on the corner. The man there has a drawer of washers sorted by size. He held mine up to the light. Then he picked one out without looking. Half inch. 30 cents. I bought three.

At home the new washer went on with a small nut. I tightened it by hand, then a quarter turn with pliers. Not more. Rubber needs room to press.

Then back in with the headgear. Snug, then a little more. I wiped the threads first. Grit in the threads is how you strip them.

Handle on. Screw in. Blue cap pressed home with my thumb.

So then the valve under the sink. I turned it slowly. You want the pipes to fill without a bang. There was a hiss. Then a knock somewhere in the wall. Then quiet.

I opened the cold tap. It spat twice and ran clean. I closed it.

And I waited.

I stood at the sink and watched the spout for a full minute. Nothing. I went and made a coffee and came back. Nothing.

The whole job took 40 minutes. Most of it was the stop valve and the walk to the shop. The actual work was ten minutes. That is how it goes with small repairs. The work is short. The getting ready is long.

I put the two spare washers in a jar marked TAP. The jar lives in the drawer by the back door. It holds the old one too. I keep the old one so I remember the size.

That night the kitchen was very quiet. I noticed it when I switched off the light. No tick. No small wet sound in the dark.

## Draft A

The old seal came out in pieces. It was flat, hard and cracked along one edge. No wonder the tap dripped.

So I took it to the local hardware store. I put it on the counter and asked for the same again. It cost [price needed]. That is the whole fix, really. One small ring of rubber.

Here is the rest of it.

First I cleaned the seat where the seal sits. Grit and scale build up there. A new seal on a dirty seat will leak again. Then I pressed the new seal in with my thumb. Not with a screwdriver. A screwdriver can nick the rubber, and a nick is a leak.

Then the valve went back in. I turned it by hand until it stopped. Then a little more with the spanner. Not hard. Brass is soft and the threads strip easily. [example needed: a past slip with a thread]. I said I would be more careful. So I was.

Then the handle. It only fits one way. I put it back the way it came off.

So then the water. This is the part people rush. I did not. I opened the supply valve a quarter turn and waited. The pipes knocked, then spat. Air came out of the tap first. Then a thin run of water. Then a full one. Another quarter turn. Then another. If you open it all at once, the full force hits the new seal in one go. That is how you undo an hour of work in a second.

The fix itself took [time needed]. Getting ready took far longer. Finding the tools. Clearing out the cupboard under the sink. Working out which valve was which, and which way it turned. That is always the way.

The spare seals went in a small bag. The bag went in the drawer with the spanner. Next time they will be there.

This morning the tap is dry. I checked it twice.

## Draft B

The old seal came out in pieces. It had gone flat and hard, and it was cracked across one side. That was the drip. Water had been getting past that crack for a long time.

I took the pieces to the local hardware store. Take the old one with you. Seals look alike, and they are not. I held mine against the new ones until I found the match. It cost very little. [price needed] I bought two.

Then the reassembly. This is where you slow down. Everything goes back in the order it came out. I had laid the parts on a towel in a line, left to right, so the order was right there in front of me. New seal first. Then the valve. Then the handle. Hand tight to start. Then a gentle turn with the spanner. No more than that. Brass is soft. If you lean on it, you strip the thread. Then you have a bigger job than the one you started with.

Now the water. Do not open the supply valve all the way in one go. Open it a little. Listen. Let the pipe fill. The air comes out first, in coughs and spits. Then water. Then a bit more. Only then did I open it the rest of the way. I stood there and watched the spout.

Nothing. No drip. I watched it a while longer to be sure. Still nothing.

The repair itself was quick. The preparation was not. Clearing out the cupboard under the sink. Finding the right tools. Shutting off the water. Laying out the towel. Working out how the thing came apart. That took far longer than putting a new seal in. It is always the way with jobs like this. The doing is short. The getting ready is long.

The spare seal went into a small bag, and the bag went into the drawer with the spanner. The old pieces went in too, so I know what to ask for next time. Because there will be a next time. There always is.

## Draft C

With the worn seal finally out, the rest of the job turned out to be the easy part. I had half expected to need a whole new cartridge, or worse, a new faucet, but the fix was a single replacement seal from the local hardware store. It cost very little. I took the worn seal with me, which I would recommend to anyone: comparing the two side by side removes all the guessing.

Back home, the temptation was to rush. The leak had been annoying me and the new part was in my hand, so why not just push everything back together and be done? Because reassembly is where a cheap repair turns into an expensive one. I fitted the seal carefully, made sure it sat flat and evenly in its seat, and put the valve assembly back in the same order it came out. Each part went on snug, not forced. Overtightening brass is a good way to crack something or crush the very seal you just bought.

Then came the water. I did not open the supply valve all the way in one go. I opened it slowly, a little at a time, and watched the faucet and the joints underneath for any drip or seep. Letting the pressure build gradually gives you a chance to spot a problem before it becomes a puddle, and it is gentler on a fresh seal. Nothing leaked. I ran the tap, shut it off, and waited. The drip was gone.

What struck me afterwards was the ratio. The repair itself, the actual swapping of one small part, took very little time. Getting there took far longer: finding the right tools, shutting off the water, taking things apart without damaging them, and the trip to the store. That is normal, I think, for most jobs around the house. The preparation is the work.

The hardware store sold the seals in a pack, so the spares went into a labelled bag in the toolbox. Next time, and there will be a next time, I will not need the trip.
