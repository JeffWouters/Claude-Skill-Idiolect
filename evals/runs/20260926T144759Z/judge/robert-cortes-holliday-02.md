# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-02

## The passage (by the author)

There was also last summer an apartment to let to a "respectable man" or, the announcement said, it "might do for friends." One of the reasons why many people are bored by hunting lodgings is that they are not humble in spirit. They seek proud lodgings.

As to apartment houses, which are a very different matter: the newspapers publish at various seasons of the year copious Apartment-House Directories, with innumerable half-tone illustrations of these more or less sumptious places. And these directories are competent commentaries on their subject. George Moore remarked, "With business I have nothing to do--my concern is with art." Except that I live in one, with apartment houses I have nothing to do--my concern is with lodgings.

There is only one philosophical observation to be made upon apartment houses. And that is this: How can all these people afford to live in them? When you go to look at apartments you are shown a place that you don't like particularly. You don't think, Oh, how I'd just love to live here if I could only afford it! But you ask the rental as a matter of form. And you learn that this apartment rents for a sum greater (in all likelihood) than your entire salary. And yet, there are miles and miles of apartment houses even better than that. And goodness knows how many thousand people live in them! People whose names you never see in the newspapers as ones important in business, in society, art, literature, or anything else. Obscure people! Very ordinary people! Now where do they get all that money? But about lodgings:

I one time went to look at lodgings in Patchin Place. I had heard that Patchin Place was America's Latin Quarter. I thought it would be well to examine it. Patchin Place is a cul-de-sac behind Jefferson Market. A bizarre female person admitted me to the house there. It was not unreasonable to suppose that she had a certain failing. She slip-slod before me along a remarkably dark, rough-floored and dusty hall, and up a rickety stair. The lodging which she had to let was interesting but not attractive. The tenant, it seemed, who had just moved away had many faults trying to his landlady. He was very delinquent, for one thing, in the payment of his rent. And he was somewhat addicted to drink. This unfortunate propensity led him to keep very late hours, and caused him habitually to fall upstairs.

## Draft A

Most people who are looking for rooms are unhappy. I have observed this. You will see them upon the steps of houses in the side streets, with a newspaper folded back to the advertisements in one hand and a pencil in the other, and in their faces a look of settled grievance. The trouble is not with the rooms. The trouble is that the person looking for rooms wants a flat, and not merely a flat but a grand flat, with a man in a uniform at the door, a lift, a view, and a bath with the water hot at all hours. He has seen these places. He has walked past them. And after that the third floor back with the gas ring does not look quite what it did.

How the grand flats are paid for is a mystery to me. The rents are something tremendous. I have looked at the figures, and the figures are [example needed] a year, and more. Now, who are the people who live in them? You never hear of them. They are not in the newspapers. They are not famous for anything. Nobody knows who they are. And yet there they are, by the hundred, by the thousand, row above row, lights in every window, paying these rents, quarter day in and quarter day out, without, apparently, the smallest inconvenience. I have given a good deal of thought to it, and I cannot make it out.

I, for my part, looked at a room the other day in a house off a little lane in Greenwich Village. It was a shabby house. The front of it had been painted at one time, but not lately. The landlady took me up. The room had been, she said, "just vacated." It had. It had been vacated so recently that the last tenant, in a manner of speaking, was still there. He had been, it appeared, a man of habits. He had smoked, and not in moderation; the curtains knew it. He had not cared for the waste-paper basket; the floor knew it. He had, it would seem, [example needed]. The landlady said he was "a very quiet gentleman." I dare say he was. I did not take the room.

## Draft B

There is a great deal of complaint, I observe, among persons engaged in the search for furnished rooms. They travel about the city with melancholy countenances. They declare that there is nothing available, that whatever is available is excessively expensive, and that whatever is excessively expensive is not habitable. I have listened to a considerable number of them, and I have arrived at the conclusion that the difficulty is not with the rooms. The trouble is that the people who look for rooms are, nearly all of them, looking for something else. They want quarters. They want imposing quarters: a drawing-room with a prospect, a vestibule with a mirror, a bathroom of marble and an attendant at the entrance in a uniform with brilliant buttons. Well, there is a proper place for such magnificence, and it is not a rented room.

Now, the curious part of it is that somebody evidently has the grand quarters. You have only to promenade along any of the avenues where the new apartment houses stand to perceive that they are occupied, floor upon floor, window upon window, from the pavement to the cornice. Who are these people? I do not know one of them. Nobody I know knows one of them. They are not in the newspapers; they are not, so far as one can discover, in anything. Yet every month, it appears, they pay a rental which would maintain a sensible citizen in comfort for a season. How they manage it is one of the impenetrable mysteries of the metropolis, and I have abandoned the attempt to solve it.

For my part I went down to Greenwich Village. There is a house there, off one of those little crooked lanes that seem to have been laid out by a cow with no sense of direction, which had a card in the window. It was a shabby house. The steps were worn in the middle, the railing had lost some of its paint and most of its conviction, and the bell rang somewhere far off, as if in another building. I was admitted, conducted upstairs, and shown the apartment.

It was not the room that interested me; it was the previous occupant. The previous occupant had departed, but had left a character behind, so to speak, in every corner. ## Draft C

Anyone who has gone looking for rented rooms in the city will have met a great many unhappy people doing the same thing, and I have come to think that most of their unhappiness is of their own making. They are not really looking for rooms. They are looking for grand quarters. They carry in their heads a picture of lofty ceilings, polished floors, a view, a quiet street and a sympathetic landlord, all at a rent they can comfortably afford, and every door they knock on is measured against that picture and found wanting. Naturally they come away disappointed. The city does not keep a supply of palaces for people of modest means, and it never has.

The luxury flats are another matter, and a puzzling one. They go up in block after block, and they are let, apparently without difficulty, at rents that seem to me steep beyond reason. Who pays them? That is the mystery I have never solved. One does not know these people. They are not famous; their names are never in the papers; they do not, so far as anyone can see, do anything remarkable. Yet there are evidently a great many of them, enough to fill building after building, and every one of them manages each month to find a sum that would keep a more ordinary tenant for a good deal longer. I have sometimes stood outside such a building in the evening and looked at the lighted windows and wondered what all those unknown people do for a living, and how they do it so well.

The rest of us go elsewhere. Not long ago I followed an advertisement to a house off a lane in Greenwich Village. It was shabby in the way that only an old house can be shabby, with the paint gone thin and the stairs worn into hollows, and the room I had come to see was at the top. The door was opened for me and I went in. The last tenant had left, but he had not left entirely. His habits were written all over the place. [Details of what the room showed about the previous tenant needed.] It was not hard to form a picture of the man, and it was not a flattering one. I stood there for a while reading the evidence, as one might read a letter left open on a table, and then I thanked the person who had shown me up and went back down the worn stairs.

I did not take the room. But I did not regret the visit, either. A room that tells you so plainly who lived in it before is, in its way, better company than a luxury flat that tells you nothing at all.
