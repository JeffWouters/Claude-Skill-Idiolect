# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-03

## The passage (by the author)

Then there are the remarkable people who "keep up" with the flood of fiction; who say, "Oh, I've read that," in a tone which implies that they are not so far behind as that! "Have you no new novels?" they inquire. Novels get "old," one might suppose, like eggs, in a couple of days. The quest of these seekers of books suggests the story of the lady at a public library who, upon being told that seven new novels had come in that morning, said, "Give me, please, the one that came in last." There are, too, those singular folks who appear regularly every year just before Christmas, buy a great quantity of books for presents, and disappear again until the next year just before the holiday season. What, we have wondered, do they do about books the rest of the time? Ministers are always very trying characters to book clerks. "Beware of the gallery," says a fellow serf to us, "there's a minister browsing around up there." The official servants of the Lord fall, in the book clerk's mind, into that class technically described by him as "stickers." All gentlemen wearing high hats also belong to this classification. Deaf customers are embarrassing, for the reason that one always addresses one's next customer as though he were deaf, too. Foreigners are invariably very polite to clerks. They bow when they enter and take off their hats upon leaving. Very respectful people. "There," said a fellow thrall, "come two old women in at the door. Now, if I were my ancestor, I'd dance around that table with a stone club and brain them." As it is, they ask, "Have you Hopkinson Smith's 'Gondola Days'?" He says, "I think so." A lady, very rich and important looking, wants a book "without an unpleasant ending." "I wonder how this is" (looking at the last page). "No" (closing the book with a thump), "that won't do." A gentleman orders two sets of the Prayer Book and Hymnal, to be marked upon the cover with his name, the words Grace Church and his pew number. He informs us that every year while he is away in the summer his set of these books is stolen.

## Draft A

There are customers, and there are customers. Some of them, I will say, are no trouble at all. Others are another matter.

There is, for instance, the lady who wants only the newest novel. Not a new novel; the newest. She has read the one that came out last week. She wants the one that is coming out to-morrow. If you hand her a book that has been out a month she holds it a little away from her, as one might hold a fish that has been some time out of the water. "Oh," she says, "that's old." A month, in her view of literature, is a long time. A year is antiquity.

Then there is the customer who comes in at Christmas. He does not come in at any other time. All the year round, in the spring, through the summer, in the fall of the leaf, he is somewhere else. Then, in the middle of December, there he is, in a great hurry, with a list, and he wants everything wrapped.

There are the clergymen. Clergymen are very fond of bookshops. They come in, and they take down a book, and they read it. They read it for some time. Then they put it back and take down another. There are, too, gentlemen in top hats, very well brushed, who do much the same. They linger. They are very agreeable about it. But they do not buy.

And then there are the requests. One lady desired me to find her "a book with a happy ending." She did not mind what it was about, she said, so long as it came out right. I asked her whether she would not like to look at the last page herself before she bought it. She said she always did. There was a gentleman, too, who came in every year about the same time for a new prayer book. He had worn out, it appeared, the old one. He did this regularly, a prayer book a year. I have sometimes wondered how. Whether it was piety, or whether he was merely very hard upon his books, I was never able to learn.

## Draft B

Every bookshop assistant learns, sooner or later, to sort the customers into kinds. It is not done out of malice; it is simply the only way to survive the day. And some of the kinds are harder to serve than others.

There is, first, the customer who wants only the newest novel. Not a new novel, but the newest: the one that came out this week, or better still the one that is not quite out yet. Anything published a season ago is stale to him, however good it may be, and if you offer him an older book he looks at you as though you had tried to sell him yesterday's bread. He does not want to read so much as to be first, and when the next book appears he will want that one instead.

Then there is the customer who appears only at Christmas. For eleven months of the year the shop does not exist for him. In December he arrives, flustered and in a hurry, with a list of people to buy for and no notion of what any of them read, and he expects the assistant to supply in five minutes the knowledge of his family that he has not troubled to acquire in a lifetime. When the holiday is over he vanishes again until the following winter.

Some customers do not buy at all. Clergymen are much given to lingering. They come in, take down a volume, read a chapter or two standing at the shelf, put it back, and take down another. Gentlemen in top hats do the same, with a more dignified air, as though the shop were a club to which they happened to belong. They are no trouble, exactly, but they occupy space and time, and at the end of an hour they go out as empty-handed as they came in.

And then there are the odd requests, which are the hardest of all because there is no answering them sensibly. One customer wants a novel with a happy ending and would like to be assured of it before paying. Another comes in each year for a new prayer book, having apparently worn out or lost the old one, and treats the purchase as a regular annual chore, like having the chimney swept. [Further example needed.] The assistant takes them all in with a straight face. That, too, is part of the work.

## Draft C

Every bookshop assistant comes, in the course of time, to recognise certain species of customer, much as a naturalist comes to recognise the birds of his particular neighbourhood. Some of these species are harmless. Some are delightful. And some, it must be admitted, are difficult.

There is, first, the customer who wants only the newest novel. Not a new novel; the newest. He does not inquire what it is about, or who wrote it, or whether it is any good. He wishes to know whether it came out this week, and if you are obliged to confess that it came out last week his interest perceptibly cools. A book, to him, is rather like an egg; its freshness is its whole virtue. Then there is the customer who appears solely at Christmas. For eleven months of the year you never see him. In December he arrives, bewildered and in a hurry, with a list in his pocket and an expression of profound suspicion, as if the entire establishment had been assembled for the purpose of deceiving him.

Next, the loiterers. The clergy are, as a class, extraordinarily devoted to bookshops, and extraordinarily reluctant to purchase anything in them. A clergyman will stand at a table for the better part of a morning, reading steadily, turning the pages with a gentle and considerate hand; and then he will smile upon you, very kindly, and depart. The gentleman in the top hat is of the same persuasion, though less gentle. He examines everything; he buys nothing; he leaves, you feel, with the impression that the stock was not quite up to his standard.

Finally there are the odd requests, which are a category in themselves.

"Have you anything with a happy ending?"

"What kind of book did you have in mind?"

"Oh, any kind. So long as it ends happily."

There is also the customer who wished to purchase a prayer book, and who explained, when we inquired about a preference in bindings, that it did not matter in the least, since a new one was bought every year. We did not ask what became of the old ones. It seemed, somehow, indelicate. But I've wondered about them since.
