# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-06

## The passage (by the author)

'Tis a merry life, the book clerk's, and a hard one. Customers: Two youngish women. "Can you wait on us?" They want to get something, do not know just what, for a present. "Oh, no!" they say, "we don't want anything like so big a set as that. Something nicely bound." A copy of "Cranford" is near by. "Oh, when I read it I didn't think it much good." "Poetry?" "No, I don't think she is much interested in poetry." "Do you suppose an art book?"------"No, she is not interested in art." "Memoirs, then?" "No, she would not care for that." "Why, I had no idea," said one somewhat reprovingly to us, "that it would be as hard as this."

A calling which requires the practitioner to turn easily from the recondite gentleman inquiring the author of "Religious Teachers of Ancient Greece" to consideration of the problem (no less recondite) of a lady anxious to find something to entertain a child of five and a half inculcates some degree of mental agility. "I want," said the very fashionable lady, "to get a book for an old man--a" (with some petulance) "very stupid old man." "I want," from a serious old lady, "to get a book for a young man studying for the ministry." "I want," exclaimed a very smart apparition, "a dashing book for a man!" "What is the best book on Russia?" "Do you know, now, if this is a good story?--there are so many poor books nowadays." Says a large, uncommonly black lady, "I want 'Spears of Wheat, No. 3.'" (Discovered to be a prayer book.) "I want the latest book, please, on how to bring up a baby." "I'd like to see what you have on 'physical research.'" "Can you recommend a book for a young man with softening of the brain? Poor fellow, he's in Bloomingdale." "Is there any discount to Christian workers?" "Do you know," a demure person, an awful blank look coming over her face, "what I want has gone quite out of my head." There is an appealing look for help. "Something American," in a patrician voice, "for the ladies to read going over on the boat. This is American, now, is it? New York society? Ah, very good! Have you anything about the Rocky Mountains, or that sort of thing?"

## Draft A

Nobody who has not stood behind the counter of a bookshop for a whole day can quite appreciate the extraordinary variety of what is asked of the person who stands there. The public imagines, I believe, that a bookseller sells books. He does. But he also advises, consoles, instructs, arbitrates and, upon occasion, guesses, and he is expected to do all of these things promptly, cheerfully and without the least appearance of surprise.

Consider, to begin with, the customer who is looking for a present. This customer is the most hopeful of all at the door and the most despondent of all at the counter. He wants something for somebody. He does not know what. He knows only what he does not want, and he discovers this by the simple method of rejecting everything you show him. You offer him a novel, and it is too light. You offer him an essayist, and it is too heavy. You offer him poetry, and he looks at you as though you had suggested something slightly improper. Well, you proceed through the entire stock in this fashion, and at the end of it he thanks you very kindly and says that he will think it over.

Then there is the constant change of altitude, so to speak. At one moment you are called upon to discuss, with a learned person, some nice point of scholarship [example needed: a scholarly query], and you must do it gravely and with every appearance of erudition. At the next you are required to find, for somebody else entirely, some trifle of no consequence whatever [example needed: a trivial query]. There is no interval between these transactions. You descend from Olympus to the parlour in the time it takes to turn round, and you are not permitted to look dizzy.

And the demands themselves! In a single day you may be asked for a book on the care of babies, for something to read on a journey, not too long, not too short, not too exciting, and for [example needed: further customer requests]. You supply what you can. You apologise for what you cannot. And you go home in the evening feeling, in a modest way, like an encyclopaedia that has been rather roughly handled.

## Draft B

If you want to know what a bookshop assistant does all day, the honest answer is that he answers questions, and that no two of them are alike.

A good part of the day goes to people buying presents. They are the most difficult to please, because they do not know what they want and are determined not to be told. The assistant suggests a novel; it is too light. He suggests a volume of essays; it is too heavy. He suggests a book of travel, a biography, a collection of poems, and each is turned over, considered, and put back with a small shake of the head. At the end of it the customer is no nearer a decision than at the start, and quite often he leaves without buying anything, having rejected every suggestion the shop could offer. The assistant has spent half an hour and learned only what the recipient does not like, and even that is uncertain.

What makes the work tiring is not the difficulty of any single question but the speed with which the subject changes. One moment a scholar wants to know whether the shop has a particular edition of an old text, and which of two translations is to be trusted. The next, someone wants a book of riddles for a party. Then comes a question about a reference work, and then one about a picture book, and the assistant has to shift his mind from the one to the other without a pause, taking each as seriously as the customer takes it. There is no time to settle into any one level of seriousness.

And the range of what people ask for is very nearly endless. In the course of one day, customers may want a book on the care of a baby, and quickly; something to read on a long journey, not too heavy to carry; [example needed]; [example needed]; a book that a particular person will certainly like, although the customer cannot say what that person reads; and something, anything, on a subject the customer cannot quite name.

A few of these can be satisfied at once. Most need a little conversation first, to find out what is really wanted, which is seldom quite what was asked for. Some cannot be satisfied at all, and the assistant must say so politely and hope the customer comes back another day. By closing time he has been, in turn, a librarian, a nursemaid's adviser, a travel agent and a mind reader, and he has sold, perhaps, a handful of books.

## Draft C

Consider the person who wants a present. He comes in, and he wants a book for somebody, and he does not know what. You suggest something. No, he does not think that would do. You suggest something else. No, not that. You suggest a third thing, and a fourth, and a fifth. He is very polite about it. He looks at each one and he puts it down. He has, it appears, a very clear idea of what he does not want, and no idea whatever of what he does. After half an hour or so he goes away, thanking you, to try somewhere else. I have never learned what he gave.

That is one customer. There are a great many others, and they come all in the same day, one on the heels of another, and the bookshop assistant must meet each of them where he stands. One moment a learned gentleman is asking after an edition of [example needed], with notes; the next, a young woman wishes to know whether we have anything about the moving pictures. You go from the one to the other without a breath between. You must be scholarly, and then you must be frivolous, and then you must be scholarly again; and all the time you must keep your countenance.

I will set down, as nearly as I can remember them, some of the things that I have been asked for. A book on the care of the baby. A book on the care of the teeth. Something to read on the train; not too long, as the gentleman was going only as far as [place]. Something to read on the steamer; rather long, as the lady was going abroad. A book in a red binding, to go with a room. A book that a lady had seen in somebody's house, with a picture on the cover, of which she did not know the name nor the author. A book on etiquette. A book of jokes for a banquet. A book for a boy who did not like books. A book to give an invalid, "nothing sad." A book on how to train a dog. A book on how to be happy. A book for a man who had everything.

Early and late, week in and week out, you give your mind to these things. It is, you might say, a liberal education. Whose education, I have never been quite sure.
