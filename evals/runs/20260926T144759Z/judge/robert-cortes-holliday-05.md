# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-05

## The passage (by the author)

Well, I told her, by way of making talk, that I believed I was held to be a reasonably honest person, and that I was frequently sober.

"Oh," she said, "I can see that you are a gentleman--in your way," she added, in a murmur.

So, you see, in hunting lodgings you not only see how others live, but how you seem to others.

It is certainly curious, the places in which to dwell which one is shown in hunting lodgings. Once I was given to view a room in which was a strange table-like affair constructed of metal. "You wouldn't mind, I suppose," said the lady of the lodging, "if this remained in the room?"

"Oh, not at all," I replied. "But what is it?"

"Why, it's an operating table," she explained. "Of course, you know," she added, "that I'm a physician. And," she continued, "of course I should want to make use of it now and then, but not regularly, not every day."

To a lady with a patch over her eye with lodgings to let in Broome Street I one time stated, by way of being communicative, that I was often in my room a good deal doing some work there. Ah! With many ogles and grimaces, she whispered hoarsely, with an effort at a sly effect, that "that was all right here. She understood," she said. Perfectly "safe place for that," it was. "The gentlemen who had the room before were something of the same kind."

As you know, "references" frequently are demanded of one hunting lodgings. To get into a really nice place one must really be a very nice person. "You know, I have a daughter," sighs the really nice landlady.

To obtain lodgings in Kensington one must be very well-to-do, particularly if one would be on the "drawing room floor." "I like these rooms very much," I said to a prim person there, and I hesitated.

## Draft A

If you wish to learn how you appear to strangers, do not consult your friends; consult a landlady. Your friends have grown used to you. The landlady sees you, as it were, for the first time and without prejudice, and she forms her opinion upon the doorstep, in something under a minute. It is not always a flattering opinion. It is nearly always an instructive one.

I remember a house in which I was shown a room of quite reasonable dimensions, clean, fairly lighted, and furnished in the customary manner, with one exception. Among the furniture stood a surgical table. It was, the landlady explained, her own surgical table, and it was offered with the room, without apology and without further remark. I do not know what she supposed I should do with it. I did not inquire. I have sometimes thought that she took me for a person who would find it useful, and I have never been able to decide whether that was a compliment.

In another house the landlady, having looked me over with great deliberation, arrived at the conclusion that my business, whatever it was, was not a thing to be spoken of. She said nothing directly. But she let me understand, by a series of nods and lowered tones, that she was a woman of the world, that she asked no questions, and that my comings and goings would be entirely my own affair. I don't think I have ever been so thoroughly misunderstood, or so generously forgiven for it.

The respectable houses are another matter altogether. Here nobody suspects you of anything; they merely require you to prove that there is nothing to suspect. You must furnish references as to character. You must, in the more fashionable neighbourhoods, furnish evidence of substantial means as well. Virtue, it appears, is necessary, but it is not sufficient. It must be solvent.

## Draft B

One of the unexpected rewards of looking at rooms to rent is that the landladies tell you, without meaning to, what you look like to strangers. You go to the door as yourself, with your own opinion of your character, and you come away with theirs, which is often quite different and nearly always more interesting.

Some of the encounters are simply strange. At one house I was shown a room that was pleasant enough, except that it contained the landlady's surgical table. She made no apology for it and offered no explanation; it seemed to be part of the furnishings, and she apparently expected the new tenant to live alongside it. [Details of why the table was there needed.] I did not stay long enough to find out whether it was ever used.

At another house the landlady looked me over carefully and decided that I must be engaged in some kind of secret and not quite respectable work. What gave her the idea I never learned. Perhaps it was the hour I called, or the way I asked questions, or simply that I did not volunteer an account of myself. She was not unfriendly about it. She seemed, if anything, rather pleased to have guessed, and she let me understand that she would ask no questions, which was plainly meant as a kindness.

The respectable houses are another matter. There the landladies want to know who you are before they will let you over the threshold, and they want it in writing. You must produce references, people who will vouch for your character, and the references must themselves be respectable. In the more upmarket houses it goes further: they want some assurance of wealth as well, and they make it clear that a good character, without the means to go with it, will not do.

Between the surgical table and the demand for references there is a whole range of human suspicion and trust, and the room hunter passes through all of it.

## Draft C

You learn a great deal, looking at rooms. You learn, chiefly, what you look like. A landlady will tell you. She will not tell you in so many words, but she will tell you. She opens the door, and she looks at you, up and down, and in that look you see yourself as the world sees you: not, perhaps, quite as you had supposed.

One landlady, a very pleasant woman, showed me a room at the back of her house. It was a good room, light and airy enough. In the middle of it stood a surgical table. It was hers, she said; that is, it had been used in the house. She did not explain. She offered me the room, table and all. She seemed to think that I would not mind the table. I have wondered since what there was about me that made her think so.

Another landlady had me placed, she thought, at once. She did not ask me what I did. She did not need to ask. She was quite sure that it was something that had better not be talked about. She was very understanding about it. She let me know that she asked no questions of her gentlemen, and that her gentlemen came and went as they pleased. She gave me, in short, to understand that she took me for some kind of a crook, and that she had no objection.

Then there are the respectable houses. In the respectable houses they want references. Who are you? Who knows you? What is your character? You must bring letters. And in the houses that are more than respectable, the really superior houses, with a brass plate by the bell, they want more than character. They want money. They want to know about your means. They want, you might say, a character for your bank account as well as for yourself. It is a curious thing, but they seem to regard the one as rather more important than the other.
