# Judge packet synthetic-noor-09

## The passage (by the author)

Every October the heating comes back on. And every October, the radiators knock.

It starts in the night. A tick, then a knock, then a long gurgle like a drain. The one in the back bedroom is the worst. It sounds like someone walking in the wall.

So on the first cold Saturday I bleed them. All nine. It takes about an hour.

The tool is a small brass key. It costs a few euros and it is the one tool I always lose. I keep three now. One on a hook in the boiler cupboard. One in the kitchen drawer. One in the toolbox. I can usually find one of the three.

First I turn the heating on and let it run for 20 minutes. The radiators get hot. Then I feel each one with my hand. Flat palm, top to bottom. A radiator with air in it is hot at the bottom and cold at the top. You can feel the line where the heat stops. On the back bedroom one, the line is halfway down.

Then I turn the heating off. This is the step people skip. Bleed a radiator with the pump running and you pull in more air. Or you get a jet of hot black water in the face. I have had both.

I wait ten minutes for things to settle. I make a coffee.

So then I start at the bottom of the house and work up. Downstairs first, then the landing, then the bedrooms. Air rises. If you start at the top, the air from below comes up after you.

At each radiator, the same steps.

Put an old cloth under the valve. The valve is a small square nut at one top corner. Fit the key. Hold a second cloth round the key. Then turn it a quarter turn anticlockwise. Slowly.

## Draft A

Every autumn, when the heating comes back on after the summer, our radiators announce that they need attention. They tick, gurgle and knock, and some of them heat only halfway. That is the sign that air has collected inside them, and bleeding them has become a fixed part of the season.

The first step is finding a radiator key, which is harder than it should be. They are small, they look like nothing much, and they go missing. So I keep several spares, in different places around the house [example needed: where they are kept]. Having more than one also means I am never stuck halfway through the job because the only key has vanished.

Before opening anything, I switch off the pump, or turn the heating off, and let things settle for a while. With the pump running, water is being pushed around the system, and it is harder to get the air out cleanly. With the pump off, the air rises to the top of each radiator and stays there, ready to be released.

Then I go round and feel each radiator. You do not need any instruments for this. Run your hand from the bottom to the top. A radiator with trapped air is warm below and noticeably cold in its upper zone, and the border between the two tells you roughly how much air there is. The ones that are warm all over can be left alone. The cold-topped ones get bled.

I work upward from the ground floor. Air moves up through the system, so starting at the bottom and finishing at the top means I am not pushing air into radiators I have already done. At each one, I open the bleed valve slightly, let the air hiss out, and close it again as soon as water appears.

When the house is done, I check the boiler pressure and switch everything back on. The noises fade, the radiators heat evenly, and the heating is ready for winter until the same time next year.

## Draft C

Every autumn the radiators start to knock. It happens the first week the heating comes on. Gurgles, ticks, a sound like water running in the wall. That is air. So I bleed them.

First, find a key. This is harder than it sounds. A radiator key is small and it goes missing. I have [number needed] of them now. One in the kitchen drawer, one on a hook, one in the toolbox. [locations needed] Even then, some years I can only find one.

Then feel each radiator. Put the heating on and wait for it to warm up. Then go round with a flat hand. Lay it on the front, low down, and move it up. Warm. Warm. Warm. Then cold. That cold band at the top is the air. It sits there and the hot water cannot get in. Some radiators are cold across the whole top. Some only in one corner. Some are fine. I do the whole house, every one, so I know which ones need doing.

Then switch the pump off. Turn the heating off at the boiler and let it settle. If the pump is running, it pushes air around the system while you are trying to let it out, and some of it goes straight back in. Or it pulls air in through the valve you have just opened. Give it a while. [time needed]

Then start at the bottom. Ground floor first. Then upstairs. Air rises, so it collects at the top of the house. If you do the top first and then the bottom, the air you let out downstairs just pushes up to fill the ones you have already done. Bottom to top, and you are chasing it the right way.

At each radiator, a cloth under the valve. Key in. A slow quarter turn. The hiss. Then water. Then shut it.

It takes most of an afternoon. The knocking stops. Until next autumn.
