## The passage (by the author)

There is a considerable literature on techniques that promise to improve the situation — relative sizing, probabilistic forecasting, the breaking down of work into ever smaller pieces — and I do not wish to dismiss any of them, since each captures something true about the problem. Yet I am struck by how often these techniques are adopted as a kind of procedural reassurance rather than as a genuine change in how uncertainty is discussed. A team may replace its hours with points, or its points with ranges, while the conversation surrounding the estimate remains exactly as it was: somebody asks when the work will be finished, somebody else provides an answer they suspect is optimistic, and everyone proceeds as though the answer were reliable. The technique changes; the institutional relationship to uncertainty does not. It may be that no method of estimation can compensate for an organisation that is unwilling to hear the word "unknown".

What would it look like to take uncertainty seriously? You might begin by treating every estimate as a statement about the present state of our knowledge rather than about the future, and by expecting it to change as that knowledge changes — which is to say, by treating revision as a sign of learning rather than as a failure of discipline. You might also distinguish, more carefully than we usually do, between the parts of a project that resemble work we have done before and the parts that are genuinely novel, since the former can often be estimated with reasonable confidence while the latter resist prediction almost entirely. None of this is especially original; it is, arguably, what thoughtful engineers have been saying for a very long time. The obstacle has never been a shortage of ideas but a reluctance, on all sides, to relinquish the comfort that a precise-sounding figure provides.

## Draft A

When forecasts keep missing, teams often reach for a new estimation method. Hours become story points, story points become t-shirt sizes, t-shirt sizes become some newer technique. Each switch brings a burst of optimism. A few months later, the forecasts are just as unreliable as before.

The reason is that the method was never the real problem. Teams change the units, but the conversations around the numbers stay the same. The same people still want a firm answer, the same pressure still pushes the figure towards what someone hopes to hear, and the same result is still treated as a commitment once it is written down. Swapping hours for points does not change any of that. It just gives the old habits a new vocabulary. [example needed]

What does help is a different way of thinking about what an estimate is. Any estimate is a snapshot of what the team knows right now. It reflects current understanding of the work, the system and the risks, and that understanding will shift as the work proceeds. Treating the number as a snapshot, rather than a fixed truth, makes it natural to update it as new information arrives, instead of defending it against the evidence.

It also helps to separate two kinds of work. Some work is familiar: the team has done something very like it before and has a reasonable sense of how long it takes. Estimates for that kind of work can be fairly dependable. Other work is truly new, involving unknown territory where nobody can say with confidence what they will find. Forecasting both kinds in the same way, with the same apparent precision, misleads everyone.

The barrier to all of this is not technical. It is attachment to precise numbers. A single exact figure feels reassuring, even when everyone suspects it is wrong. Letting go of that comfort is what actually improves forecasting.

## Draft B

Why is it that a new estimation method so rarely makes the forecasts of a team any more reliable than the method it replaced? It may be that the method was never the source of the difficulty, and that changing it is attractive precisely because it allows everyone to feel that something has been done without anyone having to change how they actually talk about the work.

Teams move from one unit to another, from one method to the next, from one ritual of guessing to another, and each change arrives with a certain optimism; the conversations, meanwhile, remain very much as they were. The same questions are asked in the same tone, the same pressures shape the answers, and the numbers that emerge are, I suspect, about as trustworthy as before, merely expressed in different units. Is it any wonder that the improvement so often fades once the novelty of the new scheme has worn off?

There is also a more useful way to think about what an estimate is. Any estimate is best treated as a snapshot of what a team knows at the moment it is made, a record of present understanding that will shift as that understanding grows, rather than as a commitment that must be defended once it has been spoken. It helps, too, to separate the familiar work that a team has done many times from the genuinely new work it has never attempted, since the first can be estimated with some confidence and the second, arguably, can hardly be estimated at all.

It seems to me that the real barrier is neither the method nor the skill of the team but a shared attachment to precise numbers, which offer a reassurance that the work itself cannot honestly provide. Perhaps a forecast becomes more reliable not when it grows more exact but when it admits how much it does not yet know.

## Draft C

Every so often a team that is frustrated with its forecasts decides that the problem lies in the method. Hours give way to points, points to sizes, sizes to some newer scheme that promises greater accuracy, and for a while there is a pleasing sense of progress. Then, gradually, the forecasts become as unreliable as they were before. I suspect this happens because the unit was never really the difficulty. The team has changed the vocabulary of its estimates while leaving the underlying conversation exactly as it was, and it is the conversation, rather than the unit, that determines whether a forecast means anything.

What would a different conversation look like? It seems to me that it would begin by treating every estimate as a snapshot of what the team knows at a particular moment, rather than as a fixed commitment about the future. Knowledge changes as work proceeds; so, inevitably, should the estimate. A figure that is revisited and revised as understanding grows is not a sign of poor planning but of honest planning, and it would be strange to expect otherwise.

It would also help to distinguish more carefully between kinds of work. Some tasks resemble things the team has done many times before, and for these a reasonably confident estimate is entirely possible. Others are genuinely new, involving unfamiliar systems or unclear requirements, and for these any precise number is largely a matter of hope. Treating both kinds as though they deserved the same confidence is, I think, one of the more reliable ways of being wrong.

None of this requires a new method. What stands in the way is something rather more stubborn: an attachment to precise numbers, and to the reassurance they seem to offer. Letting go of that reassurance is uncomfortable, and I suspect it is the real reason teams keep reaching for the next technique instead.
