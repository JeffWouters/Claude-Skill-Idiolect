# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-07

## The passage (by the author)

One can usually either begin or end with Mr. Chesterton, though one can seldom do both. “It is simpler to eat caviare on impulse than to eat grape-nuts on principle,” he says, in one of his intervals of pure lucidity. I should like to make a Chestertonian transposition, and pronounce that it is better (I do not say simpler) to eat caviare on principle than to eat grape-nuts on impulse. The fact is that the modern fad of simplicity for its own sake has ceased to be merely ridiculous; it has become dangerous. May not some of us lift our voices against it?

I have no right, I suppose, to ally, in my own mind, socialists and vegetarians. But I nearly always find, when I ask a vegetarian if he is a socialist, or a socialist if he is a vegetarian, that the answer is in the affirmative. I am sure that they, on their side, confuse snobs with meat-eaters. One could forgive them, were they more bitterly logical. For my own part, I should be quite willing to go the length of all Hinduism and say that rice itself has a soul. I can even see myself joining a “movement” for giving the vote to violets and disfranchising orchids. This, however, is not their desire. They do not wish to make even the ox a citizen--only a brother; and I have never discovered that vegetarians--even when they were “hygienic,” not “sentimental,” ones--were anxious to reproduce the history of the rice-fed peoples. But let their logic take care of itself. My point is really that socialists and vegetarians are banded together to fight for the simplifying of life. Socialism, of course, organizes as furiously as Capital itself; and I leave it to any one if a nut-cutlet is not complicated to the point of mendacity. But ostensibly both sects are on the side of Procrustes against human vagaries. Both would surely consider caviare immoral; either because no one ought to eat it, or because every one cannot. It does not much matter, I fancy, which point you make against the dried roe of the sturgeon. My own plea for caviare rests precisely on the fact that it is not, and cannot be, thrust into every one’s mouth. It is not simple, no. The only really “simple” food-stuff is manna. Imagine, for example, calling anything simple that has to be shot out of a cannon by way of preparation. In point of fact, very few people eat caviare save on impulse,--otherwise, they find it too nasty. But it is an impulse worthy of being dogmatized; of becoming a principle.

## Draft A

It has become fashionable to admire simplicity, and I would like to say a word for caviar.

I choose caviar because it is the kind of thing that the admirers of simplicity find hardest to forgive. It is costly, it is rare, and it is not to everyone's taste. Most people do not like it the first time. Those who come to like it do so by degrees, and having learned to like it they cannot easily explain the pleasure to anyone who has not. It is, in short, a luxury in the full sense: unnecessary, acquired, and delightful.

The cult of plainness regards all this with suspicion. What began as a sensible preference for less clutter has become a creed, and like most creeds it has grown intolerant. Plain food, plain rooms, plain clothes, plain speech: each is now offered not as one choice among many but as the virtuous choice, and anything more elaborate is treated as a small moral lapse. I think this has gone far enough to do real harm. It teaches people to distrust their own pleasures, and to mistake a narrowing of experience for an improvement of character.

The movement draws strength from some unlikely allies. The socialist wants everyone to live on the same terms, and the vegetarian wants everyone to eat on the same terms. They would not agree about much else, but they share an impulse to flatten life to a single standard, to find the one proper way of living and bring everyone into it. The trouble with a single standard is that it is always set low enough for everyone to reach. Whatever cannot be shared by all is ruled out, and what is ruled out first is exactly the rare and difficult thing, the pleasure that takes time and cultivation to arrive at.

I would argue the reverse. Rare, acquired pleasures are among the best things a civilization produces. They depend on long practice, on discrimination, on a willingness to learn to like what does not please at once. That is not so different from the way we come to value good books or good music. A taste for caviar is a small thing, but it belongs to the same family. It is worth holding not as a guilty indulgence to be apologised for, but as a principle: that life is richer for containing things that not everyone wants, and that not everyone can have.

Simplicity has its merits. It does not need a monopoly on virtue, and it should not be allowed to claim one.

## Draft B

I wish to say a word for caviar. It is not a popular thing to do, just now; I am aware that I shall be thought frivolous, if not actually wicked. But some one ought to say it, and I am not sure that any one else will.

For we have lived for some time under the reign of plainness, and it has gone too far. Simplicity was a very good thing when it meant getting rid of the horsehair sofa and the wax flowers under glass. It is not so good a thing when it means getting rid of everything that is not strictly necessary. The cult of the plain has become a kind of puritanism without a God--a determination that nothing shall be enjoyed which could possibly be done without. And I have come to think it is doing real harm. It is making us poorer, not in money, but in the capacity to take delight in things.

Consider who is pressing us toward it. The socialist would have every one live alike, on the ground that it is unjust for any one to live better than his neighbour. The vegetarian would have every one eat alike, on the ground that it is wrong--or unwholesome, or both--to eat what he does not eat. They do not, as a rule, dine together; I dare say they would disagree about everything else under the sun. But they agree in this: that life ought to be flattened to one standard, and that the standard ought to be theirs. Let us resist them, by all means. There is no one so worth resisting as the person who wishes to arrange one's dinner.

Now caviar is precisely the thing that no reformer could approve. It is expensive; it is rare; it is not nourishing in any sense a dietitian would admit. Worst of all, one does not like it at first. It is an acquired taste, and to acquire it one must have been willing to try, and try again, and wait for the pleasure to come. That, it seems to me, is exactly its virtue. The pleasures that come easily are within every one's reach, and nobody need defend them. It is the rare and difficult pleasures--the ones that take a little training and a little persistence--that need their champions.

I would go further. I would hold such pleasures as principles, and not merely as indulgences. A world in which nobody took the trouble to like caviar would be a world in which nobody took the trouble to like anything difficult: not Greek, not Bach, not the late novels of [person]. The palate and the mind are not so far apart as the plain-livers think. And I, for one, do not intend to starve either of them to please a committee.

## Draft C

I wish to say a word for caviar. It is not a fashionable thing to say; the fashion at present is all for simplicity, for brown bread and plain water and the virtuous discomfort of the unupholstered chair. There was a time when this cult of plainness did some good--when it was a reproach to vulgar display and a reminder that ostentation is not the same thing as taste. That time is past. The cult has outlived its usefulness and become a positive harm, for it no longer teaches us to distinguish between the good and the gaudy. It teaches us to distrust whatever is not ordinary.

Consider the allies it has made. The socialist wishes every one to have the same; the vegetarian wishes every one to eat the same, and that the dullest thing on the menu. They would not, I suppose, much enjoy each other's company; but they are agreed on the essential point, which is that life should be flattened to a single standard and that the standard should be low. One would level the purse, the other the palate. Between them they would leave us nothing that anybody had to learn to like.

And that, I think, is the heart of the matter. Caviar is not a pleasure one is born to. No child likes it; very few grown people like it at first. It is an acquired taste, which is only another way of saying that it is a taste one has to earn--by attention, by patience, by a certain willingness to be taught. So it is with most of the things worth having: with music that is not a tune, with verse that does not jingle, with wine that is not sweet. Some one will object that these are trifles. Granted; but a civilization is very largely made of trifles, and the capacity to value a rare thing is not trivial at all.

My point is only this: such pleasures are not indulgences to be apologized for. They deserve to be held as principles--defended, cultivated, handed on. The man who has learned to like caviar has learned something about the uses of discipline, and he has learned it more agreeably than most of us learn anything. Let the simple-lifers have their brown bread (and much good may it do them!). I shall keep my caviar. I shan't be ashamed of it.
