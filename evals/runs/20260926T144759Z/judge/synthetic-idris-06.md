# Judge packet synthetic-idris-06

## The passage (by the author)

It would be easy to conclude from this that recurring meetings should simply be abolished, and there are teams that have tried precisely that experiment with a certain evangelical enthusiasm. I am not convinced that the conclusion follows. What the meeting provides, even in its degraded form, is a guaranteed occasion for contact — a moment when you can raise something half-formed without having to justify interrupting anyone, when the quieter members of a team can be seen and heard, when small frictions surface before they harden into grievances. Perhaps the ritual persists precisely because it performs this function, however inefficiently, and because nobody has designed a better substitute. In some sense the meeting is less a tool for making decisions than a piece of social infrastructure; its value is not visible in any particular session but in the accumulated familiarity it produces over months. Remove it without replacing that function, and we may discover that the team has become efficient in a way that is also, subtly, lonelier.

What, then, might we do differently? One possibility is to treat each recurring meeting as something that must periodically earn its renewal, rather than as a permanent fixture whose continuation requires no argument. A team might pause every so often and ask, with genuine openness, whether the original purpose still holds, whether a different purpose has replaced it, and whether that new purpose is worth the collective attention it consumes. The conversation is often uncomfortable, since it touches on questions of status and inclusion that are rarely discussed openly; the person who organised the meeting may feel that their judgement is being questioned, and the people who value it most may be the least inclined to say so. It is nevertheless the kind of discomfort that tends to be productive, because it forces the team to articulate what it actually wants from its time together. I suspect that many meetings would survive such scrutiny in a modified form — shorter, less frequent, or reframed around a clearer question — and that a few would be quietly and gratefully retired.

## Draft A

Anyone who has sat through enough pointless recurring meetings has felt the urge to delete them all. Clear the calendar, reclaim the hours, and let people talk when they actually need to. It is an appealing idea, and it is usually a mistake.

Recurring meetings do something that is easy to overlook until it is gone. They create regular contact. People who might otherwise only meet in writing see each other at a predictable time, pick up on each other's moods, and hear about problems before they become formal. Small things get mentioned in passing that would never justify a message of their own. That steady rhythm is part of what holds a team together. Scrap every standing meeting and much of that contact quietly disappears, and it tends not to come back on its own. People mean to reach out when needed, but in practice they reach out less.

The opposite approach, keeping everything as it is, has its own cost. Meetings drift. They outlast their purpose and keep consuming time long after they stopped earning it. So neither deleting nor preserving by default is a good answer.

A better approach is to make each recurring meeting justify itself again from time to time. Every so often, ask plainly: what is this meeting for, and is it still doing that? Who needs to be here? Would a shorter slot, or a less frequent one, do the same job? The question is not whether the meeting was a good idea once, but whether it is a good idea now. [example needed]

This review is awkward. Someone usually set the meeting up and feels some ownership over it. Others may worry that suggesting a change looks like disengagement. It takes a little courage to ask whether an hour that everyone has grown used to is still needed.

But it is useful, and the results are usually modest rather than dramatic. Many meetings shrink: shorter, less frequent, with fewer people. Some end outright, and nobody misses them. The ones that survive come out of the review with a clearer purpose, which makes them better. The team keeps its regular contact and loses the parts that had stopped serving it.

## Draft B

Should a team simply scrap its repeating meetings, or is there something in them worth keeping even when their agendas have grown thin? It may be that the question is framed too starkly, since the choice between abolishing a meeting and tolerating it indefinitely leaves out a third possibility that is, I suspect, more useful than either.

The case for scrapping them is easy to make and is made often. Recurring meetings consume time, interrupt concentration and frequently persist long after anyone can say what they are for; removing them can feel like reclaiming a morning. What that case tends to overlook is the value of regular contact itself, which is harder to see precisely because it is so ordinary. A standing meeting is, in some sense, one of the few places where a team reliably encounters itself as a team, where people who otherwise work in parallel notice one another and where small difficulties surface before they become large ones. Remove every such occasion and the team may discover, rather later than it would like, that something holding it together has quietly gone.

There is also, however, a middle course, which is to ask each recurring meeting to justify itself again from time to time. This means setting aside a moment, at some regular interval, to ask what the meeting is for, whether it still does that, and whether it needs to happen as often or last as long; it means treating the answer as genuinely open rather than as a formality. The review is awkward, since it asks people to say aloud that a gathering they have attended faithfully may no longer be needed, and nobody quite enjoys that conversation.

It seems to me that the awkwardness is precisely what makes the exercise worthwhile. The most common outcome, arguably, is not abolition but shrinkage: many meetings become shorter or less frequent, and some are allowed to end altogether with rather less regret than anyone expected. The meetings that remain are then held with a clearer sense of why. Perhaps the aim is not to have fewer meetings but to have only the ones that can still explain themselves.

## Draft C

When a team finally notices that its standing meetings have become more habit than help, the temptation is to sweep them all away at once. There is a certain appeal in the clean calendar, the sense of reclaiming hours that had been quietly lost, and I understand why the gesture is so often made. I am less convinced that it is wise. Repeating meetings, whatever their faults, provide something that is easy to overlook until it disappears: a regular point of contact, a moment each week in which the people who share a piece of work are reliably in the same place. Much of what holds a team together happens in the margins of such meetings, in the brief exchanges before the agenda begins or the question someone would not otherwise have thought to ask. Scrap the meetings entirely and that contact tends to go with them, often without anyone noticing until the team has drifted apart.

The better course, it seems to me, is neither to abolish these meetings nor to leave them untouched, but to ask each one, from time to time, to justify itself again. What is this meeting for now, as opposed to when it began? Who needs to be here, and for how long? Would anything important be lost if it happened less often, or ended altogether? These are simple questions, and yet they are rarely put, perhaps because the meeting has become so familiar that it no longer seems like something that could be otherwise.

I should admit that the review is not a comfortable exercise. Proposing that a meeting shrink or stop can feel like a criticism of whoever started it, or of the people who value it, and there is always someone for whom a particular gathering matters more than the others realise. That awkwardness is, I think, part of its usefulness, since it forces into the open a conversation that would otherwise never happen. In practice, the result is seldom dramatic. Many meetings turn out to deserve a shorter slot or a smaller audience; a few, on reflection, have genuinely run their course and can be allowed to end.
