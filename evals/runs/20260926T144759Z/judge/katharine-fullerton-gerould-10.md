# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-10

## The passage (by the author)

Simplicity is an acquired taste. Mankind, left free, instinctively complicates life. The hardest command to follow has always been that which bids us take no thought for the morrow. Perhaps that is what Mr. Chesterton means when he talks of the difficulty of eating grape-nuts on principle. The real drawback to “the simple life” is that it is not simple. If you are living it, you positively can do nothing else. There is not time. For the simple life demands virtually that there shall be no specialization. The Hausfrau who is living the simple life must, after all, sweep, scour, wash, and mend. She must also cook; from that, even Battle Creek cannot save her. She may dream sternly of Margaret Fuller, who read Plato while she pared apples; but in her secret heart she knows that either Plato or the apples suffered. And from what point of view is it simpler to have a maid-of-all-work than to indulge one’s self in liveried lackeys? Not, obviously, for the mistress; and it is surely simpler to be an adequate second footman than to be an adequate bonne-à-tout-faire. We should really simplify life by having more servants rather than fewer; more luxury instead of less. The smoothest machinery is the most complicated; and which of us wants to sink the Mauretania and go back to Robert Fulton’s steamboat? One would think that the decision would be made naturally for one by one’s income. But it is the triumph of the new paradox that this is not so. Thousands of people seem to be infected with the idea that by doing more themselves they bestow leisure on others; that by wearing shabby clothes they somehow make it possible for others to dress better--though they thus admit tacitly that leisure and elegance are not evil things. Or perhaps--though Heaven forbid they should be right!--they merely think that by refusing nightingales’ tongues, they make every one more content with porridge. Let us be gallant about the porridge that we must eat, but let us never forget that there are better things to eat than porridge.

## Draft A

I have never understood why the simple life should be called simple. It is, so far as I have been able to observe, the most complicated life there is. The people who lead it are forever busy--baking their own bread, mending their own shoes, weeding their own gardens and teaching their own children--and they have not a moment left for anything else. They set out to escape the burdens of civilization, and they have only exchanged a few large burdens for a great many small ones.

For if you do everything yourself, you do nothing else. That is the whole of the matter. There are only so many hours in the day, and the woman who spends them all on the necessities of life will have none to spend on the luxuries of it--on reading, on talk, on the leisurely friendships that were, I had supposed, the point of living at all. She has simplified her possessions, very likely. She has not simplified her days.

The remedy, it seems to me, lies in exactly the opposite direction. What we need is not less specialization, but more: more people doing the one thing they can do well, and fewer of us doing badly the things we were never meant to do. Let us have more servants, by all means, and not fewer--and let us pay them properly and treat them decently, and let them be as expert in their work as we should like to be in ours. That is not a luxury. It is the only arrangement under which any one has time to think.

Oh, I know the answer. It is not right, we are told, that some should serve while others are served; and so the high-minded give up their servants and do their own work, by way of protest. But whom does the protest help? Not the servant, who has lost a place. Not the high-minded, who has lost her leisure. Voluntary self-denial is a very comfortable thing to the conscience, but I have never seen that it put a single extra loaf on any one else's table.

## Draft B

The simple life, so called, is the most complicated life I know. Its disciples have given up their servants and their luxuries in order, they say, to be free; and they spend the rest of their days doing, badly and at great length, what somebody else once did for them quickly and well. They cook; they wash; they mend; they sweep. By the time the house is in order, the day is over, and there is no hour left for the reading or the thinking or the talking for which the simplicity was supposedly undertaken. One is not freed by doing everything oneself. One is merely kept busy.

The remedy seems to me obvious, though I know it is not fashionable. Life would be made easier, not harder, by more specialization rather than less--by more people doing each the thing they do well, and doing it for others. That means, among other things, more servants, not fewer. Some one will cry out at this. Granted: it sounds like a reactionary's dream. But the principle is precisely the one on which every modern industry is run, and no one thinks it tyrannous that the baker bakes and the cobbler cobbles. Why it should become tyranny the moment it crosses the threshold of a private house, I have never understood.

There remains the moral argument, which the simple-lifers like best. They deny themselves, they tell us, out of sympathy with those who have less. It is a generous impulse, and I don't mock it. But whom does it help? The woman who dismisses her cook does not thereby feed a single hungry family; she merely adds one to the number of people looking for work. Voluntary self-denial improves the soul of the self-denier, perhaps. It does nothing whatever for the lot of any one else. My point is only this: if we wish to do good, we must do it to others, not to ourselves.

## Draft C

The simple life is recommended to us as a relief. Put away the elaborate household, do things for yourself, need less and depend on no one, and you will be free. In practice it works the other way. The so-called simple life is one of the most burdensome ways of living there is.

The reason is arithmetic. There are only so many hours in a day, and a household makes steady demands on them. Meals must be cooked, rooms cleaned, clothes washed and mended, errands run. When one person undertakes all of this, it swallows the day. The man who does everything himself has no time left for anything else: not for reading, not for friends, not for the work he is actually good at, and not, very often, for rest. What was sold to him as freedom turns out to be a full-time occupation with no wages and no holidays.

The better arrangement is the one that civilization has always moved towards: specialization. Each person does the work he does best and relies on others for the rest. The cook cooks, the gardener gardens, and the scholar studies, and each of them does his part better and faster than an amateur could. In a household, this means more servants rather than fewer. That is not a confession of idleness. It is a division of labour, and a division of labour makes life easier for everyone in it, including the people who are paid to do the work and would otherwise be looking for it elsewhere.

The advocates of simplicity sometimes answer that their choice is not about convenience but about conscience. They give up comforts, they say, out of solidarity with those who have none. The feeling does them credit, but the reasoning does not hold. Voluntary self-denial does nothing to improve anyone else's lot. The poor are not better fed because a comfortable family has decided to eat plainly, nor better housed because it has dismissed its maid. The sacrifice is real to the one who makes it and invisible to everyone else.

Simplicity, then, is not simple. It is complicated and tiring, and it helps nobody.
