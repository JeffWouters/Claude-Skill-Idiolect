# Judge packet synthetic-idris-01

## The passage (by the author)

What is a meeting, really, once it has been placed on the calendar and allowed to recur indefinitely? It may be that the recurring meeting is the closest thing most organisations have to a ritual, in the anthropological sense of the word — a gathering whose original purpose has quietly dissolved while its form persists, maintained by a vague institutional reluctance to be the person who suggests cancelling it. We arrive, we exchange the customary pleasantries, and we wait for someone to articulate why we are there; perhaps someone does, and perhaps the explanation is convincing, but more often the conversation drifts toward whatever happens to be most visible that week. I suspect that very few of us would defend the meeting if asked to justify it from first principles, and yet we continue to attend with considerable loyalty. There is something in this that deserves more attention than the usual complaints about wasted time, because the persistence of the ritual tells us something about what teams actually need from one another.

Consider the way a meeting begins its life. Usually it is created in response to a genuine difficulty — a project that has lost its sense of direction, a dependency between teams that nobody quite owns, a decision that keeps being deferred — and in those first weeks it is arguably the most valuable hour anyone spends. The people in the room have a shared problem; they bring information that cannot easily be written down; they leave with a clearer sense of who is doing what. The difficulty is that the problem eventually resolves, or changes shape, while the invitation remains exactly as it was, reproducing itself week after week with the indifference of any automated process. Does anyone notice the moment at which the meeting stops serving its purpose? In my experience the transition is almost always invisible, since the attendees are still talking, the notes are still being taken, and the calendar still insists that this is where we ought to be.

## Draft A

Most teams have at least one meeting that nobody would invent today. It sits in the calendar every week, people show up, the agenda gets worked through, and the hour fills itself. Ask why it exists and you will usually get a shrug, or a vague answer about keeping everyone aligned. The meeting has become a ritual, and rituals are very good at outliving the reason they started.

That origin was almost always sensible. Somebody noticed a real problem. Two groups kept stepping on each other's work, a release kept slipping because nobody had the full picture, or a new team needed a place to settle how it would operate. A standing slot was a reasonable answer. It gave people a predictable moment to raise issues, and for a while it did exactly that. The meeting earned its place because it solved something people could point to.

Then the problem went away, or changed shape. The groups learned to coordinate on their own. The release process got fixed. The new team stopped being new. None of this happened on a particular day, so there was never a moment when anyone could say the meeting had done its job. The invite kept recurring because recurring is what invites do by default. Cancelling takes an action; continuing takes nothing.

This is the part that makes the pattern so persistent. Usefulness fades gradually, while the meeting itself stays exactly the same size. Each individual session feels only slightly less valuable than the last, which is never enough to trigger a decision. People adjust instead. They bring their laptops, half-listen, and wait for the one item that concerns them. The meeting keeps running, but the attention has already left.

Nobody is at fault here, which is precisely why nobody acts. The person who set it up has moved on to other things, and everyone else assumes it still matters to someone. So the hour stays booked, long after anyone could explain what it is for. [example needed]

## Draft B

Why do standing team meetings so often continue long after anyone can remember the reason they were set up? It may be that we are looking at them in the wrong way, treating them as items on a calendar when they behave far more like rituals, which are known, after all, for outliving whatever first gave them meaning. A ritual does not need a purpose to survive; it needs only to be repeated, and repetition is precisely what a recurring invitation supplies.

It is worth remembering that most of these meetings began well. Someone noticed a real problem, perhaps that work was being duplicated or that decisions were reaching people too late, and the weekly gathering was a sensible, even a thoughtful, answer to it, which for a while arguably did exactly what it was meant to do. The difficulty is that the problem can quietly disappear while the answer stays in place, and nobody, in the ordinary course of things, is asked to notice the moment at which one stopped needing the other. Is it any wonder that the slot keeps filling itself, week after week, with updates that might have been an email?

There is also the matter of who would be the one to say so. Proposing to end a meeting can feel like a small accusation against whoever started it, or against everyone who has kept attending without complaint, and so the silence that allows the meeting to continue is in some sense shared by all of us. I suspect that many teams hold at least one meeting that every participant privately considers unnecessary, and that each, reasonably enough, assumes the others still find it useful.

It seems to me that the first step is simply to ask what problem a given meeting was meant to solve, and whether that problem is still with us. The answer will sometimes be yes, in which case the meeting can continue with a clearer sense of its purpose; sometimes it will be no, and the meeting, having served its turn, can be allowed to end. Perhaps the habit worth building is not the meeting itself but the willingness, now and then, to question it.

## Draft C

Most teams, if pressed, could not say exactly why their weekly meeting exists. It is simply there, occupying the same hour on the same day, as fixed a feature of the calendar as the weekend. I have come to think that repeating meetings behave less like tools than like rituals, and that, like many rituals, they have a remarkable capacity to outlive whatever first gave them meaning. The words are still spoken and the chairs are still filled; what has quietly departed is the reason anyone gathered in the first place.

It is worth remembering that such meetings rarely begin as empty gestures. Almost always they start as a sensible answer to a real problem: work that kept falling between two people, a decision that needed a regular place to be made, a group that had drifted out of touch and needed somewhere to find each other again. In those early weeks the meeting earns its hour without difficulty, because everyone in the room can feel the problem it was invented to solve. The trouble is that a successful meeting tends to dissolve its own justification. Once the problem has been addressed, the meeting continues, carried along by habit and by a vague sense that stopping it would be a kind of negligence.

What makes this so difficult to notice is that the decline is gradual rather than sudden. There is no single week in which the meeting becomes useless; there is only a slow thinning, in which the agenda shortens, the updates grow more ceremonial and the conversation drifts towards whatever happens to be on people's minds. Nobody is responsible for observing this, and so nobody does. Perhaps the more honest question to ask of any standing meeting is not whether it is useful today but whether we would invent it now, knowing what we know. I suspect that the answer, surprisingly often, would be no, and that we continue less out of conviction than out of a reluctance to be the one who asks.
