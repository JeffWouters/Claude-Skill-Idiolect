# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-03

## The passage (by the author)

At last I said, “You have told me what you did before you concluded to reform. I am curious to know how, in those days, you looked at things. Was there anything which you wouldn’t have done, not because you were afraid of the law, but because you felt it would be wrong?”

“Yes,” he said, “there is one thing I never would do, because it always seemed low down. I never would steal.”

It was evident that further discussion would be unprofitable without definition of terms. I found that by stealing he meant petty larceny, which he abhorred. In our condemnation of the sneak thief and the pickpocket we were on common ground. His feeling of reprobation was, if anything, more intense than that which I felt at the time. He alluded to the umbrellas and other portable articles he had noticed in the hallway. Any one who would take advantage of an unsuspecting householder by purloining such things was a degenerate. He had no dealings with such moral imbeciles.

It seemed to me that I might press the analogy which instantly occurred to me between “stealing” and forgery.

“Do they not,” I said, “seem to you to amount to very much the same thing?”

I had struck a wrong note. Analogies are ticklish things to handle, for things which are alike in certain respects are apt to be quite different in other respects. His mind was intent on the differences. The sneak thief, he told me, is a vulgar fellow of no education. The forger and the check-raiser are experts. They are playing a game. Their wits are pitted against the wits of the men who are paid high salaries for detecting them. They belong to quite different spheres. If we are looking for analogies we should look up and not down.

## Draft A

I once had a long talk with a man who had been a forger and had since reformed. His repentance was genuine, but what interested me was his former conscience, for he had one, and it was considerably more exacting than I had anticipated.

He entertained a profound contempt for petty theft. The sneak who lifts a purse from a coat pocket was to him a creature beneath notice, and he spoke of such persons much as a surgeon might speak of a barber who draws teeth. I ventured to suggest that the difference was one of degree. "You would put the pickpocket and the forger in the same class?" he asked, with the air of a man who has been insulted in his profession. He would not tolerate the comparison.

The pickpocket, he explained, takes advantage of the unwary. There is no skill in it worth mentioning, and no hazard that a man of spirit would consider respectable. But the forger enters a contest. On the other side are the detectives, generously paid, thoroughly trained and furnished with every resource that money can supply; on his side there is nothing but his own ingenuity. If he wins, he has won against the best. If he loses, he has at least lost honourably.

This is all very well, and there is a certain logic in it. It is the logic of the sportsman rather than of the moralist. The injured party, whoever he may be, does not appear in the argument at all. He is merely the ground on which the game is played. I did not press the point, since my friend had already retired from the competition. But I came away with the feeling that the boundary between right and wrong is drawn in a great many places, and that every man draws it a little below himself.

## Draft B

I once spent an afternoon talking with a man who had been a forger and had given it up. He was open about his past, and what interested me most was not what he had done but what he had thought about it at the time. He had, it turned out, a very firm sense of right and wrong. It simply did not fall where most people would draw the line.

Petty theft, for instance, he despised. He spoke of it with real contempt, as something beneath him. To take a purse from a coat or a coin from a till was, in his view, a low and graceless act, the work of people with no skill and no self-respect. He would not have done it, and he made it clear that he had looked down on those who did.

I made the mistake of suggesting that a pickpocket and a forger were, after all, in the same general line of business. Both took what was not theirs. He rejected the comparison at once and with some heat. There was, he said, no likeness between them. The pickpocket preyed on the careless and the unlucky. The forger did something else entirely.

What the forger did, as he described it, was take part in a contest. On one side stood the forger, with his pens, his papers and his patience. On the other stood the detectives, well-paid men whose whole occupation was to catch him. It was a match of skill against skill, and he seemed to feel that the fact of their salaries made the game fair, even honourable. If they were paid to stop him and failed, that was their loss, not a wrong he had done anyone.

I do not think he was trying to excuse himself when he said all this. He had plainly believed it, and believed it for years. That was the unsettling part. His conscience had been working the whole time, carefully and consistently. It had only been pointed in the wrong direction.

## Draft C

I once had a long talk with a man who had been a forger, and who had, as he assured me, given up the profession. I had supposed that a man who had lived outside the law would have no very nice distinctions as to what was right and what was wrong. I was mistaken. He had a code, and he held to it with a strictness that would have done credit to a deacon.

I asked him, in the course of our conversation, whether there was anything he had considered really wrong in the days of his activity. He answered promptly that there was. He had always despised petty theft. The man who took a purse or a watch was, in his eyes, a low fellow, beneath the notice of a gentleman.

I ventured to suggest that, after all, there might be some resemblance between the pickpocket and the forger. Both, I said, were engaged in getting other people's money without their consent. He would not hear of it. The comparison, he said, was entirely unfair. There was no more likeness between the two than between a sneak and a soldier.

Then he explained his point of view. The pickpocket took advantage of the unwary. He preyed upon people who were not expecting him and could not defend themselves. But the forger was engaged in a contest of skill. On the other side were the banks, with all their resources, and the detectives, who were well paid to catch him. It was a fair fight, and the odds were against him. If he won, it was because he was the cleverer man.

I confess that I found the argument hard to answer on its own ground. He had simply taken the moral judgment that belongs to honest work and transferred it to his own trade. It was the pride of the artist, and it kept him, in his own estimation, a respectable member of society.
