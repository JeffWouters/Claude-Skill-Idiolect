# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-05

## The passage (by the author)

I cannot explain to an unsympathetic reader why it was that we found life in our dusty little metropolis so charming, and why it was that we felt such pity for those who had never experienced the delights of our environment. Nor can I justify to such a reader the impulse which led a woman whose husband had died far away in New England to bring his body back to be laid to rest in the bare little cemetery amid the sage brush.

“It’s not such a homelike country as the other,” I ventured.

“No,” she answered, “it isn’t, but he liked it.”

And so did we all; and the liking was not the less real because it was an acquired taste. There was nothing in it akin to serious public spirit. It was a whimsical liking, like that of Touchstone for Audrey,--“An ill-favoured thing, sir, but mine own; a poor humour of mine, sir, to take that that no man else will.”

When several thousand people, set down in the midst of a howling wilderness, tacitly agree to consider it as the garden of the Lord, they can do much. It pleases the ephemeral community to make believe that it is permanent. The camp organizes itself into a city, with all the offices and dignities appertaining thereto. Civilization is extemporized like a game of dumb crambo. It amuses the citizens to see their beloved city going about in institutions several sizes too large for it. Nothing is taken literally. Humor is accepted not as a private possession, but as a public trust, and cultivated in a spirit of generous coöperation.

In the town were men whose education and experience had been in the great world. There were mine superintendents who a little while ago might have been in Germany or Cornwall; there were assayers and engineers fresh from the great technical schools, and “experts” full of geological lore. The mines were as rich in litigation as in silver, and there were lawyers great and small.

## Draft A

It is easy to love a place that is lovely. The dusty frontier boomtown I have in mind was not lovely, and the people who lived in it knew as much, yet I have seldom seen a community more attached to its surroundings. The attachment was not an accident. It was a matter of choice, and like most choices made against the evidence, it was held with a good deal of conviction.

I remember a widow in the town whose decision about her husband's burial [example needed about what she chose and why] told me more about the place than any of its boosters ever managed to. She could have chosen otherwise; nobody would have thought the worse of her for it. I have always thought her choice a better civic tribute than most of the speeches I have heard since.

The town itself was a temporary camp, and everybody knew it. But it did not behave like one. It conducted itself, in all its public dealings, as though it were a great and lasting city which had been temporarily inconvenienced by a shortage of buildings, of history and of almost everything else that a city is commonly supposed to require. Civic occasions were observed with a gravity that would not have disgraced an ancient capital. Whenever the solemnity threatened to become oppressive, somebody could be relied upon to puncture it.

For the humour of the place was a common possession. It was not the private wit of a few clever men, hoarded for their own amusement, but something that belonged to the whole population and was spent freely in public. This was the more remarkable because a surprising number of the inhabitants were educated professional men who could have been expected to take themselves seriously. It was perhaps precisely because they were educated that they did not. They had seen the grand cities and knew very well what they were imitating. The imitation was the joke, and everybody was in on it.

## Draft B

It is hard to explain to anyone who never lived there why people grew so fond of that dusty frontier boomtown. It was not a comfortable place. The ground was bare, the weather was hard, and very little about it would recommend itself to a stranger stepping off the stage. Yet the people who stayed became attached to it, and they did so by choice. Nobody was obliged to remain.

The clearest proof I know of this is a small one. A widow, when the time came to decide where her husband should be buried, chose to bury him there, in that harsh ground, rather than send him back to wherever he had come from. [example needed: any further detail of the widow's decision] It was a quiet statement that this was home now, and meant to be home for good.

That was part of the charm and part of the comedy of the place. It was a temporary camp, and everyone knew it was temporary. Mining towns rise fast and fall faster. But it behaved as though it were a grand and lasting city. It had its civic affairs, its public occasions, its sense of its own dignity, all conducted with a seriousness that was never quite serious. The residents played at being a great municipality, and they knew they were playing, and the game was the better for it.

The humor there was not a private thing kept for one's own household. It was shared, and it was communal. A joke made in the street belonged to the whole town by evening. People laughed together at the town's pretensions and at their own, and the laughter seemed to bind them more than any charter could have done.

It helped that so many of the residents were educated people. Among the miners and traders there were a good number of professional men, trained in one field or another, who had come out for the same reasons as everyone else. They brought with them a turn of mind that enjoyed irony, and they gave the town's playful civic life a wit it might otherwise have lacked. I think of that place still with more affection than it had any right to earn.

## Draft C

It is not easy to explain to one who has never lived in a frontier town how people could grow fond of it. The town was dusty; it was hot; there was not a tree within [example needed] miles. Nature had done nothing to make it attractive, and man had done very little more. And yet people did grow fond of it, and not because they had to. They chose it.

I remember a widow whose husband had died there. She might easily have taken his body back to the old home in the East, where there were green fields and a churchyard with his people in it. Everybody expected her to do so. But she decided to bury him in the town. He had liked it, she said, and so did she. That was all the explanation she gave, and it was enough. It was the kind of loyalty which a place can inspire only when people have entered into its life.

The town was, of course, a temporary camp. Everybody knew that it might be deserted in a year. But nobody admitted it. It was laid out on the scale of a great metropolis. There were broad avenues which ended abruptly in the desert, and building lots which were sold as if they were in the heart of a capital. There was a mayor and a council, and public questions were debated with all the gravity of a legislature. It was a city in everything but permanence, and we did not allow permanence to count.

What made it all tolerable was the humor. It was not the private joke of one man at the expense of another; it belonged to the whole community. Everybody saw the absurdity of the situation, and everybody enjoyed it. The pretensions of the place were a kind of game in which all took part.

It should be said that there was a surprising number of educated men among us. There were lawyers and doctors and engineers, men who had been to college and had read books. They brought with them a lightness of touch which made the civic life of the town a thing to remember.
