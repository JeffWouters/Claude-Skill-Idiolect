# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-04

## The passage (by the author)

Are we really, at this late day, going to be duped by the mid-century fallacy that “plain living and high thinking” are a natural combination? Even if Shakespeare at New Place teaches us nothing, we cannot fail to be impressed by the memory of Thoreau, stealing home from Lake Walden by dark, to provide himself secretly with better fare than the woods afforded. As if, indeed, any one who had tried plain living did not know that high thinking was done, if at all, in spite of it! “The hand of little employment hath the daintier sense,” as Shakespeare long since said. Let us open our own front doors, polish our own shoes, dust our own bibelots, and make messes on a chafing-dish when the cook is out; and let us do it gallantly. But let us not pretend that it is more civilized to do these things ourselves than to have them skilfully done for us. The prince in disguise makes the most charming beggar in the world, no doubt; but that is because--as all fairy-tales from the beginning of time have taught us--the prince wears his rags as if they were purple. And, to do that, he not only must once have worn purple, but must never forget the purple that he has worn. And to the argument that all cannot wear purple, I can, as I say, only reply that that seems to me to be no reason why all should wear rags.

Until every one is too good to be a parlor-maid, let us open our own doors, if we must--provided we do it according to the great tradition of door-opening; but how can we do it according to the great tradition if we abolish parlor-maids and dry up the fount of the great tradition? And, whatever the simplifiers say, there is no doubt that, as yet, there are, to one person who is too good for door-opening, ten persons who are by no means good enough for it. I have never been able to imagine just how the sound of the Last Trump is going to shiver the aristocracy of earth into the democracy of Heaven. To be sure, it is not my affair. But at least one can have, this side the grave, little patience with the altruisms of the Procrusteans. They merely wish to make each of us an incompetent Jack-at-all-trades. And one had thought the German universities, if they had done nothing else, had blown that bubble!

## Draft A

"Plain living and high thinking" is a very pretty phrase, and I am sure it has comforted a great many people who could not afford anything else. But it has come, in some quarters, to mean something more than a consolation; it has come to be a creed. We are asked to believe that the woman who washes her own dishes thinks nobler thoughts over the dishpan than the woman who has them washed for her. I do not believe it. I have washed a good many dishes, and I cannot remember a single lofty thought that came to me in the process.

Let us grant, by all means, that there is no shame in doing one's own work. It is honest and it is often necessary; there are times when it is even pleasant. What I deny is that it is more civilized. Civilization, if it means anything, means that we need not all do everything--that some of us may be spared the chores so that the chores may be done well, and the other things done at all. To go back to doing everything oneself is not to go forward. It is simply to go back.

There is another thing the plain-livers forget. The graceful traditions of a household--the way a table is laid, the way a guest is received, the hundred small courtesies that make a house a home and not a shelter--do not keep themselves alive. Somebody has to keep them. They live in the hands of people who know them and practise them daily, and a great many of those people have been servants. When the cook and the parlour-maid go, a good deal more goes with them than the cooking and the dusting. A whole body of knowledge goes, and I have not noticed that the high thinkers are in any hurry to learn it.

For the end of all this levelling is not a nation of philosophers; it is a nation of amateurs. If every one is to do his own laundering and his own carpentry and his own nursing, then every one will do all of them badly. No one will have time to do any one thing well--least of all to think. It is a strange ideal that would make us all indifferent cooks in order that we may all be indifferent scholars. I, for one, would rather have a good cook in the kitchen and a good book in my hand; and I cannot see that either the cook or the book is the worse for the arrangement.

## Draft B

We have all heard the phrase "plain living and high thinking," and most of us have heard it used as if the one produced the other. I have never been able to see why. That a man should black his own boots is entirely his affair; there is nothing disgraceful in it, and a good deal that is sensible. But it is not more civilized than having them blacked. The hand that wields the brush is not thereby ennobled, and the mind above it is not thereby set free. On the contrary, the mind is, for the moment, pre-occupied with boots. A woman may spend a whole morning over the grate and emerge, perhaps, with a cleaner grate, but she will hardly come away with a loftier view of the universe than she took into it; and I do not see why any one should expect her to. Frugality may be a necessity or a discipline; it is not, of itself, a school of thought.

Consider what the graceful traditions of living actually require. A well-kept house, a table properly laid, a garden that is something more than a patch of cabbages--these do not maintain themselves. They need people who know how to keep them, and who have given years to learning it. Some of those people are the owners; many of them, to be sure, are servants. The butler who knows how the thing ought to be done is as much a custodian of civilization as the scholar who knows how a Greek verb ought to be conjugated--rather more, perhaps, since fewer people will miss the verb. Do away with him in the name of simplicity, and you do not simplify the tradition. You end it.

Some one will say that the tradition is not worth keeping if it costs another man his independence. I do not think the case is so simple, but let it pass. My point is only this: when every one does everything, no one does anything well. The levelling-down that is so much admired produces a nation of amateurs--amateur cooks, amateur gardeners, amateur carpenters, and, in the end, amateur thinkers, since the thinking must be done in whatever hour is left over from the scrubbing. It isn't high thinking that follows plain living. It is tired thinking. That is a very different thing.

## Draft C

There is an old and flattering idea that plain living produces high thinking. Strip life down, the argument runs, do your own chores, want little and own less, and the mind will be freed for nobler things. It is an attractive idea, and I think it is mostly a myth.

I have no objection to anyone doing his own housework. Sweeping a floor, cooking a meal or mending a fence is honest labour, and there is nothing shameful in it. What I object to is the claim that it is more civilized than the alternative. A man who blacks his own boots is not thereby more refined than one who has them blacked for him. He has simply spent part of his day on boots. Whether the saved hours of the other man go to thought or to idleness is a separate question, but the chance of thought is at least there. For the man doing everything himself, it is often not.

The deeper trouble is that civilized life is not only a matter of ideas. It is also a matter of forms: the way a table is laid, the way guests are received, the way a house is kept, the small ceremonies that make ordinary days pleasant and give them a shape. These traditions are graceful, and grace of that kind does not maintain itself. It needs people who know how things are done and who do them regularly. Some of those people are the owners of the household. Many of them, historically, have been servants, whose skill and memory kept the customs alive. Remove them in the name of simplicity and the customs do not become simpler. They disappear.

Then there is the question of competence. When everyone is levelled down to doing every task for himself, nobody does any task especially well. The householder becomes a mediocre cook, a clumsy carpenter, an indifferent gardener and a hurried scholar, all in the same week. We end up with a nation of amateurs, each busy at a dozen jobs and expert at none. That is not a triumph of equality; it is a loss of skill spread evenly across everyone.

I do not say that wealth guarantees wisdom, or that servants guarantee grace. I say only that the frugal life has no special claim on lofty thought. Thought needs time, and good living needs hands that know their work. A society that disdains both in the name of plainness should not be surprised to find itself neither simpler nor wiser, but only more tired.
