# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-06

## The passage (by the author)

In regard to the lie of exaggeration, a word should perhaps parenthetically be said. We all know the child who has seen two elephants in the garden eating the roses. We also know the delightful grown-up who “embroiders” his narratives. He will never tell the same adventure twice with the same details. The fact remains that he may each time leave you with precisely the same impression of the adventure in its entirety. It is quite possible that you trust him exceedingly. Of course it is also possible that his ben trovato is never vero. You will have to determine after long experience of him whether he is fundamentally false, or merely has a sense of style. Personally, I know exaggerators of both kinds: people whose lies are only picturesque adjectives, and people whose picturesque adjectives are only lies. There is a subtle distinction between the two. At the risk of being at loggerheads with the rhetoricians, one must say that truth goes deeper than words, and that there is not much in a truthfulness which is only phrase-deep.

The old ladies who are shivering on the back porch will disapprove of me for saying these things, almost as much as I disapprove of them for being on the back porch. To speak frankly, I have not found that the people who cling to the letter are always the people who cling to the spirit of the law. Some of the men and women who will not say in so many words the thing which is not, will deliberately give a false impression. They are not the servants of truth; they are the parasites of truth. The ladies I have referred to may be technically “out”; but they are really “out” only to the undesired visitor--exactly as much as if they had stopped in their own sitting-rooms. (Remember, please, that I am not speaking of the people who receive the unwelcome caller rather than permit a maid to fib--they are in a very different case.) I should not instinctively go to these people for an accurate account of a serious situation. Any one whose conscience is satisfied with that kind of loyalty to fact knows very little about the spirit of truth.

## Draft A

I have a great deal of patience with the person who exaggerates. She comes in from her walk and tells you that she met a dog as big as a pony, and that it chased her half a mile; and you know perfectly well that the dog was an ordinary dog, and the half-mile a few yards. But you also know that she was frightened, and that the dog was not a pleasant dog; and that, after all, is what she meant you to know. The story was embellished, if you like. The picture it left in your mind was a true one.

For truth, it seems to me, has less to do with the words than with the impression. We are all too ready to think that a man has been honest if he can defend every syllable he uttered. He can defend them, very likely; but what did he make you believe? That is the question--the only question, I sometimes think. A statement may be literally accurate in every particular and still be a lie from beginning to end, if it was framed to send you away with a false notion. And a statement may be wildly inaccurate in its particulars and still be, in the sense that matters, perfectly true.

Now the exaggerator is not dangerous, because every one knows how to discount her. It is the other sort who is dangerous: the careful person who never tells an outright lie. She is proud of it, and she has reason to be proud of her skill, if not of her honesty. She chooses her words as a lawyer chooses them. She lets you draw the wrong conclusion, and then--if you reproach her--reminds you that she never said anything of the kind. Nor did she. She only arranged that you should think it.

For my part, I find that I trust her less than any one I know. With the teller of tall tales I always know where I am; I have only to divide by three. With the technically truthful I never know where I am at all. (It is always the technically truthful who are most shocked by a fib!) And I would rather, on the whole, be told that the dog was as big as a pony.

## Draft B

There is a man in every club--one hopes there is only one--who cannot tell a story without improving it. The fish grows in the telling; the insolent waiter becomes more insolent; the dinner-party at which he said the clever thing becomes a dinner-party at which every one fell silent to hear it. We laugh at him, and we are right to laugh. But I am not at all sure that we are right to call him a liar. Very often, when he has done embellishing, the picture he leaves is the true one: the waiter really was insolent, and the remark really was clever. He has falsified the details and got the whole thing right.

For truth, after all, concerns the impression given, not merely the words used. A statement may be accurate in every syllable and yet leave the hearer believing something false; a statement may be loose in every particular and yet leave him believing something true. Which is the honest one? The literalist says the first. I venture to say the second, and I think most people, if they considered the matter, would agree. It is rather a question of what one means to leave behind in the other man's mind, and whether, if he could see the whole matter as one sees it oneself, he would feel that he had been dealt with fairly or merely dealt with cleverly.

This brings us to a far more dangerous person than the story-teller: the man who never tells a lie. He is very proud of it. He will mislead you by omission, by emphasis, by a careful choice of which true things to say--but he will not, on his honour, say anything that is not so. [example needed: a technically true deception] Granted, his words will bear inspection. His meaning won't. I would trust the embellisher sooner; at least one knows where his colouring lies, and can allow for it. The other man's deceptions are built to pass inspection, and that is precisely what makes them deceptions. Of the two, he is the less trustworthy. He is also, I fancy, the more respected.

## Draft C

We are usually taught that truthfulness is a matter of words: say only what is so, and you have been honest. This is a useful rule for children. For adults it is not quite enough, and in some cases it points the wrong way.

Take the person who embellishes a story. He tells of an evening that went badly and makes it a little worse than it was, or of a journey that was difficult and adds an obstacle or two. Strictly, he has said things that are not true. Yet his listeners come away with a fair idea of what happened: the evening really was a disaster, the journey really was hard. The details have been heightened, but the picture is accurate. I do not recommend the habit, and it can certainly be carried too far. But it is hard to call it a serious offence against the truth when the truth is, in the end, what it conveys.

Now take the opposite case. Here is a person who never says anything literally false. He chooses his words with care, answers exactly the question asked and no other, and leaves out whatever would change your mind. He lets you draw a conclusion he knows to be wrong, and when you discover your error he points out that he never said what you believed. Every sentence he spoke will bear inspection. The impression he left will not.

The comparison suggests that truth is not a property of sentences taken one at a time. It concerns the impression one person creates in another's mind. If I set out to make you believe something false, I have deceived you, whether I did it with a lie or with a careful arrangement of facts. If I make you believe something true, I have not deceived you, even if some of my words were loose.

This is why I find the scrupulously literal deceiver less trustworthy than the storyteller who exaggerates. The storyteller's lapses are open and generally harmless; one learns to discount them. The literalist's are hidden, and they are deliberate. He has found a way to mislead while keeping his own record clean, and he will do it again, because he does not think he has done anything wrong. That confidence is precisely what makes him dangerous.
