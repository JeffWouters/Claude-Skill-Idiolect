## The passage (by the author)

Where do deadlines actually come from? The question may seem odd, since most deadlines arrive with an air of inevitability, as though they had been imposed by the nature of things rather than decided by particular people for particular reasons. Some are genuinely external — a regulatory requirement, a contractual obligation, an event that will happen whether or not the software is ready — but a considerable proportion are, in some sense, invented; they emerge from planning conversations, from the desire to have something to announce, from a general feeling that work without a date attached is work that will never be finished. I do not say this to dismiss them, since invention is not the same thing as arbitrariness. It may be that invented deadlines serve a real purpose, concentrating attention and forcing decisions that would otherwise be postponed indefinitely; the difficulty is that we so rarely distinguish between the invented and the unavoidable, and we therefore tend to treat all of them with the same anxious reverence.

Consider what happens to a team as a deadline approaches. There is often a gradual narrowing of attention, in which everything not directly connected to the immediate goal begins to seem like a distraction; conversations become shorter, decisions become quicker, and the careful deliberation that characterised the early stages of the project gives way to a more pragmatic, sometimes more ruthless, style of working. Some of this is healthy — the pressure clarifies priorities and dissolves the kind of endless refinement that can paralyse a project. Some of it, arguably, is not; corners are cut that will have to be repaired later, people work hours that they would never accept in calmer periods, and the quality of judgement declines precisely when the stakes are highest. Who decides whether the trade-off is worth making, and on what basis? In my experience the decision is almost never made explicitly; it simply happens, as the logic of the deadline asserts itself.

## Draft A

It is worth asking, now and then, where a particular due date actually came from. Some dates are genuinely imposed from outside: a regulation takes effect, a contract specifies a delivery, an event will happen whether or not the work is ready. Many others, though, were chosen, sometimes carefully and sometimes almost casually, by people inside the organisation who needed something to plan around. What strikes me is how rarely this distinction survives contact with the team. By the time a date reaches the people doing the work, it tends to arrive with the same weight whatever its origin, and it is met with the same low-level dread whether it was dictated by law or picked because it fell at the end of a quarter.

The effect of an approaching date on a team is, I think, genuinely double-edged. There is no denying that a deadline can sharpen attention in a way that little else does; decisions that had been deferred for weeks are suddenly made, and work that had been drifting acquires a clear shape. But the same pressure that produces focus also invites shortcuts. Tests are skipped, reviews are hurried, known problems are set aside to be dealt with later, and people begin to work evenings and weekends in a way that feels temporary but often is not. The date is met, perhaps, and the cost is paid afterwards, in defects and in tiredness that accumulates quietly.

What seems to me most troubling is not that this cost exists but that nobody openly decides whether it is worth paying. The trade-off between meeting a date and preserving the quality of the work, or the wellbeing of the people doing it, is real and sometimes perfectly defensible. Yet it is almost never made explicitly. It is made instead by default, one shortcut at a time, by people who assume that the date is not theirs to question. Perhaps the least we could do is to say aloud, before the pressure mounts, how much we are prepared to spend.

## Draft B

Where do the due dates that shape so much of a team's working life actually come from? It may be that we rarely ask, because by the time a date reaches the people who must meet it, its origin has usually been forgotten and only its authority remains.

Some dates are genuinely imposed from outside, by a customer, a regulation or an event that will not wait for anyone; many more, I suspect, are simply chosen, by someone who needed a number for a plan and picked one that seemed reasonable at the time. The curious thing is that both kinds tend to be received with the same dread. A team rarely distinguishes between a date that cannot move and a date that merely has not yet been moved, and so it treats every deadline as though the consequences of missing it were equally severe.

There is also the question of what an approaching date does to the people working towards it. You might say, with some justice, that it sharpens attention, since a deadline has a way of clarifying which tasks truly matter and which can be quietly set aside. The same pressure, however, invites shortcuts that would not otherwise be taken, and it encourages long hours that feel heroic in the moment and considerably less so afterwards; what is gained in focus is, arguably, paid for in quality and in the energy of the team.

It seems to me that the difficulty lies less in the existence of deadlines than in the silence that surrounds their cost. Somebody ought to decide, openly and deliberately, whether the shortcuts and the overwork that a particular date will demand are a price worth paying, yet in most organisations nobody does, and the cost is simply absorbed by whoever happens to be closest to the work. Perhaps a date deserves our dread only when someone has been willing to say aloud what it is worth.

## Draft C

Every team works against due dates, but it is worth asking where those dates actually come from. Some are genuinely imposed from outside. A regulation takes effect, a contract specifies a delivery, an external event will happen whether the team is ready or not. These dates are real constraints, and missing them has real consequences.

Many other dates are chosen. Someone inside the organisation picked them, perhaps to create momentum, perhaps to align with a plan, perhaps simply because a date was needed and that one seemed reasonable. There is nothing wrong with choosing dates. The problem is that once they exist, teams treat them exactly like the imposed ones. A self-set target and a legal deadline produce the same dread, the same late nights, the same sense that failure is not an option. The origin of the date is forgotten, and only its weight remains. That weight has effects, and not all of them are bad. An approaching date sharpens focus. It forces decisions that might otherwise drift, cuts debate short, and makes people concentrate on what really matters. Plenty of teams do some of their clearest work in the run-up to a deadline.

But the same pressure invites shortcuts. Tests get skipped, reviews get rushed, known problems get pushed to later. It also invites overwork: long hours that feel necessary in the moment and leave people drained afterwards. These costs do not disappear when the date passes. They show up later as defects, as fragile code, and as tired people who are slower for weeks.

What is striking is that nobody openly decides whether that cost is acceptable. No one sits down and says that this particular date is worth this much risk and this much exhaustion. The trade-off is made implicitly, by default, one late evening at a time. For an imposed date, the cost may well be worth it. For a chosen date, it is at least worth asking the question out loud.
