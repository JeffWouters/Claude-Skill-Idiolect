# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-07

## The passage (by the author)

Happening, by our close proximity, to turn to us the first time in the shop that he required attention, upon each succeeding visit he sought out us to attend to his wishes. The position of retail salesman "on the floor" is one completely exposed to every human attitude and humour. Against arrogance, against contempt of himself as a shop person, a species of "counter-jumper," against irascibility, against bigoted ignorance, against an indissoluble assumption, perhaps logical, that he is of inferior mentality, this factotum has no defence. His very business is to meet all with amenity. It is his daily portion, included in the material with which he works.

It (he finds) injures him not, essentially; it ceases to particularly affect him, beyond his inward appraisement of the character before him. Toward him one acts simply in accordance with the instincts of one's nature. His status counsels no constraint, invites no display, has no property of stimulation. Thus the view of a famous man's character from the position of retail clerk is valuable. Mr. James's manner with Mr. Brownell would hardly be the same as toward us. But it was, exactly. There was present in his mind at the moment, was quite apparent, absolutely no consciousness of any distance of mind, or position, between him and us. He sought conversation (any suggestion of so equalising a thing as conversation with a clerk is not uncommonly repressed by the important as preposterous). In his own talk with us, he seemed to us to be a man consciously striving with the material of words and sentences to express his thought as well as he could.

He was very earnest. He looked up at us constantly (we are a little tall) with fixed concentration of gaze, and moved his hand to and fro as though seeking to balance his ideas. He asked questions with deference. Among other things, he desired very much to know what per cent. of the novels on the fiction table was the product of writers in England. "I live in England myself," he said, very simply, "and I am curious to know this." He expressed a little impatience at the measureless flood of mediocre fiction, making a fluttering gesture conveying a sense of impotence to give it attention. He barely glanced at the pile of his own book, and did not mention it. He did not seem at first (though we believe later he changed this opinion) to think highly of Arnold Bennett (this was at the first bloom of Mr. Bennett's vogue here), nor to have read him. "Oh, yes, yes; he is an English journalist," in a tone as though, merely a journalist. Clear artist in fibre. When he took his departure he bade us "Good day," and lifted his hat.

## Draft A

If you wish to know what a person is really like, ask the person who has sold him something. I do not mean his friends. His friends see him at his best, or at least at his most careful. I mean the girl behind the glove counter, the man at the ticket window, the clerk in the bookshop. These people see him when he does not think it matters how he behaves.

It is part of the business of selling things to be spoken to rudely. You are expected to take it. You do take it. You are spoken to sharply, and you are spoken to impatiently, and you are spoken to as if you were not quite there, as if you were a part of the counter that happened to be able to fetch things down from a shelf. All this you take, day in and day out, with a pleasant face. And after a while, taking it, you come to have a pretty clear notion of people. You come to know who is a gentleman and who is not, and you find, oftener than you might think, that it is not always the one you would have picked from his clothes.

So when I say that the novelist was courteous to me I do not say it lightly. He spoke to me as one man speaks to another. There was nothing of the great man about him. There was nothing, either, of that other manner, worse in its way, of the great man who is being very kind to the clerk. He was simply in earnest. He asked me questions, and he waited for my answers, and he listened to them, as if they were answers that it was worth a man's while to hear. He was, in the plain sense of the word, polite. I do not know that I have met with anything quite like it before or since, across a counter.

What he wanted to know about was the trade. How much of what we sold, he asked, was British fiction? What share of the stock? He wanted, it seemed, to get at the figures. I told him what I could, which was [example needed]. He took it in thoughtfully, and nodded, and seemed to turn it over.

Then I mentioned, I forget in what connection, a certain very popular novelist, a writer whose books at that time we could hardly keep upon the tables. I thought he would be interested. He was not interested. He dismissed him. He did it quite without heat, in a sentence, as a man might brush a crumb off his sleeve. It was not jealousy; I am sure of that. It was simply that, so far as he was concerned, there was nothing there to talk about. And then he went back, very earnestly, to the question of British fiction.

## Draft B

Anyone who has stood behind a counter knows that selling is, among other things, a lesson in taking rudeness without answering it. The customer may be curt, impatient, condescending or openly contemptuous, and the person serving him must smile and fetch the next book. It is part of the job, and after a while one stops resenting it. But it has a curious side effect. A salesman sees people as they are when they believe it does not matter how they behave. Nobody puts on his best manners for a shop clerk unless the manners are really his. So the clerk's view of human character, though narrow, is unusually honest, and I have come to trust it more than most.

That is why I think it worth recording how the novelist treated the assistant who served him. He had every reason, by the usual standards, to treat him as furniture. He was a famous man, and the clerk was nobody, a young man in a shop whose name he did not know and would not need to know. Plenty of lesser people had made that distinction plain across the same counter.

He did nothing of the kind. He spoke to the clerk as one man speaks to another, as an equal, without any sign that he was conferring a favour by doing so. He was earnest; he seemed genuinely to want to know what the young man thought, and he listened to the answers. And he was polite, not with the careful politeness some people use to mark a distance, but in a plain, natural way, as if it had simply never occurred to him to be anything else. [Example of his words or manner needed.] I have served a good many customers of much less consequence who could not manage half as much.

His questions, too, were of a kind one does not often hear. He wanted to know what share of the shop's stock of fiction was British. It was not idle curiosity; he seemed to want a real figure, or at least a real impression, and he pressed the point until he had one. [The clerk's answer needed.] He did not explain why he wanted to know, and the clerk did not ask.

Then the conversation turned to a rival, a writer who was very popular at the time. [Rival's name needed.] The clerk may have mentioned him, or the novelist may have raised him; either way, he disposed of the man briefly and without much ceremony. It was a dismissal, not an argument. He did not seem angry or jealous, only unconvinced that there was much to discuss, and he moved on.

It is a small thing, the way a famous man speaks to a clerk. But small things, seen from behind a counter, are often the ones that tell the truth.

## Draft C

It is a commonplace that you may learn a good deal about a man by watching how he treats those who can do nothing for him. The shop assistant is in an excellent position to make observations of this kind, and he makes them, whether he intends to or not. He is, by the nature of his employment, obliged to take whatever is handed to him across the counter: impatience, condescension, bad temper, and that particular brand of rudeness which some people reserve for those who are paid to be civil to them. He may not answer back; he must smile, and wrap the parcel, and say that it is no trouble at all. And so, in the course of a few years, he comes to possess a knowledge of human character that is, I think, rather more reliable than most. People are not on their guard with him. Why should they be? He does not count.

It is for this reason that I have always attached some importance to the manner of the novelist toward me. He did not treat me as a person who did not count. He treated me, and I can put it no other way, as an equal. He spoke to me earnestly, as though what I thought were really a matter of some interest to him; and he spoke to me politely, with a kind of grave and deliberate courtesy that was not, I am convinced, put on for the occasion. It was simply the way he was. I have been spoken to by a good many distinguished persons in my time, and by a great many more who believed themselves distinguished, and I can say that the difference between the two classes is very often precisely this.

Our conversation was not, by any standard, a momentous one. He wished to know what proportion of the fiction we carried was British. He put the question with care, and he listened to the answer with care, as though it were a matter of genuine concern to him, as perhaps it was. I told him what I could [number needed: the proportion given]; he considered it for a while in silence.

Then the talk turned to a certain popular writer of the day [name needed: the rival novelist]. He disposed of the writer in a sentence [quote needed: his dismissal], with no heat whatever, as a man might remove a small object from a table. It was not malice; it was not even, I think, contempt. It was more in the nature of a verdict, delivered by a judge who had long since heard the evidence and did not propose to hear it again.

I did not argue with him; it would not have occurred to me. But I've remembered the verdict, and I have remembered still more the courtesy with which the question before it was put.
