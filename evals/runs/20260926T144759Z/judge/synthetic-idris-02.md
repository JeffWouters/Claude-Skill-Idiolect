## The passage (by the author)

Why is it that we continue to ask for estimates when so few of us believe in them? The question is not entirely rhetorical, since the practice of estimation survives in almost every software organisation despite a long and well-documented history of disappointment, and the persistence of a practice that consistently fails usually suggests that it is doing something other than what it claims to do. Arguably the estimate is not a prediction at all; it is a negotiation conducted in the vocabulary of prediction, a way of reconciling what the business wants with what the engineers fear, in which both parties agree to pretend that the resulting figure describes the future. I suspect most experienced engineers understand this intuitively, even if they would not articulate it in quite those terms. The difficulty arises when the pretence is forgotten — when the negotiated figure hardens into a commitment, and the commitment becomes a measure by which people are judged.

It may be helpful to consider what we are actually doing when we produce an estimate for a piece of software work. We are imagining a sequence of activities that has not yet happened, in a system we only partially understand, subject to interruptions we cannot foresee, and we are compressing all of that uncertainty into a single figure that must fit neatly into a planning document. The act is, in some sense, heroic; it asks the imagination to perform a task for which it is poorly equipped, and it rewards confidence over accuracy. Is it any wonder that the results are unreliable? What is more surprising, perhaps, is how rarely the unreliability is acknowledged in the planning process itself, as though each new estimate were an entirely fresh attempt rather than the latest entry in a long series of similar misjudgements.

## Draft A

What is a software team really doing when it is asked, yet again, how long something will take? It may be that the estimate was never meant to be a forecast at all, but a kind of negotiation between what the business hopes for and what the engineers are worried about, conducted in the language of days and weeks because that language sounds considerably more neutral than either hope or anxiety; I suspect most people in the room understand this, even if nobody says it aloud.

The negotiation is harmless enough while everyone remembers that it is a negotiation. The trouble begins once the guess becomes a promise, and the promise becomes something against which people are quietly judged; at that point the number stops describing the work and starts describing the engineer, who will naturally learn to pad it, defend it or, arguably worst of all, stop offering it honestly. You may have seen a team in which estimates grow a little larger each quarter without the work growing at all, and wondered where the additional time disappeared to.

There is also the question of memory, which planning rarely touches. Planning sessions seldom begin by asking how often the previous round of guesses turned out to be accurate, perhaps because the answer would be uncomfortable for everyone involved, and so each new estimate is produced as though the earlier ones had never missed. It is a curious form of optimism to keep asking a question whose answers we have so rarely examined.

It seems to me that teams might learn more by revisiting their estimates than by producing new ones, since the record of past misses is the only honest evidence they possess about how uncertain their work really is. Perhaps the most useful estimate is not the one that proves right but the one that makes its own uncertainty visible.

## Draft B

Few rituals in software work are as persistent, or as quietly disappointing, as the time estimate. We ask for them at the start of almost every piece of work, we record them with considerable care, and we are then surprised, again and again, when they turn out to have been wrong. It is tempting to treat this as a failure of skill, as though engineers simply needed to become better at predicting the future. I am increasingly inclined to think that the estimate was never really a prediction at all. It is better understood as a negotiation, conducted in the language of numbers, between what the business hopes will be possible and what the engineers worry might go wrong. Each side arrives with its own anxieties, and the figure that emerges is less a forecast than a truce.

Truces of this kind are not in themselves harmful. The difficulty begins at the moment when the guess is quietly converted into a promise, and the people who offered it find that they are now being judged against it. A number that was spoken tentatively, perhaps with a caveat or two, acquires a firmness it was never meant to carry, and the conversation shifts from what we are learning about the work to why we have fallen behind. Once that happens, everyone involved has an incentive to pad their guesses or to defend them past the point of reason, and the estimate becomes still less useful than it was.

What strikes me most, though, is how rarely planning acknowledges the history of its own misses. We could, if we chose, look back at how often past guesses held and allow that record to temper the next one. We seldom do. Perhaps this is because such an admission would expose the negotiation for what it is, and it is more comfortable to go on believing that this time the number will be right.

## Draft C

Software teams ask for estimates constantly, and they are wrong far more often than anyone likes to admit. Yet the asking never stops. That is less strange than it looks, because an estimate is rarely just a prediction. It is a negotiation.

On one side sit the hopes of the business: a launch that lands before a competitor, a feature ready for the next sales cycle, a budget that holds. On the other side sit the worries of the engineers: the legacy code nobody fully understands, the integration that has surprised them before, the requirements that will certainly shift. The number that comes out of an estimation session is the point where those two pressures meet. It says as much about who pushed harder as it does about the work.

That on its own would be manageable. A negotiated guess is still a useful input, as long as everyone treats it as a guess. The trouble begins when it quietly turns into a promise. The number appears on a roadmap, then in a status report, then in someone's performance conversation. Now people are being judged against it. At that point engineers learn to pad, managers learn to squeeze, and the estimate stops carrying information at all.

What makes this cycle so durable is that planning seldom looks back. Teams rarely sit down and compare last quarter's estimates with what actually happened. If they did, they would see how often the guesses missed and by how much, and they could plan with that in mind. Instead each new round of estimation starts fresh, as if the track record did not exist. [example needed]

So the requests keep coming, not because estimates have proven reliable, but because they serve a purpose other than accuracy. They give both sides something to agree on. The cost is that everyone ends up defending a number that was never meant to carry that much weight.
