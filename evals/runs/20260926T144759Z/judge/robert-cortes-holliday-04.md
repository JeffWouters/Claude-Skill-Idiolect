# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-04

## The passage (by the author)

Succeeding visits caused us to suspect that Mr. James's ideas of the machinery of business are somewhat naive. He seemed to regard us as, so to say, the whole works. It entered our head that maybe Mr. James thought we received and answered all manner of correspondence, editorial as well as that connected with the retail business, opened up in the morning, read, accepted, and rejected manuscript, nailed up boxes for shipment, swept out the shop, and were acquainted perfectly with all confidential matters of the House. "I wrote you" (us), "you know," he said. And he referred by the way, apparently upon the assumption that the matter had been laid before us, to business of which we could not possibly have cognizance. And then he desired to send some books. Fumbling in his breast pocket, he produced a letter, from which he read aloud a list of his own works apparently requested of him. Carefully replacing his letter, he said: "I should like to send these books to my sister-in-law." With that he started out.

Now, it was not a difficult problem to assume that this could be no other than Mrs. William James, still, it is customary for purchasers to state the name of the person to whom goods are to go, and many people are sceptical that the salesman has it down right even then. "Your sister-in-law, Mr. James, is------?" we suggested. "Oh, yes, of course--of course; Mrs. William James; of course--of course," Mr. James said. Now, certainly, he supposed (it was evident) he had got finally settled a difficult and complicated piece of business. Mrs. William James's regular address we might reasonably infer. Still it might be that she was at the moment somewhere else, on a visit. It were better to have Mr. James give his order in the regular way. "And the address?" we mentioned. "Oh, yes--oh, yes; of course--of course," Mr. James said apologetically. Then, pausing a moment to see if there was anything more in this bewildering labyrinth of details to such a complex transaction, he departed, taking, as he drew away, his hat, as Mrs. Nickleby says, "completely off."

## Draft A

It was on one occasion that the novelist decided to place an order, and I shall not soon forget the manner in which he did it. He approached the business, as he approached most things, from a considerable distance and by a series of gradual and deliberate stages.

He appeared to be under the impression that the assistant before him constituted, in his single person, the entire organisation of the firm. I was, it seemed, the salesman, the bookkeeper, the shipping department, the correspondence clerk and very possibly the proprietor. He addressed me accordingly, with instructions that touched upon every branch of the establishment at once, and I had not the heart, nor indeed the opportunity, to correct him. One does not interrupt a long sentence of that sort; one waits, with a pencil, for it to arrive somewhere.

What he wanted, when at length it emerged from among the digressions, was that copies of certain of his own books should be sent by mail to his brother's wife; this much was clear, and it was only the rest that was difficult.

"You will send them, then, to my sister-in-law."

"Certainly. And the name?"

"The name. Yes. Her name, of course."

There was a pause, as though the question, while perfectly reasonable, had not previously occurred to him. Then, as from a very deep pocket, he produced the name, [name needed], and I wrote it down.

"And the address?"

"The address. Ah."

There was another pause, rather longer than the first. He regarded the ceiling; he regarded the shelves; he regarded me, with a kind of courteous perplexity, as if it were I who had mislaid it. By degrees, with patience on my side and a visible effort on his, the address was assembled, piece by piece, like a sentence of his own: [address needed]. I read it back to him, and he agreed that it was correct, and seemed, I thought, a little surprised that it should be.

I've often reflected since that there was something entirely characteristic in the whole transaction. It was not that he was careless; he was, on the contrary, most anxious that everything should be done properly. It was merely that the practical particulars of the matter, the names and streets by which the rest of us conduct our affairs, lay, so to say, on the far side of his attention, where he visited them only with reluctance.

## Draft B

He wished to order some books. That was plain enough. It was the only thing about the transaction that was plain.

It was clear, to begin with, that he believed there was only one of us. He addressed me as though I were the firm. I was, in his view, the man who took the order, and the man who kept the books, and the man who wrapped the parcel, and the man who carried it to the post, and, I dare say, the man who swept out the shop in the morning. He did not say so. But he spoke as if whatever he told me would be seen to, by me, personally, from beginning to end. It seemed a pity to disturb this idea. I did not disturb it.

The books he wanted were his own. He wanted several of them, copies of his own titles, and he wanted them sent. To whom? To his brother's wife.

"Very good," I said. "And the lady's name?"

He looked at me with a kind of mild surprise, as though I ought to have known it. After all, she was his brother's wife. Everybody must know who his brother's wife was. Presently it came out. It came out slowly, a little at a time, in the manner of a man remembering something he had not had occasion to think about for a while. Then there was the address.

"And where," I asked, "shall they be mailed?"

To his brother's wife, he said.

"Yes," I said, "but where does she live?"

This took some drawing out. It was drawn out. First there was the town, which was [example needed]. Then, after a pause, there was the street. Then, after another pause, there was the number, or something that we agreed between us would do for the number. I wrote it all down. I read it back to him. He listened with the greatest attention, and nodded, and seemed to regard the whole matter as having been settled in the simplest possible way. I think he supposed that he had given me very complete instructions. In a sense he had. It had merely taken the two of us rather a long time to find out what they were.

## Draft C

When the novelist finally came to the counter to place his order, it became clear that he had no idea how a bookshop worked, and no particular wish to learn.

He seemed to assume that the one assistant standing in front of him was the whole firm. In his mind, I think, the young man who took down the order would also find the books in the stockroom, wrap them, address the parcel, carry it to the post, and keep the accounts afterward. The firm had departments for all of these things, and people in each of them, but that did not enter into his picture. He spoke to the clerk as though a single pair of hands would see the business through from beginning to end, and he gave his instructions accordingly, in no very particular order.

What he wanted, it emerged, was copies of his own books. But he wanted them sent by mail, and not to himself. They were to go to his brother's wife.

That was as far as he went on his own account. He seemed to feel that "my brother's wife" was a sufficient direction, and that the rest would somehow take care of itself. The assistant had to ask what her name was. The novelist gave it, a little as though he had been asked something slightly beside the point. Then the assistant had to ask where she lived, and the address had to be drawn out of him too, piece by piece, with a pause before each part while he recollected it. [Details of the name and address exchange needed.] At no point did he seem impatient, only vague, as a man is vague about matters that are not really his to attend to and that have always been attended to by someone else.

It would be easy to make fun of this, and I do not mean to. There was nothing grand or overbearing in the way he went about it. He was simply muddled, in the manner of someone whose attention lives almost entirely somewhere else, and who comes down to the practical world only when he must and then with some reluctance. The order was placed in the end, correctly, and the books went off. But the clerk who took it had done most of the placing.
