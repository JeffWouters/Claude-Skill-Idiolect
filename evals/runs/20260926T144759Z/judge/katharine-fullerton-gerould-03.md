# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-03

## The passage (by the author)

Most lies of the decenter sort are social. “The admixture of a lie doth ever give pleasure,” said the moralist Bacon. There is certainly very little defence for the lie that does not give pleasure. It is to save other people’s feelings, not our own, that we tell lies. Let me put a case quite bluntly. How, without lying, is a man to thank his small niece properly for the necktie which she has selected for his Christmas present? No one wants merely to be thanked for one’s trouble; every one wants to be told that his taste has been perfect. Now that the late Phillips Brooks’s handsome evasion of fact has become historic, who ever dares not to praise a baby explicitly? I confess that it goes against the grain with me to say that I have enjoyed something which I have detested; and I have frequently accepted invitations (especially over the telephone) because my tongue would not twist itself round the phrase “another engagement” when the other engagement was non-existent. But I have never had the slightest compunction about saying that I was sorry I had another engagement, when I did have another engagement and was not sorry.

I know only one person whom I could count on not to indulge herself in these conventional falsehoods, and she has never been able, so far as I know, to keep a friend. The habit of literal truth-telling, frankly, is self-indulgence of the worst. Nothing could be more delightful, in an evil sense, than telling certain people that their Christmas presents, their babies, and their hospitalities are all horrors which defy description; especially if one could count it a virtue to one’s self to say those things starkly. But one cannot keep that weapon only for one’s foes: the only excuse for saying inexcusable things is that one always says them. Roughly speaking, one’s friends are the people of whom one thinks, habitually, pleasant things. But even friends can be annoying, or unbeautiful, or dull. And it is of the essence of those manners which are morals not to tell them so if one can help it. “Faithful are the wounds of a friend”--and must sometimes be dealt. But no stabbing over non-essentials! And above all, no stabbing when it is a pleasure to stab. Sometimes these truth-tellers congratulate themselves that their praise is immensely enhanced by its rarity. There, I fancy, they are mistaken: for in the first place, praise that is too long on the way loses its savor; and in the second, they acquire, I have noticed, a censorious habit of mind that prevents them from praising at all.

## Draft A

There is a school of persons--they are usually proud of belonging to it--who will not tell the small lies of courtesy. They will not say they are delighted when they are merely present, nor that the soup was excellent when it was only soup. They call this honesty. I call it, on the whole, bad manners with a good conscience. The little social falsehood is not told for the liar's benefit; it is told to spare some one else a pang, and it deceives nobody who knows the language. When I tell my hostess that I have had a charming evening, she does not take it for sworn testimony, and I do not offer it as such. It is a façon de parler, as much as "yours faithfully" at the foot of a letter to a stranger.

Some one will object that truth is truth, and that a lie is a lie whatever its size. Granted; but the blunt truth-teller is seldom so disinterested as he supposes. His candour is a luxury, and like most luxuries it is paid for by other people. He enjoys the sensation of his own sincerity; his friends endure it. I fancy that few of them endure it for long. [example needed: a friendship lost to needless candour] One can say a hard thing when a hard thing is needed--and it sometimes is--but to say it on every trivial occasion is not virtue. It is self-indulgence.

The truth-teller has a second argument, which he thinks unanswerable. His praise, he says, is worth having precisely because it is rare. Well: is it? Praise that comes once in a long while is not treasured; it is simply forgotten, or disbelieved when it arrives. And the habitual critic does not, in practice, save up his praise for the great occasion. He loses the habit of it. He has so trained his eye to find the flaw that he can no longer see the thing itself (and he calls this discernment!), and when at last something deserves his admiration, he has nothing left to give it but a qualification. The point is that generosity, like any other faculty, withers if it is never used. Let us keep our small lies, then, and keep them kindly. They are the small change of affection. The man who won't spend it ends with none.

## Draft B

We are all, I suppose, acquainted with the person who "never tells a lie." She will say so herself, and more than once. You have only to show her your new hat, or read her the little verses you wrote last winter, and she will let you know--with the air of one doing a public service--exactly what she thinks of them. She calls this candour. I have another name for it, but I will keep it to myself; for I, at least, believe in the polite falsehood.

Let us be honest about the polite falsehood, since honesty is in question. When I tell my hostess that I have had a charming evening, I am not always reporting the facts. When I say that the baby is a beautiful baby, I may be stretching a point. But nobody is deceived to any purpose, and somebody is spared. These small courtesies are not really statements at all; they are the oil that keeps the social machine from shrieking. To refuse them is not to be more truthful than one's neighbours. It is only to be more uncomfortable to live with.

For there is a kind of truth-telling that is nothing but self-indulgence. The blunt person enjoys her bluntness; she enjoys it far more than any one else does. She has the pleasure of speaking her mind, and her friend has the pain of hearing it--and, frankly, I do not see that the arrangement is fair. It is a luxury she pays for, sooner or later, with her friendships. One by one they fall away, not because they could not bear the truth, but because they could not bear being made the occasion for her virtue. [example needed]

The candid critic has, of course, her defence ready. Her praise, she tells us, is worth having precisely because it is rare. When she says a thing is good, you may be sure it is good. I used to believe this; I believe it no longer. Praise does not gain in value by being hoarded, any more than a smile does. And the habit of criticism is a habit like any other--it grows on what it feeds on. The woman who begins by withholding praise from things that do not deserve it ends by withholding it from everything. She has trained her eye to find the flaw, and presently she cannot see anything else. I have known such people (and not the unkindest, either) who could not, at the last, have praised anything at all without a "but."

## Draft C

Every day, decent people tell small lies. They say they are delighted to see someone they had hoped to avoid. They praise a dinner that was overcooked and a hat that does not suit. They say they have had a lovely evening when they have mostly been waiting to leave. None of this troubles most of us, and I do not think it should.

These courtesies exist for a reason. They protect the feelings of other people, and they do so at almost no cost. The hostess who hears that her dinner was good has lost nothing by the kindness; she has been spared a disappointment she could do nothing about. The friend whose new hat is admired goes out happier than she would otherwise have gone. A great deal of ordinary social life runs on these small untruths, and it runs more smoothly for them. To call them lies at all is almost to use the word in a technical sense. Nobody is deceived about anything that matters.

There is, however, a certain kind of person who refuses to tell them. He prides himself on saying exactly what he thinks. If the dinner was poor, he will say so; if the hat is unbecoming, he will mention it. He regards this as a form of integrity, and he is puzzled when it is not admired.

I think he is mistaken about his own motives. Blunt truth-telling of this sort is seldom a sacrifice. More often it is an indulgence. The man who speaks his mind on every occasion is pleasing himself: he enjoys the sense of his own honesty, and he enjoys, perhaps, a little of the discomfort he causes. Others pay for his pleasure. Over time he pays too, because people do not keep friends who wound them for sport. He will explain the loss as the price of candour, when it is really the price of self-regard.

He also tends to believe that his praise, being rare, is worth more. If he says only what he means and seldom says anything kind, then surely a kind word from him is a prize. It does not work that way. Praise that is rationed does not become more valuable; it simply falls out of use. The habitual critic finds, after a while, that he no longer praises anything at all. He has trained his attention to look for faults, and faults are what it finds. The generous word that he was saving for the right occasion never comes, because no occasion ever seems quite good enough.

A little polite falsehood keeps that from happening. It keeps us in the habit of looking for something to be glad about, and of saying so.
