# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-10

## The passage (by the author)

Mr. Brownell, the critic, entered through the front door and moved to the elevator.

There stepped from the elevator car a somewhat portly little man who joined Mr. Wharton. He wore a rather queer looking, very big derby hat, oddly flat on top. His shoulders were hooped up somewhat like the figure of Joseph Choate. A rather funny, square, box-like body on little legs. An English look to his clothes. Under his arm an odd-looking club of a walking-stick. Mr. Brownell turned quickly to this rather amusing though not undistinguished figure, and said, "Mr. James--Brownell." The quaint gentleman took off his big hat, discovering to our intent curiosity a polished bald dome, and began instantly to talk, very earnestly, steadily, in a moderately pitched voice, gesticulating with an even rhythmic beat with his right hand, raised close to his face.

Joined presently by Mrs. Wharton, the party, bidding Mr. Brownell adieu, took a somewhat humorous departure (we felt) from the shop; Mr. James, with some suddenness, preceding out the door. Moving nimbly up the Avenue, he was overhauled by Mrs. Wharton under full sail, who attached herself to his arm. Her husband by an energetic forward play around the end achieved her other wing. In this formation, sticks flashing, skirt whipping, with a somewhat spirited mien, the august spectacle receded from our rapt view, to be at length obliterated as a unit by the general human scene.

We saw Mr. James after this a number of times. Accompanied again by Mrs. Wharton, and later in the charge (such was the effect) of another lady, who, we understood, drives regularly to her social chariot literary lions. In something like six years' observation of the human being in a book shop, we have never seen any person so thoroughly in a book store, a magazine, that is, of books, as Mr. James. One can be, you know--it is most common, indeed--in a book store and at the same time not be in a book store--any more than if one were in a hotel lobby. Mr. James "snooked" around the shop. He ran his nose over the tables, and inch by inch (he must be very shortsighted) along the walls, stood on tiptoe and pulled down volumes from high places, rummaged in dark corners, was apparently oblivious of the presence of anything but the books. He was not the slightest in a hurry. He would have been, we felt, content and quite happy, like a child with blocks, to play this way by himself all day.

## Draft A

I had formed, from his books, a very definite picture of the novelist. It was a tall picture. It was, I think, rather pale and rather lean, with something of the ascetic about it, as befitted the author of books so fine, so scrupulous and so long. The original, when at length he came through the door, corrected this picture at every point.

Short. Stout. Bald. Not, by any means, the figure one had supposed; and yet, after the first moment of surprise, not a disappointing figure either. There was a solidity about him, a kind of massive and deliberate composure, that suited him better than any leanness could have done. He did not hurry. He did not look about him in the eager, uncertain way of most people who come into a bookshop. He simply stood, and talked.

And as he talked his hands moved. It was not the quick, fluttering gesture of the excitable conversationalist, nor the broad sweep of the public speaker. It was a steady, measured motion, continued without a break from the beginning of a sentence to the end of it, as if he were drawing the sentence out of the air, so to speak, and laying it carefully down before his listener, clause by clause. I found I could not take my eyes from those hands. I have forgotten, I am ashamed to say, most of what he said on that occasion. I have not forgotten how he said it.

He did not leave alone. He went out, when his business was done, walking arm in arm with a lady who wrote for society [name needed: the society author] and with her husband, the three of them proceeding up the street together in a very companionable, very leisurely fashion; and I stood at the window and watched them go, as one might watch a small procession.

He came back, however, on other occasions, and on these he was a different man, or at any rate a differently occupied one. He came to look at books, and he looked at them with an absorption that was complete. He would stand before a shelf, take down a volume, turn it over, open it, read a little, close it, replace it, and take down the next; and during the whole of this proceeding he was, to all appearances, entirely unaware that there was anybody else in the shop, or indeed in the world. One did not approach him. One would as soon have interrupted a man at his prayers.

## Draft B

The first time I saw him he was standing near the front of the shop, talking. I did not know at first who he was. He was a short man, and stout, and quite bald; the light from the window fell upon the top of his head. He was not, to look at, in the least what one would have expected. He talked steadily, not loud, and as he talked he moved his hands. He moved them all the time, evenly, one after the other, as though he were shaping what he said in the air before him and setting it down, piece by piece, where it belonged. I remember the hands better than I remember anything he said, for I was not near enough to hear much of that.

Somebody told me who he was. I looked again. I confess it did not seem possible.

He was with a lady who was also, in her way, a writer: a society author, very much in the fashion at that time, whose books we sold a good many of. Her husband was with them. They stood about among the tables for a while, the three of them, and he went on talking, and the lady listened, and the husband listened too, or looked as if he did. Then they went out together. They went out arm in arm, the novelist in the middle, a short stout figure between the two of them, still talking, I think, as they passed through the door. I watched them go up the street.

He came back afterwards, more than once, and alone. And on these occasions he was another man altogether. He did not talk. He did not, so far as I could see, notice anybody. He went to the shelves and he stayed there. He took down a book and looked at it, at the title page, at the binding, at the print, turning it over; and put it back; and took down the next one. He went along the shelves in this way, slowly, from one end to the other, entirely absorbed. You could have spoken to him and I believe he would not have heard. Once or twice I did go near him, to ask whether there was anything that I could find for him, and then thought better of it, and went away again. It seemed to me that he was, just then, very busy; and that whatever it was that he was busy about, it was his own affair.

## Draft C

I saw him first from across the shop, before I knew who he was. There was nothing about his appearance to announce a famous man. He was short and stout, and quite bald, and if I had passed him in the street I should not have looked twice. What drew my attention was the way he talked. He was speaking to the people with him, and as he spoke his hands moved continually, not in sudden flourishes but in a steady, even rhythm, as if they were keeping time with the sentences or shaping them in the air as they came out. He did not stay long on that first occasion. When he left, he went out walking arm in arm with a society author and her husband. [Names of the society author and her husband needed.] The three of them went through the door together, still talking, his free hand still keeping up its measured movement, and I remember thinking what an unlikely group they made: the lady plainly at home in drawing rooms, her husband attentive at her side, and between them this short, bald, stout man who looked as if he had wandered in from somewhere much less fashionable.

He came back, though, more than once, and on those later visits he came alone, or at least without company that claimed his attention. It was then that I saw him as I like best to remember him. He went to the shelves and simply lost himself in them. He would take down a volume, look at the title page, turn it over, read a passage, put it back, and take down the next, and he did all of this with a completeness of attention that shut out everything else in the shop. Customers moved around him; assistants passed with armfuls of books; conversations went on at the counter; and none of it seemed to reach him. [Example of a book he examined needed.]

I have watched many people browse, and most of them do it with half their minds elsewhere, glancing at the clock or at the door. He was not like that. For as long as he stood at the shelves, the shelves were the whole of his world, and I found it oddly moving to see a man who wrote books himself still so entirely absorbed by other people's.
