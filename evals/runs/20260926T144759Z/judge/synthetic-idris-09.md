## The passage (by the author)

What does it feel like to become a beginner again after a long period of competence? Most of us in technical work will face the question repeatedly, since the tools and languages and platforms we depend upon change with a regularity that makes permanent expertise almost impossible; the skills that made us valuable not long ago may now be of diminishing relevance, and the new skills we need must be acquired while we continue to be expected to perform. It is an uncomfortable position, arguably more uncomfortable for experienced practitioners than for newcomers, since the experienced have grown accustomed to the feeling of knowing what they are doing and have perhaps forgotten what it is like not to. I suspect that a great deal of resistance to new technology, which is usually described in terms of technical merit, is really a resistance to that feeling of incompetence. We defend the familiar tool not because it is better but because we are better at it, and because the alternative would require us to be seen struggling.

There is also the question of where the learning is supposed to happen. Organisations speak warmly about continuous learning and professional development, and some of them genuinely mean it; yet the actual structure of most working weeks leaves remarkably little room for the slow, inefficient, exploratory activity that real learning requires. You are expected to acquire the new skill somehow, perhaps in the margins of the day or in your own time, while delivering at the level you delivered before. Is it surprising that people tend to learn only as much as is immediately necessary, picking up fragments of a new technology in the course of solving particular problems without ever developing a coherent understanding of how it works? The result is a kind of patchwork competence, adequate for the tasks at hand but brittle whenever something unexpected occurs.

## Draft A

There is a particular kind of unease that settles over experienced engineers when they are asked to adopt a new technology. It rarely announces itself as unease. More often it takes the form of reasoned objection: the old tool works perfectly well, the new one is immature, the migration will cost more than it saves. Some of these objections are entirely sound. But I suspect that beneath many of them lies something less comfortable to say aloud, namely the fear of looking unskilled. Someone who has spent years becoming fluent in one way of working is being asked, in effect, to become a beginner again, and to do so in front of colleagues who may have come to rely on their expertise.

Organisations, for their part, tend to speak warmly about growth. Learning is praised in values statements and performance reviews; curiosity is encouraged, at least in principle. What is less often provided is time. The new technology is expected to be adopted alongside existing commitments, fitted into the gaps between deadlines, and the implicit message is that learning is something one should manage to do without it appearing anywhere on the plan. It is hardly surprising, under these conditions, that people approach it with some reluctance.

The result is a peculiar style of learning, in which engineers acquire only the fragments they need to complete the task in front of them. A command is copied from somewhere, a configuration is adjusted until it works, and the underlying model of how the thing actually behaves is never quite formed. Knowledge gathered this way is fragile. It serves well enough until something unexpected happens, at which point it becomes clear that nobody really understands the system they have been operating. Perhaps the more honest approach would be to admit that learning takes time and discomfort, and to budget for both, rather than pretending that fluency can be assembled from scraps.

## Draft B

What is it about a new technology that makes an experienced engineer so reluctant to learn it? It may be that the reluctance has less to do with the technology than with what learning it requires, which is a return, however brief, to the uncomfortable position of not knowing what one is doing.

The resistance rarely presents itself in those terms. It arrives instead as a reasoned objection to the tool, a doubt about its maturity or its fit, and some of these objections are entirely fair; others, I suspect, are a way of avoiding the experience of looking unskilled in front of colleagues who have come to rely on their judgement. Who among us has not argued against something partly because we would have been a beginner at it?

There is also the matter of time, which organisations speak about more generously than they allocate it. Employers praise growth and continuous learning in their values and their reviews, yet the working week is usually arranged as though learning happened somewhere else, in evenings or in the gaps between deadlines, and the engineer who takes a day to study something properly may find that day difficult to account for. The message received is arguably the opposite of the one intended.

It seems to me that the consequence is a particular kind of fragile knowledge. People learn only what they need at the moment they need it, a command here and a workaround there, assembling scraps that allow the immediate task to be finished without ever forming a coherent understanding of the whole; this works well enough until something unexpected happens, at which point the scraps turn out to be considerably less than they appeared. Perhaps the real cost of never having time to learn properly is not ignorance but a competence that cannot survive its first surprise.

## Draft C

Experienced engineers are often the loudest critics of new technology. When a team adopts a new framework, language or platform, the pushback frequently comes from the people with the most years behind them. It usually sounds technical: the old tool works fine, the new one is immature, the migration is not worth the risk. Sometimes those arguments are right. But often they cover something more personal.

For someone who has spent years becoming skilled, being forced back to the beginning is uncomfortable. They are used to knowing the answer, to being the person others ask. A new tool takes that away, at least for a while. Suddenly they are slow, making beginner mistakes, and asking questions that a junior colleague might answer faster. The resistance is often less about the technology than about the fear of looking unskilled in front of people who have come to rely on them.

Organisations make this harder than it needs to be. Employers talk constantly about growth, continuous learning and staying current. Yet they rarely leave real time for it. Learning is expected to happen alongside full delivery commitments, squeezed into gaps that do not really exist. [example needed]

So people do the only thing they can. They learn scraps as they need them. They look up exactly enough to finish the current task, copy a pattern that seems to work, and move on. Over time this produces knowledge that looks adequate on the surface but is fragile underneath. It holds up in familiar situations and breaks down as soon as something unexpected happens, because the underlying understanding was never built.

That fragile knowledge then feeds back into the original fear. An engineer who knows their grasp of a tool is shaky has every reason to avoid situations that might expose it. Giving experienced people genuine time to learn, and permission to be visibly bad at something for a while, would break that loop.
