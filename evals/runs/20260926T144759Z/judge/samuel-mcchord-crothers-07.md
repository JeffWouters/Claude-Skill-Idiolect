# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-07

## The passage (by the author)

It is evident that there were some points in Christianity that King Olaf did not appreciate. To cultivate these fruits of the spirit required men of a different temper. Their work is not all done yet. It is progressing.

There is one complication in the work of peacemaking which has not been sufficiently considered. It is the recurrence of Youth. I have listened to the arguments against war at a great Peace Congress. The reasoning was strong, the statement of facts conclusive. War was shown to be cruel and foolish, and incredibly expensive. The audience, consisting of right-minded and very intelligent people, was convinced of the justice of the cause of Peace. Why, then, does not the cause triumph?

In such cases I am in the habit of looking about with the intent to fix the responsibility where it belongs, on those who were not at the meeting. Mature life was well represented, but there was a suspicious absence of young men in the twenties. Ah! I said, there is the difficulty. We can’t be sure of lasting peace until we make it more interesting to these young absentees. They’ll all be peace men by and by, but meanwhile there is no knowing what trouble they may get us into.

John Fiske traced the influence which the prolongation of infancy has had on the progress of civilization. I am inclined to think that equally great results would flow from any discovery by which the period of middle age could be prolonged beyond its present term. War would be abolished without any more ado. A uniformly middle-aged community would be immune from any attack of militant fever.

It happens, however, that every once in a while the hot passions of youth carry all before them. The account of what happened at the beginning of the civil wars in Israel is typical. King Rehoboam called a meeting of the elder statesmen of his kingdom. They outlined a policy that was eminently conciliatory. But we are told, “He forsook the counsel of the old men which they had given him, and consulted with the young men that were grown up with him and which stood by him.”

## Draft A

Every effort to end war runs into the same obstacle, and the obstacle is youth. Not any particular young person, and not the young out of wickedness, but the plain fact that each generation arrives full of an enthusiasm the previous one has spent. That enthusiasm keeps undoing the careful work of peace.

Look at who attends a peace conference. The halls fill with older listeners, and the older listeners are persuaded. They hear the arguments about cost and suffering and waste, and they agree, because the arguments match what they already know. The speeches do their work on exactly the people who needed them least. Young men, meanwhile, stay away. They are not in the audience to be convinced, and nothing said there reaches them. When the next occasion for war arrives, it is their opinion, not the conference's, that carries the day.

One could almost propose, half seriously, a remedy that goes to the root. If the warlike impulse belongs to youth and fades in middle age, then perhaps the answer is to extend middle age. Let people reach it sooner and stay in it longer, and the appetite for fighting might simply have no season in which to grow. It is a fanciful idea, of course. But it names the difficulty more honestly than many serious proposals do. The trouble is not a shortage of good arguments. It is a shortage of listeners in the frame of mind to accept them.

The pattern is old. There is a biblical story of a king who, faced with a hard decision, took counsel from the elders who had served before him, and then ignored them. He turned instead to the young men he had grown up with, his peers, and followed their advice. Their advice was bold and harsh, and the result was division: the kingdom split, and it did not come back together. [example needed: the king's name and passage, if the piece is to cite them]

That story could stand at the head of every account of a failed peace. The elders spoke sensibly. The young spoke with more heat and more confidence, and heat and confidence are what move a crowd. Until the peacemakers find a way to address the young men who stay away, their best work will keep being undone by the next generation to come of age.

## Draft B

Anyone who has sat through a peace conference will have come away with the same impression, that the arguments were excellent, the speakers earnest and the audience thoroughly convinced before the chairman had so much as risen to introduce the first of them. But the audience was made up almost entirely of persons who had long since passed the age at which a man wishes to fight anybody. The young men, whom it was most necessary to persuade, were not there; they had other engagements.

This is not a small matter. Peace, when she holds her meetings, speaks in the measured voice of experience, and she addresses herself to those who have had enough experience to appreciate her. War has no need of meetings. He goes directly to the young, who have had no experience at all, and he tells them a story about glory which they are quite prepared to believe. It is an unequal contest, and I do not see how it is to be made more equal by adding to the number of conferences.

A more radical remedy suggests itself. If the warlike impulse belongs to youth and disappears with middle age, the obvious course is to extend middle age. Let us contrive, by some method that the physiologists may be left to discover, that men shall pass directly from childhood into the calm and prudent forties. The drum would beat in vain. There would be nobody left to hear it. I offer the proposal without much hope of its adoption, but it has at least the merit of going to the root of the difficulty.

For the difficulty is a very old one. There was a king in the Bible who, on coming to the throne, sought the counsel of the old men who had served his father. They advised moderation. He then consulted the young men who had grown up with him, and when they advised the opposite he followed his peers rather than his elders, as young kings and young men generally have done whenever the choice was put to them. [quotation needed from the scripture passage] The kingdom was divided in consequence.

The story has been read in churches for a great many generations. I have never heard that it made the young men of any of them more willing to listen to their fathers.

## Draft C

I have attended a good many peace conferences, and I have always come away with the same impression. The arguments were unanswerable. The speakers were eloquent. The audience was convinced. But when I looked about me at the audience, I observed that it was composed almost entirely of persons of mature years. There were gray heads in abundance, and there were bald heads, and there were heads which had long since made up their minds on most questions. The young men were not there.

This is the difficulty. The persons who are persuaded that war is a folly are not the persons who fight. They have passed the age when the call of adventure is irresistible. They have learned, by the slow process of living, that most quarrels are not worth the trouble, and that the glories of battle look better from a distance. But you cannot hand on this knowledge to the next generation as you would hand on a watch. Each generation must acquire it for itself, and in the meantime there is always a war.

I have sometimes thought that the problem might be solved in another way. If we cannot make the young wise, perhaps we might make them middle-aged. Could we but extend the period of middle life, so that men passed into it directly from their school days, the warlike impulses might disappear of themselves. A world of middle-aged men would be a peaceable world. It might be a little dull, but it would not be dangerous. Of course the danger is that middle-aged men, deprived of the young to restrain, would find nothing to do.

The difficulty is an old one. There is a story in the Bible of a king, [name needed], who came to the throne and had to decide how he would govern. He consulted the old men who had served his father, and they gave him prudent advice. Then he consulted the young men who had grown up with him, and they gave him advice of a different kind. He took the advice of his peers. The result was that the kingdom was divided. It is not recorded that the young men were in the least disturbed by the outcome. They had had their way, and it was the old men who had to live with the consequences.
