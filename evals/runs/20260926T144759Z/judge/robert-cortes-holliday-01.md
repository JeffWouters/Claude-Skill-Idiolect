# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-01

## The passage (by the author)

We have now to record an extraordinary adventure. Our later education was derived in some considerable measure from the writings of Mr. Henry James. This to explain our emotion. We had never expected to behold himself, the illustrious expatriate who had so far enlightened an unkempt mind. But the night before we had been talking of him. Indeed, it is impossible for us to fail to perceive here something of the supernatural.

But hold! "William Edwards," says a newspaper notice, "who used to drive a post stage between New York and Albany, died on Saturday at his home. He was born in Albany," and so and so, "and many were the stories he had to tell of incidents connected with the famous men who were his passengers." Even so. We were ourselves a clerk. That is, for a number of years we waited on customers in a celebrated book shop. This is one of the stories we have to tell of the personages who were, so to say, our passengers. Or perhaps we are more in the nature of those unscrupulous English footmen to high society, of whom we have heard, who "sell out" their observation and information to the society press.

Anyhow, we are of a loquacious, gossipy turn; and we were booksellers, so to speak, to crowned heads. We have recently heard, too, of another precedent to our garrulous performance, the publication in Rome of the memoirs of an old waiter, who carefully set down the relative liberality of prominent persons whom he served. After having served Cardinals Rampolla and Merry del Val, this excellent memoirist entered opposite their names, "Both no good." With this we drop the defensive.

We noticed Mr. Wharton sitting down, legs crossed, smoking a cigar. Awaiting, we presumed, his wife. A not unpicturesque figure, tall, rather dashing in effect, ruddy visage, dragoon moustache, and habited in a light, smartly-cut sack suit of rather arresting checks, conspicuous grey spats; a gentleman manifesting no interest whatever in his surroundings.

## Draft A

It is, I suppose, a small vulgarity to tell tales of the customers. Still, I do not see that I am worse than my betters in this. Coachmen have done it. The man who drove for years for [person] has told, I believe, what that gentleman said on the box and off it. Waiters have done it. The waiter who brought the soup to [person] at [place] has let it be known how that great man took his soup, and what he said of it. Nobody appears to have thought any the less of the coachman or of the waiter. And a clerk in a bookshop, who sees people when they are thinking of nothing so much as themselves, is, I venture to say, in quite as good a position as a coachman. So I shall gossip a little.

Our acquaintance with the novelist was--merely professional. I had admired him for a long time. I had read him, I think, pretty nearly all. The evening before I saw him I had been talking about him. We had gone into the matter at some length, at [place], a friend and I, and had settled, as one does settle such things in the evening, pretty much everything there was to be settled about him: what he meant, what he did not mean, and what he would be like if one met him. Then, the next morning, there he was. It gave me a queer feeling. It was, you might say, a little uncanny, as if talking of a man had called him up out of the air, and he had walked in off the street to see what all the fuss was about.

He was not alone. With him there was a companion, a very well-dressed person, of the sort who is well dressed rather thoroughly, from the hat down. This companion did not want a book. This companion did not appear to want anything. He stood about. He looked at nothing in particular, idly, with the air of a man who is waiting in a shop because somebody else has business there, and who is prepared to go on waiting, patiently, for as long as it may take.

## Draft B

I know there is something faintly disreputable in a shop assistant setting down what he saw of the customers who passed his counter. People who serve the public are supposed to be deaf and blind to it, or at least silent. But I notice that nobody has ever held the rule very strictly. Coachmen who drove the great have told what they heard through the little window behind them, and waiters who stood by famous tables have filled whole books with what was said over the soup. If they may talk, I do not see why a man who once sold books should be forbidden to. So I will tell what I remember of the day I met [novelist's name], a writer I had admired for years before I ever expected to stand within arm's reach of him.

The strange part of it began the night before. A few of us had spent the evening arguing about his books, as young men who read too much will do, and I had been defending him at some length and with more heat than the subject perhaps deserved. I went to bed with his name still in my head. The next morning I was at my post in the shop, and there he was. It is hard to describe how odd that felt. It was not quite surprise; it was more like the sensation of having summoned someone by talking about him, as though the conversation of the previous evening had reached out and fetched him through the door. For a moment I half distrusted my own eyes.

He was not alone. With him came a companion, a person very well dressed, who took no interest whatever in the books. While the writer moved along the shelves, the companion simply waited, standing about in the idle, patient way of someone who has been brought along and knows there is nothing to do but let the time pass. I remember that figure almost as clearly as the man himself, perhaps because the contrast between them was so sharp: one of them absorbed, the other merely present.

What followed is the reason I am writing at all, and I will come to it next.

## Draft C

It may be objected that a man who once sold books across a counter has no business telling tales of the people who bought them. Perhaps not. But consider the coachman. The coachman has driven the great to their dinners and afterwards, over his pipe in the stable yard, told anybody who cared to listen exactly what the great said on the way, and how they said it, and what they looked like when they thought nobody was attending to them. Consider the waiter, who has carried soup to statesmen and remembered, with a fidelity no biographer could match, how the statesmen behaved toward the soup. Nobody thinks the worse of them; their memoirs are a recognised branch of literature. I claim no more than the privilege of the coachman and the waiter. I stood behind a counter, and certain notable persons came to the other side of it.

Of these the one I remember best is the novelist. I had admired him a long while; I had read him, I may say, with a devotion I did not give to my duties. And it happened, in the way such things will happen, that on the evening before he first came into the shop I had sat up late discussing him, arguing over his books, his manner, his standing, and the old question of whether he was the greatest of living writers or merely the most difficult, until I had turned him over and over like a coin and could not have told you which side was up. Then the next morning there he was. I do not know how to describe the feeling except to say that it was a little uncanny. It was as if I'd talked him into the room.

He did not come alone. With him was a companion, very well dressed, who seemed to have no interest whatever in books. While the novelist went about his business this companion simply waited, standing about near the door and looking at nothing in particular. There was no impatience in it; it was merely idleness, in the handsome, finished way that some people are idle. I've often wondered what the companion thought of it all. Probably nothing.
