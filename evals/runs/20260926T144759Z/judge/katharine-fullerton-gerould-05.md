# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-05

## The passage (by the author)

Take one example only: the attitude towards Greek. There are two arguments against teaching our children Greek: one, that it is too hard; the other, that it is useless. The mere fact that public opinion has drummed Greek out of court as an inevitable part of a college curriculum shows that these arguments have been potent. No person who could be influenced by either has the remotest conception of the meaning or the value of culture. Culture has never renounced a thing because it was difficult, or because it did not help people to make money. And the mere fact that Greek is no longer supposed by the vast majority of parents to be of any “use”--even as a matter of reputation--to their sons, shows that the old standards of culture have changed. The larger number of our public schools no longer teach Greek at all; a great many private schools have to make special arrangements for pupils who wish to study it. And the attitude towards Greek is only a sign of our democratic, materialistic times.

Now I have done with the colleges. I have dealt with them at all only by way of hinting that they have been so democratized that culture means, even to its avowed exponents, something different from what it has ever meant before. May I speak for one moment explicitly of the public schools? For we must trace all this back to the source--must begin with the ostensible homes of “culture” and follow up the stream to the latent public consciousness. Each class that comes into college has read fewer and fewer of what are called the classics of English literature. An astonishing number of boys and girls have read nothing worth reading except the books that are in the entrance requirements. An increasing proportion of the sons and daughters of the prosperous are positively illiterate at college age. They cannot spell; they cannot express themselves grammatically; and they are inclined to think that it does not matter. General laxity, and the adoption of educational fads which play havoc with real education, are largely responsible. In the less fortunate classes, the fact seems to be that the public schools are so swamped by foreigners that all the teachers can manage to do is to teach the pupils a little workable English. Needless to say, the profession of the public-school teacher has become less and less tempting to people who are really fit for it.

## Draft A

Anyone who has watched American schooling for a generation will have noticed that the standard of culture it aims at has fallen. The decline is not dramatic in any single year, but it is steady, and it shows most clearly in what schools have stopped asking of their students.

Greek is the plainest example. It has been dropped, or allowed to wither, in one school after another. The reasons given are two: that it is too difficult, and that it is not practical. Both reasons are revealing. To say a subject is too hard is to admit that difficulty itself has become an objection, as though the purpose of education were to spare students effort. To say it is impractical is to measure learning entirely by what it will earn. Greek was never defended on the ground that it would get anyone a job. It was defended because it opened a literature and a way of thinking that shaped much of what came after. When that defence no longer carries weight, it is because the people making the decisions have come to value only what can be turned to material account.

The results are visible at the other end of the process. Students now arrive at college having read very little. The books that an earlier generation took for granted as common ground are, for many of them, only names. Their writing is worse still: loose, uncertain, and often ungrammatical. College teachers find themselves doing the work that the schools were supposed to have done, and doing it too late to do it well.

There is no single culprit. Educational fads have played their part, each promising a quicker and more agreeable route to learning, and each leaving behind a little less learning than before. There has been a general laxity as well, a reluctance to insist, to correct, to require. Standards that are not enforced do not survive long. And the public schools have been given more to do than any institution can manage. They are expected to teach large and varied classes, to prepare some students for work and others for college, and to take on a growing list of social duties besides. Under that weight, the cultivation of taste and learning is the first thing to go, because it is the easiest thing to postpone.

None of this is beyond repair. But it will not be repaired by anyone who thinks the loss of Greek is merely a change in the timetable. It is a sign of what we have decided education is for, and that is the decision that needs to be reconsidered.

## Draft B

It is a melancholy thing to watch Greek go out of the schools, and more melancholy still to hear the reasons given. It is too difficult, we are told; or it is impractical, which is to say that it will not help a boy to sell bonds. Neither argument, I fancy, would once have been thought respectable. That they are thought respectable now tells us less about Greek than about ourselves. We have come to measure a study by what it will earn, and a subject that earns nothing is, by that reckoning, worth nothing. This is materialism, pure and simple; it merely wears a cap and gown.

The results are visible to any one who has taught a freshman class. [example needed: a specific observation from teaching] The students arrive having read almost nothing--not the classics, not the moderns, not, in many cases, anything but what was set them. They write as they read: badly, and without pleasure. I do not blame them. You cannot expect a young man to write a clean English sentence if he has never been made to read one, and you cannot expect him to love books if he has only met them as examinations.

Where, then, does the blame lie? Not, I think, in any one place. There are the fads, first of all--the educational novelty of the season, adopted with enthusiasm and dropped without regret. There is the laxity that has crept into standards, so that a boy who knows little is passed because failing him would be unkind. And there are the public schools, which have been asked to do everything for every one and are, not unnaturally, doing nothing very thoroughly. Their teachers are overloaded; their classes are crowded; their curriculum is a patchwork of good intentions. It would be unjust to lay the whole fault at their door. It would be foolish to pretend they bear none.

The point is that culture is not a luxury the schools may add when the practical work is done. It is the work. Let us drop it, by all means, if we are content to raise a nation that can do sums and cannot read. But let us not pretend we've lost nothing.

## Draft C

Every now and then some one rises at a meeting of schoolmasters and moves that Greek be dropped. It is always dropped, sooner or later; and the reasons given are always the same. Greek is too difficult. Greek is not practical. Greek will not help a boy to earn his living. I do not know which of these reasons I find the more depressing. The first is an admission that we no longer expect our children to do hard things; the second and third are an admission that we no longer value anything we cannot sell. It is not Greek that has been weighed and found wanting. It is we.

For the dropping of Greek is only a symptom. The materialism that finds no use for Homer finds very little use for anything else that does not pay, and the effects of it are to be seen in every college in the land. I am told, by those who teach them, that a great many students now arrive at college having read almost nothing. They have not read the Bible; they have not read Shakespeare, except for a play or two taken apart in class; they have not, in any real sense, read at all. And they cannot write. They can put words together--after a fashion--but the words do not make sentences, and the sentences do not make sense. [example needed]

Who is to blame? Frankly, I think there is blame enough to go round. There are the fads, to begin with: the new method every year, the new subject every term, each one taken up with enthusiasm and dropped with relief. There is the laxity--the fear of asking too much, the notion that a child must never be bored and never be made to learn anything by heart. And there are the public schools themselves, which are asked to do far more than any schools can do, with far too many children and far too little time. I am sorry for the teachers; I am heartily sorry for them. They are expected to turn out citizens, mechanics and stenographers all at once, and it is hardly surprising if they have no leisure left to turn out readers.

But being sorry for the teachers does not mend the matter. The children still come to college unread, and they still go out of it--a good many of them--very little better. One cannot build culture on nothing; and nothing is, I am afraid, what we are giving them to build on.
