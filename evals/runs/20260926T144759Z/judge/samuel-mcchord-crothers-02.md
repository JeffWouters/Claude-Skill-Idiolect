# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-02

## The passage (by the author)

And there was Old Tansy, a bit of wreckage from the times of ’49. There was a tradition that Tansy had seen better days; at least, it was hard to imagine how he could have seen worse. He lived without visible means of support, and yet he was not submerged. It pleased the community to accept Tansy as a character worth knowing in spite of his fallen fortunes. His obvious failings were always clothed in soft euphemisms. No one could say that he had ever seen him drunk, and on the other hand no one would be so rash as to assert that he had ever seen him sober. In the border land between moderate drinking and inebriety, Tansy dwelt in peace.

What most endeared Tansy to his fellows was his mild religiosity, which manifested itself in persistent church-going. He was no fair-weather Christian. There was no occasion when he would not desert his favorite saloon to take his accustomed place in the back pew of the Presbyterian church. Only once did Tansy express an opinion in regard to the services which he so assiduously attended. A minister passing through the town preached a lurid sermon on the future punishment of the wicked. He spared no materialistic imagery to make his remarks effective. At the close of the service Tansy, instead of going out, as was his custom, went forward and, grasping the minister’s hands, said in a tone of quiet satisfaction, “Parson, it done me good.”

Just what the nature of the good was he did not indicate. I suppose that there was something in the unction of the preacher that recalled memories of the past.

There was one person whom I always recall with peculiar pleasure. To see him coming over the divide in a cloud of dust was to see one of the typical forms of creation. He was known, on account of the huge pair of goggles which he wore, as “Four-Eyed Nick.” He dwelt in a cabin in the most desolate part of the mountain, and he fitted his environment perfectly. He seemed as natural a product of the soil as the sage brush, for like it he had learned to exist where there was very little water.

## Draft A

Two figures from that remote mining settlement in the West have stayed with me longer than most of the people I knew far better elsewhere. Neither was important. Neither would have been remembered at all, I suspect, if the place itself had been less empty, so that every odd shape on its streets and hillsides stood out against the bare ground like a post in a field.

The first was a veteran of the gold rush, one of those men who had come early, worked hard, and somehow ended with nothing. He was plainly down and out. His clothes had been good once, and his manner had been better. He drank, and he drank steadily; there was no season of the year and very few hours of the day when he could be counted wholly sober. And yet, by a contradiction that nobody in the camp seemed to find strange, he attended church faithfully. Sunday found him in his place, upright and attentive, whatever Saturday night had been.

I remember one occasion in particular. A visiting preacher came through and delivered a sermon on damnation of the old, fiery kind, full of the pit and the flames and the certainty that most of the congregation was headed there. Many of the listeners shifted and looked at their boots. The old veteran, when it was over, went up to the preacher and thanked him for it. He seemed genuinely grateful, as though he had been handed something he needed. What he found in that sermon I never learned. [example needed: his exact words to the preacher, if recorded]

The second character was a recluse who lived alone on a barren hillside above the settlement. He wore goggles, for reasons of his own that he did not explain and that nobody asked about. He came down seldom, spoke little, and went back up again. I used to think that he suited that hillside the way the native scrub suited it. He looked as though he had grown there out of the dry soil, grey and tough and not particularly welcoming, and as though he would still be there, unchanged, when the rest of us and the camp itself had gone.

## Draft B

In the days when I lived in the mining camp of [place needed], there were not many inhabitants, but those there were had a way of standing out against the landscape. There was nothing to hide them. A man in a crowded city may be as odd as he pleases and nobody will notice, for his oddity is lost among a million others. But in a place where the whole population could be counted on the fingers of a few hands, every peculiarity had a value of its own, and we cherished it.

One of our neighbors was a veteran of the gold rush. He had come out in the great days, when fortunes were to be picked up in the bed of any creek, and he had evidently not picked up his. What he had picked up was a steady habit of drinking. He was not a violent drinker; he was simply a faithful one. He drank as other men work, with regularity and without fuss. And yet, with the same regularity, he was to be found every Sunday in his place at church. I never knew him to miss. He seemed to feel no incongruity between the two observances, and after a while neither did we.

Once a visiting preacher came to us and delivered a sermon on damnation of the old-fashioned kind. It was not a sermon for the faint of heart. The fires were hot and the pit was bottomless, and there was no hint of any way out. When the service was over, our veteran went up to the preacher, shook him warmly by the hand, and thanked him. He said it was the kind of preaching he liked. I have often wondered what he meant. Perhaps he felt that a man who had lived as he had deserved at least to be taken seriously.

On a barren hillside above the camp lived another of our citizens, a recluse who wore goggles. Why he wore them I never learned. He did not come down among us often, and when he did he had little to say. But he suited his hillside. He seemed to have grown out of it, like the native scrub, and to belong there quite as much. It would have seemed a violation of nature to transplant him.

## Draft C

There are places which are remembered for their scenery and places which are remembered for their inhabitants. The mining settlement in which I once spent some time belonged decidedly to the second class. It lay in a remote corner of the West, at the end of a road that seemed to have been built for the express purpose of discouraging visitors, and the population was so small that every eccentricity in it became, as it were, a public monument.

The first of my acquaintances was a veteran of the gold rush who had long since fallen on evil days. He had come early and stayed late; whatever fortune he had once found had departed without leaving a forwarding address. He drank steadily. I do not mean that he drank to excess on particular occasions, for that would imply a certain irregularity in his habits, and he had none. Yet on Sunday morning he was invariably in his place at church, and no member of the congregation listened more attentively.

I remember a visiting preacher [detail needed about the occasion] who was a man of the old school, and who gave us a sermon on damnation that left nothing whatever to the imagination. When the service was over, my old friend made his way to the front, took the preacher by the hand and thanked him warmly for it. It was, he said, the kind of preaching he liked. I have often wondered what he found in it. Perhaps it was merely the satisfaction, rare enough in any community, of hearing a subject treated by someone who believed in it.

The second character was a recluse who lived by himself on a barren hillside above the camp. He wore goggles, for reasons which he never explained and which nobody ventured to ask; he seldom came down among us. But I never passed his cabin without feeling that he was exactly where he belonged. He had grown into that hillside as the native scrub had grown into it, tough and patient and entirely indifferent to the opinion of strangers, so that to have transplanted him to a city would have seemed as unkind as to transplant the scrub itself.

What became of either of them I cannot say. I have met few people since who fitted their surroundings so well.
