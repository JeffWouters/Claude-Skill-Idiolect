## The passage (by the author)

How do you describe the state of a piece of work that is neither finished nor failing, but somewhere in the uncertain territory between? The question confronts most of us regularly, whenever we are asked to provide a status update, and it is considerably harder to answer honestly than the familiar formats would suggest. The traffic-light colours, the estimate of completion, the brief summary of progress and risks — these devices promise to compress a complicated reality into something a busy reader can absorb at a glance, and in doing so they inevitably discard much of what makes the reality complicated. It may be that the status update is less a report than a translation, and like any translation it involves choices about what to preserve and what to let go. I suspect that the choices we make, often without noticing, reveal a great deal about the relationship between those who do the work and those who oversee it.

Consider the familiar drift of colours in a project that is quietly going wrong. The status remains green for a long time, not because everything is well but because nobody is quite certain enough of the trouble to change it; it becomes amber only when the difficulty can no longer be ignored, and it turns red, if it ever does, at a point when very little can still be done. Why does this pattern recur so consistently? Part of the answer, arguably, is that the colours carry consequences — attention, scrutiny, sometimes an uncomfortable meeting — and people are understandably reluctant to invite those consequences on the basis of a suspicion. The format, which was designed to surface problems early, ends up encouraging people to report them late, since it offers no graceful way of saying that something feels wrong without yet being demonstrably so.

## Draft A

Anyone who has been asked to summarise the state of unfinished work will know how difficult it is to do so honestly. The situation itself is almost always untidy: some parts are going well, others are uncertain, a few are quietly worrying, and the relationships between them are not easy to explain in a sentence. The formats we use for reporting, however, tend to be simple by design. A single status, a short line of commentary, perhaps a percentage complete. These formats are convenient to read, but they flatten a messy picture into something tidier than it really is, and much of the detail that matters most is lost in the process.

The colour rating is perhaps the clearest example. Green, amber and red have the great virtue of being understood at a glance, and yet in practice they behave rather strangely. Projects tend to remain green for a surprisingly long time, sometimes long after the people working on them have begun to have doubts, and then to turn red quite suddenly, at a point when there is little left that anyone can do. Amber, which ought to be the most useful of the three, is used far less than one might expect.

Why should this be? I suspect the answer has less to do with the honesty of individuals than with what happens once a warning is raised. A project that reports a problem invites attention: questions, meetings, additional reporting, the sense of being watched. Those involved learn, reasonably enough, that it is easier to hold on to the reassuring colour for another week in the hope that things will recover. The format, in other words, does not merely fail to capture problems; it actively encourages people to postpone mentioning them. Perhaps the question worth asking is not how to make the colours more accurate, but how to make an early warning feel less like a confession.

## Draft B

Why is it so difficult to say honestly where a piece of unfinished work actually stands? It may be that the difficulty lies not with the people writing the report but with the format they are asked to fill in, which is usually far simpler than the situation it is meant to describe.

Unfinished work is messy by nature, a tangle of partial progress, open questions and risks whose size nobody can yet judge with much confidence. A simple report format flattens all of this into a few lines and a single rating, and in doing so it drops precisely the detail that a reader would most need in order to understand what is happening. What survives the flattening is, arguably, the least informative part of the picture.

There is also the peculiar behaviour of colour ratings, which you may have observed for yourself. A project will remain green for a surprisingly long time, sometimes well after those closest to it have begun to worry, and then turn red quite suddenly, at a point when it is too late for anyone to do much about it; the intermediate state that would have been most useful is rarely reported at all. The rating describes, I suspect, not the condition of the work but the confidence of the person reporting it.

It seems to me that the explanation is not dishonesty but incentive. A warning, once raised, tends to bring scrutiny, meetings and closer supervision, and so the format itself quietly encourages people to wait until they are certain before saying anything; by then the moment at which a warning could have helped has usually passed. Perhaps the most useful report is not the one that looks reassuring but the one that makes early doubt safe to express.

## Draft C

Reporting on finished work is easy. Reporting honestly on work that is still in progress is much harder, and most reporting formats make it harder still.

The typical status report is simple by design. A few fields, a percentage complete, perhaps a traffic-light colour. That simplicity is meant to make reports quick to write and quick to read. But the situation it describes is rarely simple. Unfinished work is messy: some parts are going well, others are stuck, some risks are growing and some have faded. A short format has no room for any of that. It flattens the picture into a single line, and the detail that would actually help a reader understand what is going on gets dropped. [example needed]

Colour ratings show the problem most clearly. A project sits at green for weeks, sometimes months, even as the people working on it grow uneasy. Then, abruptly, it turns red. From the outside it looks like something went wrong overnight. In reality the trouble built up slowly, but the rating stayed positive until the evidence could no longer be ignored. By the time it flips, the options for fixing the problem are few.

Why do ratings behave this way? Because raising a warning has consequences for the person who raises it. An amber or red status brings attention: extra meetings, closer questions, managers who want frequent updates. That scrutiny can be useful, but it is also uncomfortable and time-consuming. So people wait. They hope the issue will resolve itself, or that they can fix it before anyone needs to know. Keeping the status green buys a little more peace.

The format itself encourages this delay. When the only way to express concern is to change a colour that triggers scrutiny, people will hold off on changing it. A report that made room for early, low-key doubts would surface problems while there was still time to deal with them.
