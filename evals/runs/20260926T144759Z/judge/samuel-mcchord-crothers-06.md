# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-06

## The passage (by the author)

He writes: “I possess an uncommon share of sensibility, and at the same time maintain an equality of mind that is uncommon, particularly in the midst of those occurrences which are calculated to wound the feelings. I have learned fortitude in the school of adversity. In draining the cup of bitterness to its dregs, I have been taught to despise the occurrences of misfortune. This one thing I fully believe, that our happiness is more in our power than is generally thought, or at least we have the ability of preventing that misery which is so common to unfortunate situations. No state or condition in life, but from which we may (if we exercise that reason which the God of Nature has given us) draw comfort and happiness. We are too apt to be governed by the opinions of others, and if they think our circumstances unhappy, to consider them so ourselves, and of course make them so. The state of mind is the only criterion of happiness or misery.”

It was from this lofty point of view that Stephen Burroughs wrote the history of his own life. His tendency to didacticism interferes with the limpid flow of the narrative. Sometimes a whole chapter will be given over to moralizings, but the observations are never painful. They all reveal the author’s cheerful acquiescence in the inevitability of his own actions. Along with this there is the air of chastened surprise over the fact that he was made the object of persecution.

At the very beginning of the narrative one recognizes an independence which would do credit to a better man. In New England, clergymen have always been looked upon as making good ancestors, and Burroughs might have been pardoned if he had shown some family pride. From this weakness he was free. “I am,” he says, “the only son of a clergyman, living in Hanover, in the State of New Hampshire; and were any to expect merit from their parentage, I might justly look for that merit. But I am so far a Republican that I consider a man’s merit to rest entirely with himself, without any regard to family, blood, or connection.”

## Draft A

Among the curiosities of early American writing is the autobiography of a swindler, a man who spent a good part of his life cheating others and then sat down to explain himself. What he produced is not a confession in any ordinary sense. It is closer to a philosophy, and a surprisingly cheerful one.

At its centre is a single idea that he returns to again and again. Contentment, he says, depends on one's own mind and not on the opinions of other people. A man who has made peace with himself need not care what the world says of him. Put that way, it sounds almost like the advice of a sage, and in another mouth it might be. In his, it has a convenient edge. A swindler has more reason than most to wish that other people's opinions did not count.

The memoir is full of moralizing, and the moralizing is delivered in good spirits. He offers reflections on conduct and character as though he were writing a book of instruction for the young. Alongside these, and without any sign that he notices the contrast, runs a steady note of surprise at how badly he has been treated. Again and again he seems astonished that people took offence, that they pursued him, that they did not see matters as he saw them. [example needed: a particular episode of mistreatment from the memoir] He is at once the teacher of virtue and its injured victim.

There is one more thread worth pulling. He was a minister's son, and a reader might expect him either to lean on that or to be shamed by it. He does neither. He rejected credit for family lineage altogether. A man, in his account, is what he makes of himself, and owes nothing to the respectability of his father. This fits neatly with the rest of his creed. If other people's opinions do not matter and one's ancestry does not matter, then only the self is left to answer to, and the self in his case was an indulgent judge.

The result is a strange and oddly likeable book: the story of a man who broke most of the rules while holding firmly to a philosophy that let him feel he had broken none.

## Draft B

Among the curiosities of early American literature there is the autobiography of a swindler, [name needed], who took the trouble to set down his life for the instruction of posterity. It is not a book that is often read, but it deserves to be. For it is the record of a man who had arrived at a philosophy of life which made him quite independent of the opinions of his fellow citizens.

Contentment, he held, depends upon one's own mind. It is not what other people think of us that matters, but what we think of ourselves. This is a sound doctrine, and it has been taught by the wisest of the moralists. The difficulty is that it may be applied in more ways than one. The Stoic used it to fortify himself against misfortune; our author used it to fortify himself against the reproaches of those whom he had cheated. If his conscience was at ease, why should he trouble himself about the sheriff?

The memoir is full of cheerful moralizing. Every chapter has its lesson, and the lessons are excellent. He discourses on the folly of the passions and the value of a well-regulated life with the air of a man who has thought much on these subjects. And side by side with these reflections there runs a note of genuine surprise. He cannot understand why people treat him so badly. He has done them no harm that he can see, and yet they persist in misunderstanding him. There is something almost touching in his bewilderment. He is like a child who has been punished for breaking a vase which, he is quite sure, was not a very good vase.

It must be remembered that he was a minister's son. A writer of a different temper would have made much of this, either to excuse his wanderings or to claim some share of his father's virtue. He does neither. He rejects any credit for his lineage. A man, he says, is what he makes himself, and he will take no borrowed honors. I am not disposed to quarrel with the principle. It is only that, having refused the credit of his family, he seems to have felt equally free to refuse the discredit of himself.

## Draft C

Among the minor classics of early American literature there is an autobiography by a swindler which deserves more attention than it has received. It is not a great book, and its author was not a great man, but it has the uncommon merit of setting forth a complete philosophy of life from the point of view of one who lived by his wits and at the expense of other people.

The philosophy is simple enough. Contentment, he tells us, depends upon the mind of the man himself and not upon the opinions of others. This is excellent doctrine, and it has been taught by sages in every age; the sages, however, did not usually mean it as a defence of sharp dealing. Our author applies it with a directness that the philosophers never contemplated. If the world thinks ill of him, that is the affair of the world. He has consulted his own mind and found it tolerably at peace.

What gives the memoir its peculiar flavour is the mixture of cheerful moralizing with a kind of injured astonishment. On one page he is dispensing wholesome advice to the young, in the manner of a schoolmaster who has never been caught at anything; on the next he is recording, with every sign of genuine surprise, that he has been harshly treated by persons who did not appreciate his motives. He cannot quite understand why they should have taken it so personally. The reader, who understands very well, is left to supply the explanation for himself.

But the most engaging passage concerns his family. He was a minister's son, and he might have made something of it, as lesser men have made careers out of a respectable father. He declines to do so. A man, he says, should stand on his own merits, and he will take no credit for the virtues of his ancestors. It is a sentiment that would do honour to a reformer. In his case it has a slightly different significance, for if he claims none of the family credit, he can hardly be expected to share any of the family scruples.

There is a whole moral system in that refusal, and I am not sure that it is entirely his own invention.
