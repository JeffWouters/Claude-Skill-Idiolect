# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-01

## The passage (by the author)

And all time past, was it all for this? Times unforgotten, and treasures of things?

What is the use of throwing great museums open to the people, if you tell them at the same time that to possess the contents of the museums would not make a private person happier? Why should there be cordons bleus in the world, if we ought to live on bread and milk? Above all, why have we praised, through the centuries, all the slow processes, the tardy consummations, of perfection, if raw material, either in art or life, is really best? I recall at this instant a friend of mine who expresses her democracy in her footwear. Her frocks are as charming as money can induce Paquin to make them; but if her frocks are an insult to the poor, her boots are an insult to the rich. I have seen her walk to a garden-party, in real lace, and out at heel. She fancied, I think, that her inadequate boots obliterated the deplorable social distinction between herself and her cook. In point of fact, her cook would not have condescended to them; would not have considered herself a “lady” if she had.

I have other friends who feel strongly the ignominy of personal service: who agree with many ignorant young women that it is more dignified to be a bullied, insulted, underpaid shop-girl with a rhinestone sunburst, than a well-paid, highly-respected parlor-maid in a uniform. Accordingly, they conscientiously deprive themselves of the parlor-maid, and spend her wages in trying to get a vote for the shop-girl. I do not understand their distinctions in liberty, or their definition of degradation. The parlor-maid at least can choose the mistress, but the shop-girl cannot choose the floor-walker.

I am, myself, essentially an undomestic woman, and I dislike the parlor-maid’s tasks to the point of feeling excessive irritation at having, occasionally, in this mad world, to perform them. But, seriously speaking, apart from the temperamental quirk, I would don her clothes and follow officially her career, rather than that parlor-maids in uniforms should pass wholly from the world. It is as if these people said, “Since those who are parlor-maids themselves cannot very well employ parlor-maids, then let no one have a parlor-maid.” Their factitious altruism, with all its peril, might be forgiven them; but the misguided creatures (who are human beings and egotists, after all, and as such must “save their face”) go on to say that it is really much nicer not to have parlor-maids. And that lie is unpardonable, for it strikes at the root of human experience. Parlor-maids would never have become a convention if they had not been found desirable.

## Draft A

There is a certain kind of well-meaning person--we all know her, and some of us have been her--who has decided that the surest road to equality lies through the kitchen door. She will keep no servant, because to keep a servant is to hold another human being beneath one; she will have no fine linen, no old silver, no tea served otherwise than out of the pot at the table, because refinement is a wall, and walls divide. I respect her motive. I do not think anything could be more honourable than her wish. But I am bound to say that she is mistaken, and mistaken on every count.

Let us begin with her clothes. She has put off her good frocks, very likely, and goes about in something plain and serviceable, so that no one shall feel the difference between them. But the difference is there, and every one feels it. Class is not a matter of what one wears; it is a matter of how one speaks, what one has read, what one takes for granted at breakfast. You cannot dress it away. The shabby coat on the educated woman deceives nobody--least of all the people it is meant to reassure. They know perfectly well that she could buy another to-morrow, and that she has chosen not to; and I am not sure they like her the better for the choice.

Then there is the matter of service. It is the fashion to speak as if domestic service were the last refuge of the downtrodden, and as if the girl behind the counter were a free woman by comparison. I wonder. The girl at the counter (and she is always held up as the lucky one!) stands all day under the eye of a floorwalker, at wages that must cover her room and her board; the cook in a decent household has both, and a kitchen of her own, and a mistress who--if she is worth anything--is a human being and not a system. I do not say that every place is a good place. I say that a good place may be a good deal freer than a good shop, and that the woman who refuses to employ anyone, on principle, has not freed a single girl from anything.

But what I find hardest to forgive is the talk. It is one thing to give up pretty things because one cannot afford them, or because one has not the time to care for them. It is quite another to give them up and then to announce that the bare table is "nicer," that the thick cup is "more honest," that one really prefers things so. One does not. Nobody does. The absence of a lovely thing is an absence, and to call it a presence is a small dishonesty that--frankly--I would rather not have at my table.

## Draft B

There is a certain kind of good person who has decided that equality begins at home, and that the way to begin it is to do without. Such people let their servants go, or never hire any. They put away the silver, dress in plain clothes, and speak of refinement as though it were a small moral failing they have overcome. Their motives are generous. Their reasoning, I think, is mistaken.

Consider first the matter of dress. A man who owns a good coat and chooses to wear a shabby one has not joined the ranks of those who own only shabby coats. Everyone can see the difference, and so can he. The distance between classes is made of education, habit, security and expectation, and none of these comes off with the jacket. Dressing down is a costume, and a costume worn in the name of fellowship tends to look, to the people it is meant to honour, rather like condescension. It changes the appearance of the wearer and nothing at all about the circumstances of anyone else.

Consider next the servants who are dismissed on principle. It is assumed that domestic service is a humiliating condition and that anyone rescued from it has been set free. But the alternatives are not freedom in the abstract; they are particular jobs. A girl who leaves a household for a shop counter may find herself standing for long hours under a manager's eye, paid little, housed badly, and answerable to strangers at every turn. In a decent household she might have had a room, regular meals, some variety in her work, and employers who knew her by name. I do not say every household is decent, or that every shop is cruel. I say only that service is not by its nature the worse condition, and that in many cases it is the freer one. To abolish the place in the name of the person who filled it is to make a gesture at her expense.

The last point is the one I find hardest to forgive. Having given up fine things, these reformers often go on to say that life is nicer without them. The plain table is nicer, the bare room is nicer, the evening with no one to wait on it is nicer. This is not true, and I suspect most of them know it. Beautiful objects, careful service and settled forms of courtesy are pleasant; that is why people have always wanted them. One may reasonably decide to give them up for a cause. One may not honestly pretend that the loss is a gain. The sacrifice, if it is a sacrifice, should be called one.

Equality is a serious aim. It deserves serious means, and a clear account of what it costs, rather than a change of clothes and a comfortable story about how little has been given up.

## Draft C

There is a kind of well-meaning person--I have met a good many of them--who has decided that equality begins at home, and begins, specifically, with the dismissal of the cook. She will have no servants; she will have no silver; she will wear the plainest serge she can find, and she will tell you that it is all for the sake of the brotherhood of man. I do not doubt her sincerity. I doubt her logic. For the plain truth is that dressing down does not erase class differences; it merely advertises them in a new way. The girl behind the counter knows perfectly well that the lady in the shabby coat could buy the shop if she chose, and she is not in the least flattered by the shabbiness. One is not made the equal of any one by being badly dressed. One is only made uncomfortable.

Some one will object that service is itself degrading, and that no free-born person ought to cook another's dinner. Granted: the thing has been abused, and there are mistresses (Heaven forgive them!) who deserve to cook their own. But compare the housemaid with the shop-girl. The housemaid has a roof, a table, a known round of duties, and, on the whole, a household that knows her name; the shop-girl has her feet, her wage, and a floor-walker. I fancy that if one asked the two of them honestly which was the freer life, the answer would not always be the one our reformers expect. I know of [example needed: a real servant who chose service over the counter], and I doubt she is alone. Domestic service can be freer than shop work. It isn't fashionable to say so; that does not make it untrue.

What I cannot forgive is the pretence. The well-meaning person does not say, "I have given up my pretty things because I think it my duty." That would be honest, and a little heroic. She says instead that bare floors and thick crockery are nicer--simpler, cleaner, more sincere, as if a porcelain cup had ever told a lie. Well: they are not nicer. They are merely plainer, and to call the absence of a thing a positive good is a trick of the tongue, not a principle. My point is only this: renunciation may be a virtue, but it ceases to be one the moment it pretends to be a pleasure. We may give up refinement if we must; let us not, for pity's sake, tell ourselves we have gained something by the loss. That is not equality. It is dishonesty in homespun.
