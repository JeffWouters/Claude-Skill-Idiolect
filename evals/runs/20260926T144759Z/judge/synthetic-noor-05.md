# Judge packet synthetic-noor-05

## The passage (by the author)

I have a pair of wool socks that are 12 years old. They have been darned nine times. I can see every repair.

They were grey when I bought them. Now they are grey with patches of green, brown and one of bright red. The red was all I had that night. I do not regret it.

Darning is not hard. It is slow. That is different.

You need three things. A darning needle, which is long with a big eye. Wool, close to the weight of the sock. And something round to hold the sock tight while you work. There are wooden darning mushrooms made for this. I use a light bulb. An old one, dead, the big round kind. It fits inside the heel just right.

So here is how I do it.

First I turn the sock the right way out and find the hole. Heels go first on my socks. Then the ball of the foot. Then the big toe. The hole is rarely one clean hole. There is a hole, and around it a wide ring of thin, worn wool. You have to darn the thin part as well. Or next month the hole is back, one ring wider.

I slip the bulb in and pull the sock tight over it. Then I run a line of small stitches round the thin part, about one centimetre out from the edge. This marks the patch. It also stops it from spreading.

Then I lay the warp. That is a set of long stitches going one way, top to bottom, across the hole. Side by side. Close together. Each one picks up a little of the good wool at the top and bottom. On a hole the size of a coin, that is about 20 lines.

It looks like the strings of a small harp.

## Draft A

Old wool socks wear out in predictable places, usually the heel and the toe, and throwing them away the moment a hole appears feels wasteful. Darning is the traditional answer, and it needs less than you might think.

The kit is short. You need a long needle, long enough to span the patch you are going to make with room to spare. You need yarn that matches the sock, both in colour, if you care about that, and in weight and fibre, so the mend behaves like the rest of the fabric. And you need something rounded to stretch the sock over while you work. Proper darning mushrooms and eggs exist, but an old light bulb does the job just as well. It holds the fabric in its natural curve and gives the needle something firm to glide against.

Before you begin, look beyond the hole itself. The hole is only the most obvious symptom. The wool around it has usually been thinning for a while, and if you mend just the gap, the next hole will open right beside your repair. So the patch needs to cover the weakened area surrounding the hole as well, reaching into fabric that is still sound. Hold the sock up to the light: thin areas show clearly.

Start by outlining the patch. Using small running stitches, sew a shape around the damaged area, a little way into the good wool on every side. This outline marks the boundary of your work and anchors the edges of the repair.

Then lay the first layer: parallel vertical threads running across the patch from one side of the outline to the other. Keep them close together and evenly spaced, and pick up a little of the sound fabric at each end so they are firmly anchored. Where they cross the hole, they simply bridge the gap. Do not pull them tight; they should lie flat against the curve of the bulb.

These vertical threads are the warp of your new fabric. The next step is to weave through them.

## Draft B

I darn my socks. Not all of them. The wool ones. The old ones worth the time.

You do not need much. Here is the list. A long needle with a big eye. Yarn that matches the sock or near enough. And something round to hold the sock in shape. A darning mushroom if you have one. An old light bulb if you do not. The bulb works just as well. It is smooth and the right size for a heel.

First look at the hole. Then look past it. A hole in a sock does not come from nowhere. The wool around it has been wearing thin for a while. Hold the sock up to the light and you will see it. A thin pale patch much bigger than the hole itself. That is the part to mend. Not just the gap.

If you close only the hole, the thin wool next to it goes next. Right beside the neat new patch. [real failed darn needed]. So I mend the whole weak area. It takes longer. It lasts.

So the sock goes over the bulb with the hole on top. Pull it snug so the knit lies flat. Not stretched. Just flat.

Then mark out the patch. I run a line of small stitches around the whole weak area. A box, roughly. It should sit well into the good wool on every side. That line is the edge of the darn. Everything happens inside it.

Then the first layer. Start at one side of the box and lay a thread straight up across it. Then back down, next to the first. Then up again. Rows of parallel threads close together. From one side of the box to the other. Where there is still wool, you stitch through it. Where there is a hole, the thread just spans the gap.

That is half the job. The other half goes across.

Tonight the sock is on the bulb. One layer done.

## Draft C

Wool socks go at the heel first. Then the toe. Mine do, anyway. A small hole at first. Then a bigger one. You can throw them out. Or you can darn them.

You need three things. A long needle, with an eye big enough for yarn. Yarn that matches the sock. If it is too thick, you will feel the lump every time you walk. If it is too thin, it will not hold. And something round to stretch the sock over. I use an old light bulb. It is the right shape, it is smooth, and it was in the drawer anyway.

Push the bulb into the sock and bring the hole up over the top of it. The wool should be stretched, but not tight. Now look at the hole. Then look past it.

This is the part people miss. The hole is not the problem. The hole is the end of the problem. Around it, the wool has gone thin. That is the next hole. If you only fill the gap, the darn pulls on the thin wool around it, and the thin wool gives. Then you have a bigger hole with a darn in the middle of it.

So you mend the whole weak patch. Not only the gap.

First mark out the area. I run a line of small running stitches round it, well out into the good wool. [how far out needed] That is the boundary.

Then the vertical threads. Start at one side of the boundary. Take a small stitch in the good wool at the top. Carry the yarn straight down, across the hole, and take a small stitch in the good wool at the bottom. Turn. Back up again, right next to the first thread. Down again. Up again. Keep the threads parallel. Keep them close. Side to side, until the whole patch is covered.

That is half the darn. The other half goes across.
