# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-08

## The passage (by the author)

Humor is not usually looked upon as a civic virtue. It is for the most part confined to a modest sphere of usefulness, and is accepted as an alleviation to the lot of the private man. He learns to find pleasure in his small misadventures and to smile amiably at his discomfitures. The most ancient pleasantries have almost always an element of domesticity. They form the silver lining to the clouds that sometimes gather over the most peaceful homes. What comfort an ancient Hebrew must have taken in the text from Ecclesiasticus: “As climbing up a sandy way is to the feet of the aged, so is a wife full of words to a quiet man.” The quiet man would murmur to himself, “How true!” He would seize the simile as a dog snatches a bone, and would carry it off to enjoy it by himself.

But it would never occur to him to treat the large affairs of the community in this fashion. Here everything seems too dignified to allow of pleasant conceits. The quiet man could not treat the prolixity of his social superiors as he could the too long drawn out wisdom of his wife. He must take it, as he would take the invariable laws of nature, with unsmiling acquiescence. Lord Bacon in his list of works that ought to be undertaken declared the need of one to be entitled “Sober Satire; or the Insides of Things.” Such sober satire might express the moods of a philosophical statesman, who could contrast the inside of great affairs with the outside. It implies a certain familiarity with the institutions of society which the common man does not possess.

Now and then, however, there is a reversal of the usual relation. The community is of such a nature that each member can see through it and all around it. The ordinary citizen becomes a philosopher indulging habitually in sober satire. He knows that things are not as they seem, and is pleased at the discovery. In such a case humor envelops everything and becomes the last word of sociological wisdom.

## Draft A

We are accustomed to think of humour as a private virtue, something like good temper or a sound digestion, which makes a man pleasant to live with and is of no particular concern to the state. The traditional joke bears this out. It deals with the mother-in-law, the unpaid bill, the leaking roof and the husband who comes home late; it seldom ventures beyond the front door. When it does, it is generally regarded as having strayed into territory where it has no business.

But I should like to put in a plea for humour as a public virtue. There is a large department of life in which it is very rarely exercised, and that is the department of institutions. The ordinary citizen approaches the great institutions of his society much as a pilgrim approaches a shrine. He does not presume to understand them. He accepts the courts, the legislature, the ceremonies of office and the solemn language of official documents with a gravity that is entirely sincere, and it does not occur to him that any of them might be funny. Solemnity, in fact, is the tax he pays for living in a civilized country, and he pays it without complaint.

This is all very well so long as the institutions deserve it. Some of them do. But solemn acceptance has the disadvantage that it cannot tell the difference between the institution that deserves respect and the one that merely demands it. It is here that humour becomes useful. The man who can laugh at a pompous ceremony has not necessarily lost his reverence; he has only recovered his sense of proportion.

Where a whole people has learned to see through its own society, a curious thing happens. Wit ceases to be the private possession of a few clever persons and becomes a kind of shared wisdom. The jest passes from hand to hand, like a coin of the realm, and everybody knows its value. Pretension finds it harder to get a hearing. I do not say that such a people is necessarily better governed than another. But it is a good deal harder to impose upon, and that is perhaps the next best thing.

## Draft B

We are accustomed to think of humor as a private virtue. It belongs to the fireside. It is the grace which enables a man to bear the small annoyances of domestic life, and to see the absurdity of his own situation when the chimney smokes or the cook gives notice. The traditional joke has to do with the mother-in-law, the unpaid bill, the husband who comes home late. These are household troubles, and humor is the household remedy for them.

But when we pass from the household to the state, the jokes stop. The ordinary man, who can laugh at his own misfortunes readily enough, becomes solemn in the presence of great institutions. He accepts them as he accepts the weather. The government, the courts, the church, the established order of society, are to him not things to be understood, but things to be endured or revered. He may grumble at them, but he does not see through them. And what we do not see through we cannot laugh at.

This is, perhaps, as it should be in a society where the institutions are handed down from above. The citizen who has nothing to say about the government has no occasion to be amused by it. It is not his affair. But where the citizens make their own institutions, the case is different. They know how these things are put together, for they have put them together themselves. They know that the imposing edifice rests on a good many compromises, and that the dignified official is a neighbor who was elected by a narrow margin.

In such a society humor becomes a public virtue. It is no longer merely the private consolation of the individual; it is a form of common wisdom. When a whole community can laugh together at its own pretensions, it has gained something which is of more value than solemnity. It has gained a sense of proportion. It is not likely to be carried away by its own oratory, nor to mistake a passing fashion for an eternal truth.

It must be confessed that this kind of humor is not so common as we could wish. It requires a clear sight and a good temper, and these are not always found together.

## Draft C

We usually think of humor as a private virtue, something that makes a good companion or a bearable household. It belongs, in the common view, to the kitchen and the parlour rather than to the town hall. There is a case, though, for treating it as a public virtue as well, and perhaps as one of the more important ones.

Consider what jokes have traditionally been about. They concern the troubles of the household: the difficult relative, the burnt dinner, the quarrel between husband and wife, the debts, the neighbours. These are the subjects people feel free to laugh at, because they are close at hand and because laughing at them does no harm to anything larger. Civic matters are another thing. The law, the government, the church, the great institutions of the country are not, as a rule, the stuff of ordinary jokes.

That is because ordinary people usually treat grand institutions with a kind of solemn acceptance. They may grumble, but they do not mock. The institution is simply there, like the weather, and it seems both useless and slightly improper to find it funny. Solemnity is the default attitude toward anything big enough to feel permanent.

Where that attitude breaks down, something interesting happens. In a society whose citizens can see through it, who understand how its institutions actually work and where they fall short of their own pretensions, humor moves out of the house and into the street. People begin to laugh together at public things, and the laughter is not merely disrespect. It is a form of understanding. A shared joke about the government is often a shared judgment about it, arrived at quickly and held in common.

In such a place, wit becomes a kind of shared wisdom. It lets a community say what it knows without solemnity and without anger. It keeps institutions honest by reminding them that they are watched by people who are not overawed. And it binds citizens to one another, because to laugh at the same thing is to see the same thing. [example needed: a community where this has been observed] Humor of this public kind is not a distraction from civic life. At its best it is one of civic life's most useful tools.
