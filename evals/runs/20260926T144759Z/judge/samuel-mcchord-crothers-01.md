# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-01

## The passage (by the author)

That’s the difficulty! The hardest thing about a good policy is to get it accepted by the people who have the power. What avails the wisdom of the old men when all the young men are “spoiling for a fight?” Something more is needed than statesman-like plans for strengthening the framework of civilization. You may have a fireproof structure, but you are not safe so long as it is crammed with highly inflammable material.

There is a periodicity in the passion for war. It marks the coming into power of a new generation. A quarter of a century from now “the good gray poet” Rudyard Kipling may be singing sweet lyrics of peace. All things come in time. The Kipling we know simply utters the sentiments of “the young men brought up with him.” What he has been to his contemporaries Tennyson was to the generation before. Kipling never wrote a more scornful arraignment of peace or a more passionate glorification of war than Tennyson’s “Maud.”

We are listening to the invective of a youth whose aspirations have been crushed and ideals shattered by a civilization that seems to him to be soulless. He has seen something which to him is infinitely more cruel than the battle between contending hosts

Why do they prate of the blessings of peace? we have made them a curse, Pickpockets, each hand lusting for all that is not its own; And lust of gain, in the spirit of Cain, is it better or worse Than the heart of the citizen hissing in war on his own hearthstone?

We are made to see the inglorious peace in which men seek only their own ease.

Peace sitting under her olive, and slurring the days gone by, When the poor are hovell’d and hustled together, each sex, like swine, When only the ledger lives, and when only not all men lie.

## Draft A

It is easy enough to draw up a plan for keeping the peace. Any committee of reasonable men, shut up in a quiet room with pens and paper, can do it in an afternoon. The plan will be sound. It will provide for arbitration and conciliation and the orderly settlement of disputes, and it will have the great merit of being agreeable to everybody who is present. The difficulty is with those who are not present.

For a policy, however excellent, is only a piece of paper until it is accepted by those who hold the power to carry it out or to tear it up. We are apt to forget this. We think that when we have convinced ourselves we have convinced the world, and we are surprised to find that the world has meanwhile grown a year older and taken on a new set of opinions.

The truth is that the love of fighting is not a thing that can be abolished once for all, like a bad law. It comes back with the regularity of the seasons. Each generation, as it comes to its strength, discovers the old enthusiasm for itself. It does not get it out of books; it gets it out of its own blood. The young men who were children when the last war ended know nothing of its miseries, and they have heard a great deal of its glories. It must be confessed that the glories make the better story.

Nor is this feeling confined to the thoughtless. A famous Victorian poet, in lines [example needed], declared that a greedy and selfish peace was worse than war. The shopkeeper who cheated his neighbor behind his counter was, to his mind, a more shameful figure than the soldier who met his enemy in the open field. There was just enough truth in this to make it dangerous. When peace means only the quiet getting of gain, it is not surprising that a generation eager for something nobler should mistake the battlefield for the high road to virtue.

## Draft B

It is a comfortable belief that a good plan for peace, once drawn up and agreed, will keep the peace. The plan may be sound in every clause. It may have been argued over by careful people, weighed against past failures, and approved by everyone who read it through. None of that settles the matter. A policy is only as strong as the willingness of those who hold power to accept it, and power does not stay in the same hands for long.

This is the difficulty that the drafters of peace rarely face squarely. They write for the people in the room, and the people in the room are, for the most part, old enough to remember what the last war cost. They nod at the sensible arguments because they have lived through the thing the arguments describe. But the room empties, and a new cohort grows up outside it. That cohort did not bury anyone. It has read about battle rather than seen it, and what it has read is mostly glory.

Enthusiasm for combat returns in cycles, as regularly as each generation comes of age. It is not stamped out by argument, because it was never founded on argument. It is a feeling, and it rises in young people at about the time they begin to want something larger than their own lives to belong to. Peace, seen from that angle, can look small and mean.

A famous Victorian poem said as much, and said it with force. It condemned the greedy, selfish habits of peacetime as worse than battle itself, a kind of slow rot that war would sweep away. Readers who admired the poem were not all fools or brutes. They were answering a real complaint about what ordinary peaceful life can become.

That is why the plan on paper fails. It persuades the people who already agree with it, and it leaves untouched the young, who have not yet been asked and would not listen if they were.

## Draft C

It is not difficult to draw up an excellent plan for peace. The difficulty lies elsewhere. A policy is only as sound as the willingness of those who hold power to accept it, and the persons who hold power are not always the persons who have read the plans. We may convince the statesman and the scholar. We have still to convince the young man who has never seen a war and would like to see one.

For the love of fighting is not a disease that is abolished once and for all. It returns in cycles, with the regularity of the seasons, because each new generation must discover it for itself and will not take it on trust from its elders. The fathers have had their war and are tired of it. The sons have had nothing but the fathers' stories, which have been much improved in the telling. So a cohort grows up, looks about it and finds the world dull. It is then that the drum begins to sound attractive.

But it would be a mistake to dismiss this feeling as mere folly. It has its philosophy. A famous Victorian poem stated the case, and its argument was that peace is no blessing when it means a nation given over to greed, in which every man looks after his own shop and cares nothing for his neighbour. Such a peace, the poet declared, was worse than battle. [quotation needed from the poem]

That is a hard saying, and there is something in it. Selfishness in time of peace is not an edifying spectacle. Yet the remedy proposed is a strange one, and it resembles nothing so much as the conduct of a householder who, finding his rooms untidy, resolves to set fire to the building. The young are not mistaken in disliking the sordid peace; they are mistaken only in supposing that war is its opposite. The business of those who plan for peace is perhaps to make it a little less dull. Until they succeed, the young will continue to prefer the alternative.
