# Judge packet synthetic-noor-06

## The passage (by the author)

The kitchen tap started to drip in March. One drop every four seconds. I timed it with the clock on the oven.

At first I did nothing. A drip is easy to live with. You stop hearing it after a day or two.

But then I put a jug under it. By morning the jug held 600 millilitres. So that was the end of living with it.

I have fixed taps before. Not this one. This one is old, with a brass body and two cross-shaped handles. Hot on the left. Cold on the right. The drip came from the cold side.

So on Saturday I cleared the counter. I laid a tea towel in the sink. That is the first rule: cover the plughole. Small parts fall. They always fall.

Then I turned off the water. There is a stop valve under the sink, behind the bleach and a bag of old sponges. It had not been turned in years. It took both hands and a cloth for grip. Then it gave.

I opened the tap to drain it. A cough of air. A thin run of water. Then nothing.

The handle came off with one screw under a small blue cap. I lifted the cap with the tip of a knife. The screw was a flat head, soft with age. I used the right driver and pushed hard as I turned. It came out whole.

Under the handle sat the headgear. That is the brass part that holds the washer. It takes a spanner. Mine is an adjustable one, 20 centimetres long, from my father's box. I set it snug on the flats and held the tap body with my other hand. You have to hold the body. Or the whole tap turns in the counter and you make a new problem.

## Draft A

The tap had been dripping for weeks. An old brass one, the kitchen tap. I had got used to the sound. Then one night I put a jug under the spout before bed. In the morning it had [amount needed] in it. That is not a drip. That is water down the drain every hour of every day. So that was that.

First, the drain. Put the plug in. Then lay a cloth over the plug. Small parts from a tap are exactly the size of a plughole. If one falls, you want it to land on cloth, not go down the pipe.

Then the water. The shut-off valve for the tap was under the sink, behind the bottles and the bucket. It had not been turned in years. It was stiff. I did not force it with a wrench. I worked it back and forth by hand, a little each way, until it gave. Then I turned it off all the way. Then I opened the tap to check. A cough of air, a last trickle, then nothing. Good.

Now the handle. There was a cap on top. I prised it off with a small flat screwdriver. Under it, a screw. Out came the screw and the handle lifted off. Everything went on a tea towel, in a row, in the order it came off.

Then the valve assembly. This is where you have to be careful. The nut is tight, and when you turn it, the whole tap wants to turn with it. Brass is soft, and an old tap is not as solid on the sink as it looks. If it twists, you can crack the seal underneath or strain the pipes. So brace it. One hand holds the body of the tap still. The other turns the spanner. Short, steady pulls. No jerking.

It came loose on the [number needed] try. Then it turned by hand. Then it was out, and I could see the seal at the bottom.

## Draft B

Our old brass kitchen faucet had been dripping, and I kept telling myself it was not worth the trouble. A drip is small. Then, one evening, I put a container under the spout and left it overnight. In the morning it held far more than I expected [example needed: the amount collected]. That was the end of the debate. A slow drip, multiplied by every hour of every day, is real water going straight down the drain.

The temptation with a job like this is to grab a wrench and start. I have learned to resist it, because the preparation is what keeps a small repair small.

First, protect the drain. Put the plug in, or lay a cloth over the opening. Faucets are full of small parts, and the one you drop will always be the one that rolls towards the drain.

Next, shut off the water. Our supply valve under the sink had not been touched in years and was stiff. It is worth being patient here: turn it firmly but steadily rather than forcing it, because a supply valve that breaks is a much bigger problem than a dripping faucet. Once it is closed, open the faucet to release the pressure and let the last of the water run out.

Then comes the disassembly. The handle comes off first, usually after removing a cap and a screw. Underneath is the valve assembly, which is what you are really after. Here is the one lesson I would pass on: brace the faucet body while you loosen the assembly. Old brass can be seized in place, and if you just put a wrench on the valve and turn, the whole faucet may rotate with it, twisting the connections underneath. Hold the body steady with one hand, or with a second wrench, and turn the assembly with the other. Go slowly, and lay the parts out in the order they came off.

With the valve assembly out, the worn seal was finally within reach.
