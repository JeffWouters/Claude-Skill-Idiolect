## The passage (by the author)

There is a curious psychology to all this. A deadline that is widely understood to be arbitrary can nevertheless exert enormous force, because once it has been announced it acquires a reputational dimension; missing it would mean admitting that the original plan was wrong, and the people who made the plan may be more invested in its correctness than in its outcome. You have perhaps seen this dynamic play out, in which a date that everyone privately believes is unrealistic is publicly defended until the last possible moment, at which point it is quietly moved and the whole exercise begins again. The consequence is a kind of institutional theatre, in which the deadline functions less as a planning tool than as a performance of confidence. I suspect that most participants in this theatre understand its nature perfectly well; they simply feel unable to be the one who stops the performance.

Is there a better way to relate to deadlines? It seems to me that the most important step is simply to be honest about their origins — to ask, whenever a date is proposed, what would actually happen if it were missed, and to allow the answer to shape how seriously the date is treated. A deadline tied to an unmovable external event deserves one kind of response; a deadline chosen because it seemed like a reasonable target deserves another, and conflating the two does a disservice to everyone involved. Perhaps we might also learn to renegotiate dates earlier and more openly, treating a revised deadline not as a failure of planning but as the natural result of learning more about the work. None of this is technically difficult, and none of it requires new tools or elaborate methods. What makes it difficult is the reluctance to disappoint, which is, in some sense, a reluctance to be honest.

## Draft A

How does a date that somebody simply made up come to carry so much weight? It may be that the date itself matters far less than the act of announcing it, since once a deadline has been spoken aloud in front of colleagues it becomes a question of saving face, and a team will defend it well past the point at which the reasons for doing so have quietly run out.

Consider what happens when such a date begins to look doubtful. Nobody wishes to be the first to say so, because saying so feels like admitting a failure that has not yet occurred; the team works longer, trims what it can and hopes, perhaps, that the gap will close by itself. The date was never a measurement of anything, yet it is treated as though breaking it would reveal something shameful about the people involved.

There is also a simpler question that is seldom asked, which is what would actually happen if the date slipped. Sometimes the answer is genuinely serious, with a customer, a contract or another team depending on it; sometimes, I suspect more often than we admit, the answer is that almost nothing would happen at all, beyond a mild disappointment and an adjusted plan. Weighing a date by its real consequences, rather than by the discomfort of moving it, is arguably the most useful habit a team can develop.

It seems to me that the better practice is to revise dates early and openly, as soon as the evidence suggests they will not hold, when a change is still inexpensive and nobody has yet exhausted themselves defending it. What makes this difficult is rarely the planning itself; it is the fear of letting people down, which grows stronger the longer the conversation is postponed. Perhaps the kindest thing a team can do for those waiting on it is not to keep every promise but to tell them sooner when a promise will not be kept.

## Draft B

Many deadlines start life as a rough idea. Someone picks a date that sounds reasonable, says it in a meeting, and moves on. A week later that date is in a slide deck. A month later it is something the team has committed to, even though nobody ever sat down and worked out whether it was possible.

The moment a date is announced, it changes character. It stops being a planning assumption and becomes a matter of saving face. The person who named it does not want to look careless, the team does not want to look slow, and the manager above them does not want to explain a change upward. So everyone defends the date, often well past the point where the facts have moved against it. Weekends get sacrificed and scope gets quietly trimmed, all to protect a number that was invented in the first place.

There is a better way to deal with this, and it starts with a plain question: what actually happens if this date slips? Sometimes the answer is serious. A contract penalty, a regulatory window, a customer who is counting on it. Sometimes the answer is that a presentation gets moved and a few people are mildly disappointed. Those are very different situations, and they deserve very different responses. Weigh the date by its real consequences, not by how firmly it was announced.

The second step is to revise early. A date that moves two months ahead of time is an adjustment. A date that moves two days before it arrives is a crisis. Most teams know long before the end that a date is in trouble, and they still wait. [example needed]

The real obstacle is rarely the plan. It is the fear of letting people down. Saying out loud that a date will not hold feels like admitting failure. But holding on to it until it breaks disappoints people far more, and far later, when there is little left anyone can do.

## Draft C

There is something curious about the way an arbitrary due date, once spoken aloud, begins to acquire a life of its own. It may have been chosen almost at random, in a meeting where someone simply needed a date to write down, and yet within a few weeks it can come to feel as immovable as a law of nature. I suspect the reason has less to do with the work than with the people who announced it. Once a date has been shared, it ceases to be a planning assumption and becomes a matter of reputation; to move it would be to admit that one had been wrong, and so teams defend it well past the point at which any reasonable person would have let it go.

How might we treat such dates more wisely? The most useful question, I think, is also the simplest: what would actually happen if this date were to slip? Sometimes the honest answer is that a great deal would happen, that a customer is waiting or that other work depends on it, and in that case the date deserves to be taken seriously. Often, though, the answer turns out to be rather modest, a mild disappointment or a rearranged plan, and it seems only sensible to weigh the date accordingly rather than treating every deadline as though the same consequences hung upon it.

Where a date does need to change, the kindest course is to change it early, while there is still room for others to adjust. This is easy to say and surprisingly hard to do. The obstacle is rarely a lack of information, since the people doing the work usually know long before anyone else that the date is in trouble. It is, I think, the fear of letting people down, and the sense that raising the matter now would be a kind of betrayal. Perhaps the greater betrayal is to stay silent until the date arrives and passes.
