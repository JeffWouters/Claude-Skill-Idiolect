# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-02

## The passage (by the author)

Now I do not mean to say that all scientists despise culture. That would be silly and untrue. But the “scientific” obsession has changed all rankings in the intellectual world. The insidiousness of science lies in its claim to be not a subject, but a method. You could ignore a subject: no subject is all-inclusive. But a method can plausibly be applied to anything within the field of consciousness. Small wonder that the study of literature turns into philology, the study of history into archæology, and the study of morals and æsthetics into physical psychology. With the finer appeals of philosophy and poetry and painting and natural beauty, science need not meddle; because about their direct effect on the thought and wills of men if can say nothing valuable. You cannot determine the value of a Velasquez by putting your finger on the pulse of the man who is looking at it; or the value of Amiens Cathedral by registering the vibration of his internal muscles; or of the Grand Cañon of the Colorado by declaring that all perception of beauty is a function of sex. Nor does it matter very much, at the moment, to the enraptured reader or observer that such and such a work of art was the logical result of a given set of conditions. The point is that it is there; and that it works potently upon us in ways which we can scarce phrase. Culture puts us disinterestedly in communication with the distilled and sifted lore of the world. Science is in comparison a prejudiced affair--prejudiced because it seeks always to bring things back to literal and physical explanations. Far be it from me to deny that geology, biology, physics, have given us unapprehended vistas down which to stray--only, strictly speaking, it forbids the straying. The moment the layman’s imagination begins to profit, begins to get real exhilaration from scientific discoveries, it contributes something unwelcome to science. Science has its own stern value; in the end we are all profoundly affected by its gains in the field of fact. One’s quarrel is not with science as such, but with science as demanding an intellectual and spiritual hegemony. With nothing less than hegemony, however, will science be content.

## Draft A

It is not science I quarrel with; it is science with a crown on its head. The laboratory has done wonders, and I, for one, am grateful for every one of them. But somewhere along the way the method that serves so admirably for weighing gases has come to fancy itself the only honest way of knowing anything at all--and the humanities, poor things, have been hurrying to oblige it. It is perhaps natural that the younger studies should envy the elder its prestige; it is rather less natural that they should sell their own birthright to get it. Literature is no longer read; it is "investigated." Poetry is counted by its syllables, its sources are tabulated, its variants are collated in footnotes, and the student emerges knowing a good deal about the poem and nothing whatever of it. The study has become technical because only the technical can be measured, and only what can be measured is now thought respectable.

Some one will say that this is mere sentiment; that a thing which cannot be tested cannot be known. Granted: beauty is not to be weighed. But that is the whole point. You may measure the canvas, analyse the pigment, and examine the brush-strokes under a glass, and you will be no nearer to knowing whether the picture is good. Beauty is not a physical property, and no instrument yet devised has detected it. Has not every generation known this without being told? I venture to say that the man who judges a sonnet by its metrical statistics would judge a sunset by its temperature.

The point is that science is a method, not a philosophy of everything. I have no wish to see it banished from the university; I should be the first to mourn it. What I object to is its claim to dominion tout court--the assumption, so quietly made that nobody troubles to defend it, that whatever lies outside the laboratory lies outside knowledge. That is not science. It is conquest, and culture is the province it has annexed. We shan't get it back by pretending the annexation never happened.

## Draft B

Science no longer asks to be treated as one way of knowing among several. It presents itself as the way of knowing, a universal method that any subject may adopt and every subject should. The result has been a quiet displacement of culture, carried out less by argument than by imitation.

Look at what has happened to the humanities. The study of literature, history and languages has been pressed into the shape of a laboratory discipline. What can be counted is counted; what can be classified is classified; and what resists both is set aside as unscholarly. A poem becomes an occasion for tracing sources, tabulating forms and establishing dates. These are respectable tasks, but they are the scaffolding of the subject, not the subject. A student can master all of them and never learn why the poem was worth reading. The technical work has grown until it fills the room, and the thing it was meant to serve has been shown out.

The trouble is that the most important questions in art are not questions of measurement. Whether a picture is beautiful, whether a sentence is well made, whether a piece of music moves us for good reasons or cheap ones: none of this can be settled with instruments. Physical measurement can tell us the dimensions of a canvas and the chemistry of its pigments. It cannot tell us what the painting is worth. Judgment of that kind depends on trained perception, long acquaintance and comparison, and a willingness to commit oneself to an opinion that cannot be proved. To call such judgment unscientific is accurate. To call it therefore worthless is a confusion.

I want to be plain about what I am objecting to. I have no quarrel with science in its own field. Its methods have given us knowledge of the physical world that no earlier age possessed, and it would be foolish to wish that undone. My complaint is against a claim that science makes, or that is made on its behalf: that its method is the only respectable form of thought, and that every other kind of inquiry must either become scientific or admit itself to be mere feeling. That claim is not itself a scientific finding. It is an ambition for total intellectual dominance, and culture is one of the things it has cost us.

## Draft C

It is a curious thing, when one comes to think of it, how quietly science has walked in and taken the chair at the head of the table. It did not ask; it did not need to ask. It presented itself simply as the method--the one road to knowledge, good for every country and every subject--and we, being modest people and a little dazzled, moved down to make room. Culture, which used to sit there, is now somewhere below the salt.

You may see the thing happening in any university catalogue. The humanities have not been abolished; nobody would be so crude. They have merely been made respectable, which is worse. Literature has become the counting of syllables and the tracing of sources; history has become the tabulation of prices; even poetry, I am told, may now be studied by the chart. Whatever can be measured is kept, and whatever cannot is left out as unscholarly. The student learns a great deal about a poem, and nothing whatever of why any one should wish to read it.

Now I may be dull, but I have never been able to weigh beauty. One may measure the canvas and analyse the pigments; one may count the lines of the sonnet and describe the pause in each. When all that is done, the question of whether the picture is good or the sonnet lovely has not so much as been touched. It is not a physical question, and no instrument will ever answer it. Taste is a judgment--a trained and disciplined judgment, if you like, but a judgment all the same--and it belongs to a different order of things.

Let me not be misunderstood. I have nothing against science; I should be an ungrateful creature if I had. Let it have the laboratory, by all means, and the hospital, and the bridge; it has earned them. What I object to is not science, but science with a crown on its head: the claim that its method is the only method, and that what it cannot see is not there. That is not a scientific claim at all. It is merely an imperialism, and like other imperialisms it makes the conquered provinces very poor.
