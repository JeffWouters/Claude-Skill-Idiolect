Why is it so difficult to say honestly where a piece of unfinished work actually stands? It may be that the difficulty lies not with the people writing the report but with the format they are asked to fill in, which is usually far simpler than the situation it is meant to describe.

Unfinished work is messy by nature, a tangle of partial progress, open questions and risks whose size nobody can yet judge with much confidence. A simple report format flattens all of this into a few lines and a single rating, and in doing so it drops precisely the detail that a reader would most need in order to understand what is happening. What survives the flattening is, arguably, the least informative part of the picture.

There is also the peculiar behaviour of colour ratings, which you may have observed for yourself. A project will remain green for a surprisingly long time, sometimes well after those closest to it have begun to worry, and then turn red quite suddenly, at a point when it is too late for anyone to do much about it; the intermediate state that would have been most useful is rarely reported at all. The rating describes, I suspect, not the condition of the work but the confidence of the person reporting it.

It seems to me that the explanation is not dishonesty but incentive. A warning, once raised, tends to bring scrutiny, meetings and closer supervision, and so the format itself quietly encourages people to wait until they are certain before saying anything; by then the moment at which a warning could have helped has usually passed. Perhaps the most useful report is not the one that looks reassuring but the one that makes early doubt safe to express.
