What does a progress report actually tell the people who read it? It may be that it tells them a great deal about what a team has done and remarkably little about what the team has come to understand, which is arguably the more important of the two. The usual report is a list of completed tasks, tickets closed and milestones reached, and its format quietly assumes that progress is the same thing as activity.

The difficulty is that much of the most valuable work a team does leaves no trace in such a list. A week spent discovering that an approach will not work, or that a requirement was misunderstood, or that a dependency is far less reliable than everyone believed, may be the week in which the project became considerably more likely to succeed; in the report it appears as a week in which little was delivered. You might reasonably expect people to see through this, and some do, but a format that records only completion will over time teach a team to prefer the kind of work that can be completed and reported, and to postpone the kind that merely reduces uncertainty.

There is also the matter of what a better report would contain. It would, I suspect, say less about tasks and more about knowledge: how confident the team is in its current plan, which questions remain open, and what it has learned since the last report that changes its view of the work ahead. None of this is especially elaborate, and most teams could write it in the time they currently spend polishing lists of finished items. The difference is that it would give readers some sense of where the real risks lie, rather than an impression of steady movement towards a destination whose position is still, in some sense, unknown.

It seems to me, however, that the format is the easier part of the problem. A report that states its doubts plainly will only survive in an organisation that is willing to receive doubt without treating it as a failure, and many organisations, perhaps without intending to, reward the appearance of control over the admission of uncertainty. Until that changes, teams will go on writing reports that describe what they have done rather than what they know, because the first is safe and the second is not. Perhaps the most honest report is not the one that shows the most progress but the one that shows most clearly how much remains uncertain.
