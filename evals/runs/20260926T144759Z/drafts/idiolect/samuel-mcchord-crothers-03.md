I once had a long talk with a man who had been a forger and had since reformed. His repentance was genuine, but what interested me was his former conscience, for he had one, and it was considerably more exacting than I had anticipated.

He entertained a profound contempt for petty theft. The sneak who lifts a purse from a coat pocket was to him a creature beneath notice, and he spoke of such persons much as a surgeon might speak of a barber who draws teeth. I ventured to suggest that the difference was one of degree. "You would put the pickpocket and the forger in the same class?" he asked, with the air of a man who has been insulted in his profession. He would not tolerate the comparison.

The pickpocket, he explained, takes advantage of the unwary. There is no skill in it worth mentioning, and no hazard that a man of spirit would consider respectable. But the forger enters a contest. On the other side are the detectives, generously paid, thoroughly trained and furnished with every resource that money can supply; on his side there is nothing but his own ingenuity. If he wins, he has won against the best. If he loses, he has at least lost honourably.

This is all very well, and there is a certain logic in it. It is the logic of the sportsman rather than of the moralist. The injured party, whoever he may be, does not appear in the argument at all. He is merely the ground on which the game is played. I did not press the point, since my friend had already retired from the competition. But I came away with the feeling that the boundary between right and wrong is drawn in a great many places, and that every man draws it a little below himself.
