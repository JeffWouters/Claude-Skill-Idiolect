The counterfeiter who writes his memoirs is under a special obligation to explain himself, and the one I have in mind did not shirk it. His explanation, when it is stripped of its ornaments, comes to this: the country was suffering from a shortage of cash, and he supplied it. That the cash was false he treated as a detail of manufacture and not as a question of principle. The public wanted money and he provided money, and if the article was not what it pretended to be, neither was a great deal else in circulation at the time.

It is an argument with a certain economic charm. Unfortunately the authorities of Massachusetts did not find it charming, and they lodged him in prison to reflect upon it. His reflections, as recorded, show no sign that he ever came to share their view of the matter. He regarded himself as a public benefactor whose services had been misunderstood by a narrow-minded government, which is a character that has been claimed by a good many persons with better titles to it.

But it is his next step that interests me most. On his release he found himself, as he tells us, obliged to choose a profession. The law was out of the question, for it required money to study it; medicine was equally expensive, and even a trade demanded some capital to begin with, which was awkward, since capital of the genuine kind was precisely the one commodity he had never learned to produce. There remained the pulpit. He chose to become a preacher.

I do not wish to be unjust to him, nor to the calling he entered. Men have come to the ministry by stranger roads and served it well. Yet there is something in his reasoning that gives one pause. He did not choose the pulpit because he had been called to it, but because it was the only profession left that could be entered without funds, and it was, in short, the cheapest of the learned professions. The clergy, I fancy, would not have thanked him for the compliment.
