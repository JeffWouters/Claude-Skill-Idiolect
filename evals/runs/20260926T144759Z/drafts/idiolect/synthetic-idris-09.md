What is it about a new technology that makes an experienced engineer so reluctant to learn it? It may be that the reluctance has less to do with the technology than with what learning it requires, which is a return, however brief, to the uncomfortable position of not knowing what one is doing.

The resistance rarely presents itself in those terms. It arrives instead as a reasoned objection to the tool, a doubt about its maturity or its fit, and some of these objections are entirely fair; others, I suspect, are a way of avoiding the experience of looking unskilled in front of colleagues who have come to rely on their judgement. Who among us has not argued against something partly because we would have been a beginner at it?

There is also the matter of time, which organisations speak about more generously than they allocate it. Employers praise growth and continuous learning in their values and their reviews, yet the working week is usually arranged as though learning happened somewhere else, in evenings or in the gaps between deadlines, and the engineer who takes a day to study something properly may find that day difficult to account for. The message received is arguably the opposite of the one intended.

It seems to me that the consequence is a particular kind of fragile knowledge. People learn only what they need at the moment they need it, a command here and a workaround there, assembling scraps that allow the immediate task to be finished without ever forming a coherent understanding of the whole; this works well enough until something unexpected happens, at which point the scraps turn out to be considerably less than they appeared. Perhaps the real cost of never having time to learn properly is not ignorance but a competence that cannot survive its first surprise.
