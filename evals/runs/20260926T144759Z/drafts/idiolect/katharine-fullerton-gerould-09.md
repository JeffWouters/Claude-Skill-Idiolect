When is a lie permissible? The moralists have argued it for centuries, and I have no hope of settling it in a page. But I think one distinction may be drawn with some confidence. A lie told for the sake of another--to shield a friend, to spare a sick man, to keep a confidence--is a different thing from a lie told to save one's own skin or fatten one's own purse. The first may be wrong; the second is certainly base. Most people feel this without being taught it. It is odd how little of our formal ethics has troubled to say so.

The reason, I suspect, lies partly in our religious inheritance. The Puritan mind--and a good deal of American morality is Puritan still, whether it knows it or not--long looked upon honour as an enemy. Honour was worldly; it smelt of swords and of the aristocracy; it set a man's opinion of himself above his duty to God. [example needed: a Puritan authority condemning honour] So honour was put away, and conscience was put in its place, and conscience, being a private affair between a man and his Maker, has never been much concerned with the lies he tells to other men.

Yet honour is not the swaggering thing the Puritans feared. It is something far more exacting. The law tells a man what he must not do; honour tells him what he must do though no law obliges him. It is a bond he lays upon himself--a promise, not to a court, but to his own conception of what a gentleman owes. Some one will say that this is vanity dressed up as virtue. Granted: there is vanity in it. But it is a vanity that pays its debts, and that is more than can be said for a good many consciences.

For that, when all is said, is the test. The man of honour pays what he owes: his bills, his promises, his gratitude, his apologies. He pays them when nobody could compel him to, and when nobody would know if he didn't. The point is that a lie told for another may be consistent with honour; a debt left unpaid never is. The one is a fault. The other is a disgrace.
