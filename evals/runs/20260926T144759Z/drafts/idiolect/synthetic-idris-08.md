Where do the due dates that shape so much of a team's working life actually come from? It may be that we rarely ask, because by the time a date reaches the people who must meet it, its origin has usually been forgotten and only its authority remains.

Some dates are genuinely imposed from outside, by a customer, a regulation or an event that will not wait for anyone; many more, I suspect, are simply chosen, by someone who needed a number for a plan and picked one that seemed reasonable at the time. The curious thing is that both kinds tend to be received with the same dread. A team rarely distinguishes between a date that cannot move and a date that merely has not yet been moved, and so it treats every deadline as though the consequences of missing it were equally severe.

There is also the question of what an approaching date does to the people working towards it. You might say, with some justice, that it sharpens attention, since a deadline has a way of clarifying which tasks truly matter and which can be quietly set aside. The same pressure, however, invites shortcuts that would not otherwise be taken, and it encourages long hours that feel heroic in the moment and considerably less so afterwards; what is gained in focus is, arguably, paid for in quality and in the energy of the team.

It seems to me that the difficulty lies less in the existence of deadlines than in the silence that surrounds their cost. Somebody ought to decide, openly and deliberately, whether the shortcuts and the overwork that a particular date will demand are a price worth paying, yet in most organisations nobody does, and the cost is simply absorbed by whoever happens to be closest to the work. Perhaps a date deserves our dread only when someone has been willing to say aloud what it is worth.
