There is a Victorian poem, once very famous, in which the speaker turns from the peace of his time with something like disgust. He finds it greedy and dull. Greed has become the habit of the nation, and every man is so busy with his own profit that he has forgotten there was ever anything worth dying for. Against all this the speaker calls for battle, not as a misfortune to be endured but as a release from a life that has grown intolerable.

What he wants is not merely war. He wants heroic leaders, men of a larger mould than the merchants and the politicians, and he wants a noble cause for them to lead him in. [quotation needed from the poem] It is the old dream of the clean sword and the righteous quarrel, and it has never lacked for dreamers.

The appeal was addressed to a particular audience. It spoke to a British generation that was too young to remember the earlier wars, and which had therefore been spared the knowledge of what they had cost. Their fathers could have told them. But fathers are not often consulted on such matters, and when they are, their testimony is apt to be set aside as the natural timidity of age. The young had heard of glory; they had not heard of the hospitals.

It is easy to smile at all this, and the smile is perhaps deserved. Yet I think it would be a mistake to dismiss the poem as a piece of romantic nonsense. The feeling it expresses is a real one, and it is not confined to a single country or a single century. Every generation comes to it in its turn, looks at the comfortable peace its elders have made, and finds it wanting. The discontent is genuine even when the remedy is not. Those who would keep the peace had better take that discontent seriously, for it will certainly not go away because they decline to notice it.
