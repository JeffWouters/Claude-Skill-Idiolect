It is a commonplace that you may learn a good deal about a man by watching how he treats those who can do nothing for him.  The shop assistant is in an excellent position to make observations of this kind, and he makes them, whether he intends to or not.  He is, by the nature of his employment, obliged to take whatever is handed to him across the counter: impatience, condescension, bad temper, and that particular brand of rudeness which some people reserve for those who are paid to be civil to them.  He may not answer back; he must smile, and wrap the parcel, and say that it is no trouble at all.  And so, in the course of a few years, he comes to possess a knowledge of human character that is, I think, rather more reliable than most.  People are not on their guard with him.  Why should they be?  He does not count.

It is for this reason that I have always attached some importance to the manner of the novelist toward me.  He did not treat me as a person who did not count.  He treated me, and I can put it no other way, as an equal.  He spoke to me earnestly, as though what I thought were really a matter of some interest to him; and he spoke to me politely, with a kind of grave and deliberate courtesy that was not, I am convinced, put on for the occasion.  It was simply the way he was.  I have been spoken to by a good many distinguished persons in my time, and by a great many more who believed themselves distinguished, and I can say that the difference between the two classes is very often precisely this.

Our conversation was not, by any standard, a momentous one.  He wished to know what proportion of the fiction we carried was British.  He put the question with care, and he listened to the answer with care, as though it were a matter of genuine concern to him, as perhaps it was. I did not argue with him; it would not have occurred to me.  But I've remembered the verdict, and I have remembered still more the courtesy with which the question before it was put.
