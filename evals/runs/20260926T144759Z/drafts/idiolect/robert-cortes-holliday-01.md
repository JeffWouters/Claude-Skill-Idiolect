It may be objected that a man who once sold books across a counter has no business telling tales of the people who bought them.  Perhaps not.  But consider the coachman.  The coachman has driven the great to their dinners and afterwards, over his pipe in the stable yard, told anybody who cared to listen exactly what the great said on the way, and how they said it, and what they looked like when they thought nobody was attending to them.  Consider the waiter, who has carried soup to statesmen and remembered, with a fidelity no biographer could match, how the statesmen behaved toward the soup.  Nobody thinks the worse of them; their memoirs are a recognised branch of literature.  I claim no more than the privilege of the coachman and the waiter.  I stood behind a counter, and certain notable persons came to the other side of it.

Of these the one I remember best is the novelist.  I had admired him a long while; I had read him, I may say, with a devotion I did not give to my duties.  And it happened, in the way such things will happen, that on the evening before he first came into the shop I had sat up late discussing him, arguing over his books, his manner, his standing, and the old question of whether he was the greatest of living writers or merely the most difficult, until I had turned him over and over like a coin and could not have told you which side was up.  Then the next morning there he was.  I do not know how to describe the feeling except to say that it was a little uncanny.  It was as if I'd talked him into the room.

He did not come alone.  With him was a companion, very well dressed, who seemed to have no interest whatever in books.  While the novelist went about his business this companion simply waited, standing about near the door and looking at nothing in particular.  There was no impatience in it; it was merely idleness, in the handsome, finished way that some people are idle.  I've often wondered what the companion thought of it all.  Probably nothing.
