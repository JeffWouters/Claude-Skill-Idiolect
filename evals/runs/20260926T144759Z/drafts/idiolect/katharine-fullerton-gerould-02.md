It is not science I quarrel with; it is science with a crown on its head. The laboratory has done wonders, and I, for one, am grateful for every one of them. But somewhere along the way the method that serves so admirably for weighing gases has come to fancy itself the only honest way of knowing anything at all--and the humanities, poor things, have been hurrying to oblige it. It is perhaps natural that the younger studies should envy the elder its prestige; it is rather less natural that they should sell their own birthright to get it. Literature is no longer read; it is "investigated." Poetry is counted by its syllables, its sources are tabulated, its variants are collated in footnotes, and the student emerges knowing a good deal about the poem and nothing whatever of it. The study has become technical because only the technical can be measured, and only what can be measured is now thought respectable.

Some one will say that this is mere sentiment; that a thing which cannot be tested cannot be known. Granted: beauty is not to be weighed. But that is the whole point. You may measure the canvas, analyse the pigment, and examine the brush-strokes under a glass, and you will be no nearer to knowing whether the picture is good. Beauty is not a physical property, and no instrument yet devised has detected it. Has not every generation known this without being told? I venture to say that the man who judges a sonnet by its metrical statistics would judge a sunset by its temperature.

The point is that science is a method, not a philosophy of everything. I have no wish to see it banished from the university; I should be the first to mourn it. What I object to is its claim to dominion tout court--the assumption, so quietly made that nobody troubles to defend it, that whatever lies outside the laboratory lies outside knowledge. That is not science. It is conquest, and culture is the province it has annexed. We shan't get it back by pretending the annexation never happened.
