Among the minor classics of early American literature there is an autobiography by a swindler which deserves more attention than it has received. It is not a great book, and its author was not a great man, but it has the uncommon merit of setting forth a complete philosophy of life from the point of view of one who lived by his wits and at the expense of other people.

The philosophy is simple enough. Contentment, he tells us, depends upon the mind of the man himself and not upon the opinions of others. This is excellent doctrine, and it has been taught by sages in every age; the sages, however, did not usually mean it as a defence of sharp dealing. Our author applies it with a directness that the philosophers never contemplated. If the world thinks ill of him, that is the affair of the world. He has consulted his own mind and found it tolerably at peace.

What gives the memoir its peculiar flavour is the mixture of cheerful moralizing with a kind of injured astonishment. On one page he is dispensing wholesome advice to the young, in the manner of a schoolmaster who has never been caught at anything; on the next he is recording, with every sign of genuine surprise, that he has been harshly treated by persons who did not appreciate his motives. He cannot quite understand why they should have taken it so personally. The reader, who understands very well, is left to supply the explanation for himself.

But the most engaging passage concerns his family. He was a minister's son, and he might have made something of it, as lesser men have made careers out of a respectable father. He declines to do so. A man, he says, should stand on his own merits, and he will take no credit for the virtues of his ancestors. It is a sentiment that would do honour to a reformer. In his case it has a slightly different significance, for if he claims none of the family credit, he can hardly be expected to share any of the family scruples.

There is a whole moral system in that refusal, and I am not sure that it is entirely his own invention.
