If you wish to learn how you appear to strangers, do not consult your friends; consult a landlady.  Your friends have grown used to you.  The landlady sees you, as it were, for the first time and without prejudice, and she forms her opinion upon the doorstep, in something under a minute.  It is not always a flattering opinion.  It is nearly always an instructive one.

I remember a house in which I was shown a room of quite reasonable dimensions, clean, fairly lighted, and furnished in the customary manner, with one exception.  Among the furniture stood a surgical table.  It was, the landlady explained, her own surgical table, and it was offered with the room, without apology and without further remark.  I do not know what she supposed I should do with it.  I did not inquire.  I have sometimes thought that she took me for a person who would find it useful, and I have never been able to decide whether that was a compliment.

In another house the landlady, having looked me over with great deliberation, arrived at the conclusion that my business, whatever it was, was not a thing to be spoken of.  She said nothing directly.  But she let me understand, by a series of nods and lowered tones, that she was a woman of the world, that she asked no questions, and that my comings and goings would be entirely my own affair.  I don't think I have ever been so thoroughly misunderstood, or so generously forgiven for it.

The respectable houses are another matter altogether.  Here nobody suspects you of anything; they merely require you to prove that there is nothing to suspect.  You must furnish references as to character.  You must, in the more fashionable neighbourhoods, furnish evidence of substantial means as well.  Virtue, it appears, is necessary, but it is not sufficient.  It must be solvent.
