The simple life, so called, is the most complicated life I know. Its disciples have given up their servants and their luxuries in order, they say, to be free; and they spend the rest of their days doing, badly and at great length, what somebody else once did for them quickly and well. They cook; they wash; they mend; they sweep. By the time the house is in order, the day is over, and there is no hour left for the reading or the thinking or the talking for which the simplicity was supposedly undertaken. One is not freed by doing everything oneself. One is merely kept busy.

The remedy seems to me obvious, though I know it is not fashionable. Life would be made easier, not harder, by more specialization rather than less--by more people doing each the thing they do well, and doing it for others. That means, among other things, more servants, not fewer. Some one will cry out at this. Granted: it sounds like a reactionary's dream. But the principle is precisely the one on which every modern industry is run, and no one thinks it tyrannous that the baker bakes and the cobbler cobbles. Why it should become tyranny the moment it crosses the threshold of a private house, I have never understood.

There remains the moral argument, which the simple-lifers like best. They deny themselves, they tell us, out of sympathy with those who have less. It is a generous impulse, and I don't mock it. But whom does it help? The woman who dismisses her cook does not thereby feed a single hungry family; she merely adds one to the number of people looking for work. Voluntary self-denial improves the soul of the self-denier, perhaps. It does nothing whatever for the lot of any one else. My point is only this: if we wish to do good, we must do it to others, not to ourselves.
