Why is it that a new estimation method so rarely makes the forecasts of a team any more reliable than the method it replaced? It may be that the method was never the source of the difficulty, and that changing it is attractive precisely because it allows everyone to feel that something has been done without anyone having to change how they actually talk about the work.

Teams move from one unit to another, from one method to the next, from one ritual of guessing to another, and each change arrives with a certain optimism; the conversations, meanwhile, remain very much as they were. The same questions are asked in the same tone, the same pressures shape the answers, and the numbers that emerge are, I suspect, about as trustworthy as before, merely expressed in different units. Is it any wonder that the improvement so often fades once the novelty of the new scheme has worn off?

There is also a more useful way to think about what an estimate is. Any estimate is best treated as a snapshot of what a team knows at the moment it is made, a record of present understanding that will shift as that understanding grows, rather than as a commitment that must be defended once it has been spoken. It helps, too, to separate the familiar work that a team has done many times from the genuinely new work it has never attempted, since the first can be estimated with some confidence and the second, arguably, can hardly be estimated at all.

It seems to me that the real barrier is neither the method nor the skill of the team but a shared attachment to precise numbers, which offer a reassurance that the work itself cannot honestly provide. Perhaps a forecast becomes more reliable not when it grows more exact but when it admits how much it does not yet know.
