Why do standing team meetings so often continue long after anyone can remember the reason they were set up? It may be that we are looking at them in the wrong way, treating them as items on a calendar when they behave far more like rituals, which are known, after all, for outliving whatever first gave them meaning. A ritual does not need a purpose to survive; it needs only to be repeated, and repetition is precisely what a recurring invitation supplies.

It is worth remembering that most of these meetings began well. Someone noticed a real problem, perhaps that work was being duplicated or that decisions were reaching people too late, and the weekly gathering was a sensible, even a thoughtful, answer to it, which for a while arguably did exactly what it was meant to do. The difficulty is that the problem can quietly disappear while the answer stays in place, and nobody, in the ordinary course of things, is asked to notice the moment at which one stopped needing the other. Is it any wonder that the slot keeps filling itself, week after week, with updates that might have been an email?

There is also the matter of who would be the one to say so. Proposing to end a meeting can feel like a small accusation against whoever started it, or against everyone who has kept attending without complaint, and so the silence that allows the meeting to continue is in some sense shared by all of us. I suspect that many teams hold at least one meeting that every participant privately considers unnecessary, and that each, reasonably enough, assumes the others still find it useful.

It seems to me that the first step is simply to ask what problem a given meeting was meant to solve, and whether that problem is still with us. The answer will sometimes be yes, in which case the meeting can continue with a clearer sense of its purpose; sometimes it will be no, and the meeting, having served its turn, can be allowed to end. Perhaps the habit worth building is not the meeting itself but the willingness, now and then, to question it.
