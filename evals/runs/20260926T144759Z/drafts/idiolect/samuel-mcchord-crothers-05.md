It is easy to love a place that is lovely. The dusty frontier boomtown I have in mind was not lovely, and the people who lived in it knew as much, yet I have seldom seen a community more attached to its surroundings. The attachment was not an accident. It was a matter of choice, and like most choices made against the evidence, it was held with a good deal of conviction.

I remember a widow in the town whose decision about her husband's burial [example needed about what she chose and why] told me more about the place than any of its boosters ever managed to. She could have chosen otherwise; nobody would have thought the worse of her for it. I have always thought her choice a better civic tribute than most of the speeches I have heard since.

The town itself was a temporary camp, and everybody knew it. But it did not behave like one. It conducted itself, in all its public dealings, as though it were a great and lasting city which had been temporarily inconvenienced by a shortage of buildings, of history and of almost everything else that a city is commonly supposed to require. Civic occasions were observed with a gravity that would not have disgraced an ancient capital. Whenever the solemnity threatened to become oppressive, somebody could be relied upon to puncture it.

For the humour of the place was a common possession. It was not the private wit of a few clever men, hoarded for their own amusement, but something that belonged to the whole population and was spent freely in public. This was the more remarkable because a surprising number of the inhabitants were educated professional men who could have been expected to take themselves seriously. It was perhaps precisely because they were educated that they did not. They had seen the grand cities and knew very well what they were imitating. The imitation was the joke, and everybody was in on it.
