What would it take for an engineering team to treat learning as something done in the open rather than something done quietly, after hours, where nobody can see how little one knew at the start? It may be that the answer has less to do with training budgets or documentation than with whether asking a question is regarded as an ordinary act or as a small confession. Teams in which questions are asked openly tend, as far as one can tell, to improve faster together, since each question that is answered in public becomes a piece of shared understanding rather than a private repair.

Asking, however, is not free, and it would be naive to pretend otherwise. In most technical organisations knowledge functions as a kind of status, arguably the principal kind, and so admitting a gap in it feels like surrendering a little of one's standing in front of colleagues. You may have noticed how often a question in a meeting is prefaced with an apology, or disguised as a comment, or simply left unasked in the hope that someone else will raise it. Is it any wonder that people prefer to search alone for an answer they could have had in a minute from the person sitting beside them?

There is also the particular difficulty faced by senior engineers, for whom the cost of admitting ignorance appears to rise with every year of experience. A junior colleague is expected not to know things; a senior one, perhaps unfairly, is expected to know almost everything, and the expectation is often held most firmly by the senior engineer themselves. The consequence is a curious inversion, in which the people best placed to demonstrate that learning is normal are the ones least willing to be seen doing it, and the rest of the team draws the obvious conclusion.

It seems to me that the most useful change is to reconsider what we mean by competence. We tend to treat it as a state that one arrives at, a store of knowledge accumulated and then defended; I suspect it is better understood as a practice, the continuing willingness to learn and to stay curious about what one does not yet understand. A senior engineer who asks a plain question in front of the team is not revealing a weakness but modelling the very habit that keeps the team capable, and the effect of that example is likely to be considerable. Perhaps the true measure of expertise is not how rarely one needs to ask but how comfortably one can.
