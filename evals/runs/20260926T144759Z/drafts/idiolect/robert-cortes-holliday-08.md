I have never been able to understand why looking for rooms should be regarded as a hardship.  People speak of it as they speak of going to the dentist, or of making out an income-tax return: a thing to be got through with clenched teeth and forgotten as soon as possible.  To me it has always been one of the pleasantest of occupations, and I have sometimes been tempted, when I had no need of rooms whatever, to go and look at a few, merely for the entertainment of it.

For consider what it offers.  You are admitted, on the most respectable of pretexts, into the private arrangements of a great many of your fellow creatures.  You see how they live, what they value, what they put up with and what they will not put up with; you observe their furniture, their pictures, their notions of comfort and their notions of a bargain.  It is an education in human nature such as no library can furnish.  And it is, I think, an education in charity as well.  One comes away from a morning of it with a considerably enlarged sympathy for the odd, the obstinate, and the merely unfortunate, among whom, it may be, one has occasionally caught a glimpse of oneself.

Even the advertisements are a pleasure.  I read them as other people read novels.  What strikes one first about them is their extraordinary unanimity.  Every room is "desirable."  Every situation is "convenient."  Every house is "select," every landlady "refined," every neighbourhood "quiet and exclusive."  There is not, in the whole of the columns, a single room that is admitted to be dark, or a single street that is confessed to be noisy.  One begins to wonder where the dark rooms and the noisy streets have gone.  Nowhere, apparently.  They do not advertise.

But of all the places I have inspected, the most charming, beyond any comparison, are the old legal chambers of London.  There is about them something that no newer building can pretend to.
