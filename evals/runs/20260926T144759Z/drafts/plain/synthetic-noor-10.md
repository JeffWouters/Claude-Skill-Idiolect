Once the vertical threads are in place across the patch, the second half of the darn begins: weaving the crosswise threads through them. This is where the repair turns from a set of loose strands into something that looks and behaves like fabric.

Work from one side of the patch to the other, taking the needle over one vertical thread and under the next, then reversing the pattern on the way back. Each pass locks the previous one in place. Anchor the ends in the sound wool beyond the outline, just as you did with the first layer.

The one thing to watch is tension. It is tempting to pull each thread snug, because tight feels secure. It is not. If you pull the crosswise threads tight, the patch gathers and bunches, and you end up with a hard ridge inside the sock that you will feel with every step. Let the threads lie flat and relaxed against the rounded support. The weave holds itself together; it does not need to be strained to be strong.

It is slow work, and that is part of its appeal. An evening spent darning feels restful in a way that few other tasks do. Your hands are busy, your attention is gentle, and there is a finished object at the end of it. And the result is not a lesser version of the sock. A well-darned heel is thicker and stronger than the original knitting, and in my experience the reinforced heels outlast the rest of the sock.
