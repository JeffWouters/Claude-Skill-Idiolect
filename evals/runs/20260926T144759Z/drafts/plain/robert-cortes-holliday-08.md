Most people speak of looking for rooms as a chore, something to be got through as quickly as possible and forgotten. I have never been able to see it that way. To me it is one of the pleasures of life, and I am always a little sorry when I have found what I wanted and the search is over.

The reason is simple. There is no better way of seeing human nature at close quarters. In the course of a few afternoons one is let into a dozen houses, shown how other people live, and talked to by landladies, caretakers and departing tenants who would never speak so freely in any other circumstances. Each of them has a story, or at least a manner, and each of them adds something to one's sense of how various people are. I think it broadens one's sympathy. It is hard to go on judging people by a single standard after seeing how differently, and how reasonably, they arrange their lives.

Even the advertisements are a pleasure, if one reads them in the right spirit. They are written with a uniform enthusiasm that is almost touching. Every room is bright, every house is quiet, every neighbourhood is convenient, and every landlady is kind. But of all the places I have gone to inspect, the most charming are the old legal chambers in London. They have a character nothing else quite matches. The staircases are old and worn, the rooms are panelled and a little dark, and there is a stillness about the courts outside them that seems to belong to another century. One feels, going up the stairs, that generations of lawyers have climbed them before, and that the rooms have outlasted a great many tenants and will outlast a great many more. The pleasure is in seeing them, and in imagining for half an hour what it would be like to live there.

That, finally, is the whole secret of enjoying the search. One must go to look, not only to find.
