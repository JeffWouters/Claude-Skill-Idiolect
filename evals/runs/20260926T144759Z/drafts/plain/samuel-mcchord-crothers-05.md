It is hard to explain to anyone who never lived there why people grew so fond of that dusty frontier boomtown. It was not a comfortable place. The ground was bare, the weather was hard, and very little about it would recommend itself to a stranger stepping off the stage. Yet the people who stayed became attached to it, and they did so by choice. Nobody was obliged to remain.

The clearest proof I know of this is a small one. A widow, when the time came to decide where her husband should be buried, chose to bury him there, in that harsh ground, rather than send him back to wherever he had come from. That was part of the charm and part of the comedy of the place. It was a temporary camp, and everyone knew it was temporary. Mining towns rise fast and fall faster. But it behaved as though it were a grand and lasting city. It had its civic affairs, its public occasions, its sense of its own dignity, all conducted with a seriousness that was never quite serious. The residents played at being a great municipality, and they knew they were playing, and the game was the better for it.

The humor there was not a private thing kept for one's own household. It was shared, and it was communal. A joke made in the street belonged to the whole town by evening. People laughed together at the town's pretensions and at their own, and the laughter seemed to bind them more than any charter could have done.

It helped that so many of the residents were educated people. Among the miners and traders there were a good number of professional men, trained in one field or another, who had come out for the same reasons as everyone else. They brought with them a turn of mind that enjoyed irony, and they gave the town's playful civic life a wit it might otherwise have lacked. I think of that place still with more affection than it had any right to earn.
