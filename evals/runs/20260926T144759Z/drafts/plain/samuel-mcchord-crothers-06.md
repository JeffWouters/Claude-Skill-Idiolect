Among the curiosities of early American writing is the autobiography of a swindler, a man who spent a good part of his life cheating others and then sat down to explain himself. What he produced is not a confession in any ordinary sense. It is closer to a philosophy, and a surprisingly cheerful one.

At its centre is a single idea that he returns to again and again. Contentment, he says, depends on one's own mind and not on the opinions of other people. A man who has made peace with himself need not care what the world says of him. Put that way, it sounds almost like the advice of a sage, and in another mouth it might be. In his, it has a convenient edge. A swindler has more reason than most to wish that other people's opinions did not count.

The memoir is full of moralizing, and the moralizing is delivered in good spirits. He offers reflections on conduct and character as though he were writing a book of instruction for the young. Alongside these, and without any sign that he notices the contrast, runs a steady note of surprise at how badly he has been treated. Again and again he seems astonished that people took offence, that they pursued him, that they did not see matters as he saw them. There is one more thread worth pulling. He was a minister's son, and a reader might expect him either to lean on that or to be shamed by it. He does neither. He rejected credit for family lineage altogether. A man, in his account, is what he makes of himself, and owes nothing to the respectability of his father. This fits neatly with the rest of his creed. If other people's opinions do not matter and one's ancestry does not matter, then only the self is left to answer to, and the self in his case was an indulgent judge.

The result is a strange and oddly likeable book: the story of a man who broke most of the rules while holding firmly to a philosophy that let him feel he had broken none.
