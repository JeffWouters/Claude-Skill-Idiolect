Our old brass kitchen faucet had been dripping, and I kept telling myself it was not worth the trouble. A drip is small. Then, one evening, I put a container under the spout and left it overnight. In the morning it held far more than I expected [example needed: the amount collected]. That was the end of the debate. A slow drip, multiplied by every hour of every day, is real water going straight down the drain.

The temptation with a job like this is to grab a wrench and start. I have learned to resist it, because the preparation is what keeps a small repair small.

First, protect the drain. Put the plug in, or lay a cloth over the opening. Faucets are full of small parts, and the one you drop will always be the one that rolls towards the drain.

Next, shut off the water. Our supply valve under the sink had not been touched in years and was stiff. It is worth being patient here: turn it firmly but steadily rather than forcing it, because a supply valve that breaks is a much bigger problem than a dripping faucet. Once it is closed, open the faucet to release the pressure and let the last of the water run out.

Then comes the disassembly. The handle comes off first, usually after removing a cap and a screw. Underneath is the valve assembly, which is what you are really after. Here is the one lesson I would pass on: brace the faucet body while you loosen the assembly. Old brass can be seized in place, and if you just put a wrench on the valve and turn, the whole faucet may rotate with it, twisting the connections underneath. Hold the body steady with one hand, or with a second wrench, and turn the assembly with the other. Go slowly, and lay the parts out in the order they came off.

With the valve assembly out, the worn seal was finally within reach.
