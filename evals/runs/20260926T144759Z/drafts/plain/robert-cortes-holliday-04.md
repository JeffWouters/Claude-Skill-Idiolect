When the novelist finally came to the counter to place his order, it became clear that he had no idea how a bookshop worked, and no particular wish to learn.

He seemed to assume that the one assistant standing in front of him was the whole firm. In his mind, I think, the young man who took down the order would also find the books in the stockroom, wrap them, address the parcel, carry it to the post, and keep the accounts afterward. The firm had departments for all of these things, and people in each of them, but that did not enter into his picture. He spoke to the clerk as though a single pair of hands would see the business through from beginning to end, and he gave his instructions accordingly, in no very particular order.

What he wanted, it emerged, was copies of his own books. But he wanted them sent by mail, and not to himself. They were to go to his brother's wife.

That was as far as he went on his own account. He seemed to feel that "my brother's wife" was a sufficient direction, and that the rest would somehow take care of itself. The assistant had to ask what her name was. The novelist gave it, a little as though he had been asked something slightly beside the point. Then the assistant had to ask where she lived, and the address had to be drawn out of him too, piece by piece, with a pause before each part while he recollected it. [Details of the name and address exchange needed.] At no point did he seem impatient, only vague, as a man is vague about matters that are not really his to attend to and that have always been attended to by someone else.

It would be easy to make fun of this, and I do not mean to. There was nothing grand or overbearing in the way he went about it. He was simply muddled, in the manner of someone whose attention lives almost entirely somewhere else, and who comes down to the practical world only when he must and then with some reluctance. The order was placed in the end, correctly, and the books went off. But the clerk who took it had done most of the placing.
