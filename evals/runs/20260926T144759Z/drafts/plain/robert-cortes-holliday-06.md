If you want to know what a bookshop assistant does all day, the honest answer is that he answers questions, and that no two of them are alike.

A good part of the day goes to people buying presents. They are the most difficult to please, because they do not know what they want and are determined not to be told. The assistant suggests a novel; it is too light. He suggests a volume of essays; it is too heavy. He suggests a book of travel, a biography, a collection of poems, and each is turned over, considered, and put back with a small shake of the head. At the end of it the customer is no nearer a decision than at the start, and quite often he leaves without buying anything, having rejected every suggestion the shop could offer. The assistant has spent half an hour and learned only what the recipient does not like, and even that is uncertain.

What makes the work tiring is not the difficulty of any single question but the speed with which the subject changes. One moment a scholar wants to know whether the shop has a particular edition of an old text, and which of two translations is to be trusted. The next, someone wants a book of riddles for a party. Then comes a question about a reference work, and then one about a picture book, and the assistant has to shift his mind from the one to the other without a pause, taking each as seriously as the customer takes it. There is no time to settle into any one level of seriousness.

And the range of what people ask for is very nearly endless. In the course of one day, customers may want a book on the care of a baby, and quickly; something to read on a long journey, not too heavy to carry; [example needed]; [example needed]; a book that a particular person will certainly like, although the customer cannot say what that person reads; and something, anything, on a subject the customer cannot quite name.

A few of these can be satisfied at once. Most need a little conversation first, to find out what is really wanted, which is seldom quite what was asked for. Some cannot be satisfied at all, and the assistant must say so politely and hope the customer comes back another day. By closing time he has been, in turn, a librarian, a nursemaid's adviser, a travel agent and a mind reader, and he has sold, perhaps, a handful of books.
