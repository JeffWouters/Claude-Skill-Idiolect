There is an old and flattering idea that plain living produces high thinking. Strip life down, the argument runs, do your own chores, want little and own less, and the mind will be freed for nobler things. It is an attractive idea, and I think it is mostly a myth.

I have no objection to anyone doing his own housework. Sweeping a floor, cooking a meal or mending a fence is honest labour, and there is nothing shameful in it. What I object to is the claim that it is more civilized than the alternative. A man who blacks his own boots is not thereby more refined than one who has them blacked for him. He has simply spent part of his day on boots. Whether the saved hours of the other man go to thought or to idleness is a separate question, but the chance of thought is at least there. For the man doing everything himself, it is often not.

The deeper trouble is that civilized life is not only a matter of ideas. It is also a matter of forms: the way a table is laid, the way guests are received, the way a house is kept, the small ceremonies that make ordinary days pleasant and give them a shape. These traditions are graceful, and grace of that kind does not maintain itself. It needs people who know how things are done and who do them regularly. Some of those people are the owners of the household. Many of them, historically, have been servants, whose skill and memory kept the customs alive. Remove them in the name of simplicity and the customs do not become simpler. They disappear.

Then there is the question of competence. When everyone is levelled down to doing every task for himself, nobody does any task especially well. The householder becomes a mediocre cook, a clumsy carpenter, an indifferent gardener and a hurried scholar, all in the same week. We end up with a nation of amateurs, each busy at a dozen jobs and expert at none. That is not a triumph of equality; it is a loss of skill spread evenly across everyone.

I do not say that wealth guarantees wisdom, or that servants guarantee grace. I say only that the frugal life has no special claim on lofty thought. Thought needs time, and good living needs hands that know their work. A society that disdains both in the name of plainness should not be surprised to find itself neither simpler nor wiser, but only more tired.
