I take the same train every working day, and after enough repetitions the route has become a sequence of small markers rather than a blur of scenery. I no longer need to hear the announcements to know where I am. Each station has its landmark just before it, something I look for without really deciding to. One of them is a bird that stands by the waterway we cross, often in the same spot, as if it had its own timetable. I look for it every morning. When it is there, the day feels properly started. When it is not, I notice the gap.

For a long time I used the journey to work. Laptop open, emails answered, a head start on the day. At some point I stopped. It was not a grand decision, more a slow realisation that the time was worth more to me empty than full. Now the train is the one part of the day where nobody can reach me in any meaningful way and nothing is expected of me. It is free time in the most literal sense, and it arrives twice a day whether I plan for it or not.

That emptiness is what lets me notice things. Repetition has a bad reputation, but seeing the same view every day is exactly what makes change visible. The seasons do not arrive all at once from the window of a train; they creep in. Trees turn at different speeds along different stretches. On certain mornings there is frost on the fields and on the fences, and a week later it is gone. A wall that was blank one week carries fresh street art the next, and I find myself checking on it like a neighbour.
