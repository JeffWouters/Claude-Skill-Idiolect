I saw him first from across the shop, before I knew who he was. There was nothing about his appearance to announce a famous man. He was short and stout, and quite bald, and if I had passed him in the street I should not have looked twice. What drew my attention was the way he talked. He came back, though, more than once, and on those later visits he came alone, or at least without company that claimed his attention. It was then that I saw him as I like best to remember him. He went to the shelves and simply lost himself in them. He would take down a volume, look at the title page, turn it over, read a passage, put it back, and take down the next, and he did all of this with a completeness of attention that shut out everything else in the shop. Customers moved around him; assistants passed with armfuls of books; conversations went on at the counter; and none of it seemed to reach him. [Example of a book he examined needed.]

I have watched many people browse, and most of them do it with half their minds elsewhere, glancing at the clock or at the door. He was not like that. For as long as he stood at the shelves, the shelves were the whole of his world, and I found it oddly moving to see a man who wrote books himself still so entirely absorbed by other people's.
