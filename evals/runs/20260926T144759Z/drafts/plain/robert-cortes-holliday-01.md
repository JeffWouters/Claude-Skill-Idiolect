I know there is something faintly disreputable in a shop assistant setting down what he saw of the customers who passed his counter. People who serve the public are supposed to be deaf and blind to it, or at least silent. But I notice that nobody has ever held the rule very strictly. Coachmen who drove the great have told what they heard through the little window behind them, and waiters who stood by famous tables have filled whole books with what was said over the soup. If they may talk, I do not see why a man who once sold books should be forbidden to. So I will tell what I remember of the day I met [novelist's name], a writer I had admired for years before I ever expected to stand within arm's reach of him.

The strange part of it began the night before. A few of us had spent the evening arguing about his books, as young men who read too much will do, and I had been defending him at some length and with more heat than the subject perhaps deserved. I went to bed with his name still in my head. The next morning I was at my post in the shop, and there he was. It is hard to describe how odd that felt. It was not quite surprise; it was more like the sensation of having summoned someone by talking about him, as though the conversation of the previous evening had reached out and fetched him through the door. For a moment I half distrusted my own eyes.

He was not alone. With him came a companion, a person very well dressed, who took no interest whatever in the books. While the writer moved along the shelves, the companion simply waited, standing about in the idle, patient way of someone who has been brought along and knows there is nothing to do but let the time pass. I remember that figure almost as clearly as the man himself, perhaps because the contrast between them was so sharp: one of them absorbed, the other merely present.

What followed is the reason I am writing at all, and I will come to it next.
