Old wool socks wear out in predictable places, usually the heel and the toe, and throwing them away the moment a hole appears feels wasteful. Darning is the traditional answer, and it needs less than you might think.

The kit is short. You need a long needle, long enough to span the patch you are going to make with room to spare. You need yarn that matches the sock, both in colour, if you care about that, and in weight and fibre, so the mend behaves like the rest of the fabric. And you need something rounded to stretch the sock over while you work. Proper darning mushrooms and eggs exist, but an old light bulb does the job just as well. It holds the fabric in its natural curve and gives the needle something firm to glide against.

Before you begin, look beyond the hole itself. The hole is only the most obvious symptom. The wool around it has usually been thinning for a while, and if you mend just the gap, the next hole will open right beside your repair. So the patch needs to cover the weakened area surrounding the hole as well, reaching into fabric that is still sound. Hold the sock up to the light: thin areas show clearly.

Start by outlining the patch. Using small running stitches, sew a shape around the damaged area, a little way into the good wool on every side. This outline marks the boundary of your work and anchors the edges of the repair.

Then lay the first layer: parallel vertical threads running across the patch from one side of the outline to the other. Keep them close together and evenly spaced, and pick up a little of the sound fabric at each end so they are firmly anchored. Where they cross the hole, they simply bridge the gap. Do not pull them tight; they should lie flat against the curve of the bulb.

These vertical threads are the warp of your new fabric. The next step is to weave through them.
