It is a comfortable belief that a good plan for peace, once drawn up and agreed, will keep the peace. The plan may be sound in every clause. It may have been argued over by careful people, weighed against past failures, and approved by everyone who read it through. None of that settles the matter. A policy is only as strong as the willingness of those who hold power to accept it, and power does not stay in the same hands for long.

This is the difficulty that the drafters of peace rarely face squarely. They write for the people in the room, and the people in the room are, for the most part, old enough to remember what the last war cost. They nod at the sensible arguments because they have lived through the thing the arguments describe. But the room empties, and a new cohort grows up outside it. That cohort did not bury anyone. It has read about battle rather than seen it, and what it has read is mostly glory.

Enthusiasm for combat returns in cycles, as regularly as each generation comes of age. It is not stamped out by argument, because it was never founded on argument. It is a feeling, and it rises in young people at about the time they begin to want something larger than their own lives to belong to. Peace, seen from that angle, can look small and mean.

A famous Victorian poem said as much, and said it with force. It condemned the greedy, selfish habits of peacetime as worse than battle itself, a kind of slow rot that war would sweep away. Readers who admired the poem were not all fools or brutes. They were answering a real complaint about what ordinary peaceful life can become.

That is why the plan on paper fails. It persuades the people who already agree with it, and it leaves untouched the young, who have not yet been asked and would not listen if they were.
