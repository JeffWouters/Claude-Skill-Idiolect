Anyone who has stood behind a counter knows that selling is, among other things, a lesson in taking rudeness without answering it. The customer may be curt, impatient, condescending or openly contemptuous, and the person serving him must smile and fetch the next book. It is part of the job, and after a while one stops resenting it. But it has a curious side effect. A salesman sees people as they are when they believe it does not matter how they behave. Nobody puts on his best manners for a shop clerk unless the manners are really his. So the clerk's view of human character, though narrow, is unusually honest, and I have come to trust it more than most.

That is why I think it worth recording how the novelist treated the assistant who served him. He had every reason, by the usual standards, to treat him as furniture. He was a famous man, and the clerk was nobody, a young man in a shop whose name he did not know and would not need to know. Plenty of lesser people had made that distinction plain across the same counter.

He did nothing of the kind. He spoke to the clerk as one man speaks to another, as an equal, without any sign that he was conferring a favour by doing so. He was earnest; he seemed genuinely to want to know what the young man thought, and he listened to the answers. And he was polite, not with the careful politeness some people use to mark a distance, but in a plain, natural way, as if it had simply never occurred to him to be anything else. It is a small thing, the way a famous man speaks to a clerk. But small things, seen from behind a counter, are often the ones that tell the truth.
