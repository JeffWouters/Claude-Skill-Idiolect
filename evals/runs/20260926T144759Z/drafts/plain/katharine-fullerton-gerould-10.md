The simple life is recommended to us as a relief. Put away the elaborate household, do things for yourself, need less and depend on no one, and you will be free. In practice it works the other way. The so-called simple life is one of the most burdensome ways of living there is.

The reason is arithmetic. There are only so many hours in a day, and a household makes steady demands on them. Meals must be cooked, rooms cleaned, clothes washed and mended, errands run. When one person undertakes all of this, it swallows the day. The man who does everything himself has no time left for anything else: not for reading, not for friends, not for the work he is actually good at, and not, very often, for rest. What was sold to him as freedom turns out to be a full-time occupation with no wages and no holidays.

The better arrangement is the one that civilization has always moved towards: specialization. Each person does the work he does best and relies on others for the rest. The cook cooks, the gardener gardens, and the scholar studies, and each of them does his part better and faster than an amateur could. In a household, this means more servants rather than fewer. That is not a confession of idleness. It is a division of labour, and a division of labour makes life easier for everyone in it, including the people who are paid to do the work and would otherwise be looking for it elsewhere.

The advocates of simplicity sometimes answer that their choice is not about convenience but about conscience. They give up comforts, they say, out of solidarity with those who have none. The feeling does them credit, but the reasoning does not hold. Voluntary self-denial does nothing to improve anyone else's lot. The poor are not better fed because a comfortable family has decided to eat plainly, nor better housed because it has dismissed its maid. The sacrifice is real to the one who makes it and invisible to everyone else.

Simplicity, then, is not simple. It is complicated and tiring, and it helps nobody.
