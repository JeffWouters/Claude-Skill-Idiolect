Anyone who has sat through enough pointless recurring meetings has felt the urge to delete them all. Clear the calendar, reclaim the hours, and let people talk when they actually need to. It is an appealing idea, and it is usually a mistake.

Recurring meetings do something that is easy to overlook until it is gone. They create regular contact. People who might otherwise only meet in writing see each other at a predictable time, pick up on each other's moods, and hear about problems before they become formal. Small things get mentioned in passing that would never justify a message of their own. That steady rhythm is part of what holds a team together. Scrap every standing meeting and much of that contact quietly disappears, and it tends not to come back on its own. People mean to reach out when needed, but in practice they reach out less.

The opposite approach, keeping everything as it is, has its own cost. Meetings drift. They outlast their purpose and keep consuming time long after they stopped earning it. So neither deleting nor preserving by default is a good answer.

A better approach is to make each recurring meeting justify itself again from time to time. Every so often, ask plainly: what is this meeting for, and is it still doing that? Who needs to be here? Would a shorter slot, or a less frequent one, do the same job? The question is not whether the meeting was a good idea once, but whether it is a good idea now. [example needed]

This review is awkward. Someone usually set the meeting up and feels some ownership over it. Others may worry that suggesting a change looks like disengagement. It takes a little courage to ask whether an hour that everyone has grown used to is still needed.

But it is useful, and the results are usually modest rather than dramatic. Many meetings shrink: shorter, less frequent, with fewer people. Some end outright, and nobody misses them. The ones that survive come out of the review with a clearer purpose, which makes them better. The team keeps its regular contact and loses the parts that had stopped serving it.
