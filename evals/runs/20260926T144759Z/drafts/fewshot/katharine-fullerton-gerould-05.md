Every now and then some one rises at a meeting of schoolmasters and moves that Greek be dropped. It is always dropped, sooner or later; and the reasons given are always the same. Greek is too difficult. Greek is not practical. Greek will not help a boy to earn his living. I do not know which of these reasons I find the more depressing. The first is an admission that we no longer expect our children to do hard things; the second and third are an admission that we no longer value anything we cannot sell. It is not Greek that has been weighed and found wanting. It is we.

For the dropping of Greek is only a symptom. The materialism that finds no use for Homer finds very little use for anything else that does not pay, and the effects of it are to be seen in every college in the land. I am told, by those who teach them, that a great many students now arrive at college having read almost nothing. They have not read the Bible; they have not read Shakespeare, except for a play or two taken apart in class; they have not, in any real sense, read at all. And they cannot write. They can put words together--after a fashion--but the words do not make sentences, and the sentences do not make sense. [example needed]

Who is to blame? Frankly, I think there is blame enough to go round. There are the fads, to begin with: the new method every year, the new subject every term, each one taken up with enthusiasm and dropped with relief. There is the laxity--the fear of asking too much, the notion that a child must never be bored and never be made to learn anything by heart. And there are the public schools themselves, which are asked to do far more than any schools can do, with far too many children and far too little time. I am sorry for the teachers; I am heartily sorry for them. They are expected to turn out citizens, mechanics and stenographers all at once, and it is hardly surprising if they have no leisure left to turn out readers.

But being sorry for the teachers does not mend the matter. The children still come to college unread, and they still go out of it--a good many of them--very little better. One cannot build culture on nothing; and nothing is, I am afraid, what we are giving them to build on.
