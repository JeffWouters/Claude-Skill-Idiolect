It is a curious thing, when one comes to think of it, how quietly science has walked in and taken the chair at the head of the table. It did not ask; it did not need to ask. It presented itself simply as the method--the one road to knowledge, good for every country and every subject--and we, being modest people and a little dazzled, moved down to make room. Culture, which used to sit there, is now somewhere below the salt.

You may see the thing happening in any university catalogue. The humanities have not been abolished; nobody would be so crude. They have merely been made respectable, which is worse. Literature has become the counting of syllables and the tracing of sources; history has become the tabulation of prices; even poetry, I am told, may now be studied by the chart. Whatever can be measured is kept, and whatever cannot is left out as unscholarly. The student learns a great deal about a poem, and nothing whatever of why any one should wish to read it.

Now I may be dull, but I have never been able to weigh beauty. One may measure the canvas and analyse the pigments; one may count the lines of the sonnet and describe the pause in each. When all that is done, the question of whether the picture is good or the sonnet lovely has not so much as been touched. It is not a physical question, and no instrument will ever answer it. Taste is a judgment--a trained and disciplined judgment, if you like, but a judgment all the same--and it belongs to a different order of things.

Let me not be misunderstood. I have nothing against science; I should be an ungrateful creature if I had. Let it have the laboratory, by all means, and the hospital, and the bridge; it has earned them. What I object to is not science, but science with a crown on its head: the claim that its method is the only method, and that what it cannot see is not there. That is not a scientific claim at all. It is merely an imperialism, and like other imperialisms it makes the conquered provinces very poor.
