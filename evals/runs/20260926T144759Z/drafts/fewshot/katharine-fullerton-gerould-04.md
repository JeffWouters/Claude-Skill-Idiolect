"Plain living and high thinking" is a very pretty phrase, and I am sure it has comforted a great many people who could not afford anything else. But it has come, in some quarters, to mean something more than a consolation; it has come to be a creed. We are asked to believe that the woman who washes her own dishes thinks nobler thoughts over the dishpan than the woman who has them washed for her. I do not believe it. I have washed a good many dishes, and I cannot remember a single lofty thought that came to me in the process.

Let us grant, by all means, that there is no shame in doing one's own work. It is honest and it is often necessary; there are times when it is even pleasant. What I deny is that it is more civilized. Civilization, if it means anything, means that we need not all do everything--that some of us may be spared the chores so that the chores may be done well, and the other things done at all. To go back to doing everything oneself is not to go forward. It is simply to go back.

There is another thing the plain-livers forget. The graceful traditions of a household--the way a table is laid, the way a guest is received, the hundred small courtesies that make a house a home and not a shelter--do not keep themselves alive. Somebody has to keep them. They live in the hands of people who know them and practise them daily, and a great many of those people have been servants. When the cook and the parlour-maid go, a good deal more goes with them than the cooking and the dusting. A whole body of knowledge goes, and I have not noticed that the high thinkers are in any hurry to learn it.

For the end of all this levelling is not a nation of philosophers; it is a nation of amateurs. If every one is to do his own laundering and his own carpentry and his own nursing, then every one will do all of them badly. No one will have time to do any one thing well--least of all to think. It is a strange ideal that would make us all indifferent cooks in order that we may all be indifferent scholars. I, for one, would rather have a good cook in the kitchen and a good book in my hand; and I cannot see that either the cook or the book is the worse for the arrangement.
