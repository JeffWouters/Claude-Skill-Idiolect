Anyone who has spent time in more than one engineering team will probably have noticed that some teams seem to get better much faster than others, and that the difference is not always explained by talent. In my experience, one of the more reliable signs of a team that is improving is simply how freely its members ask questions. Where people are willing to say that they do not understand something, to ask how a system actually works or why a decision was made, knowledge travels quickly and misunderstandings are caught before they harden. Where such questions are rare, each person is left to piece things together privately, and the team as a whole learns far more slowly than any of its members might individually.

Why, then, is open learning so uncommon? Part of the answer, I think, lies in the peculiar role that knowledge plays in technical work. In most engineering teams, what you know is not only a means of getting things done but also a form of status, and to admit that you do not know something is to spend a little of that status in public. For junior engineers this cost is usually tolerated, since ignorance is expected of them. For more senior people it can feel considerably higher. Someone who has been hired, promoted or relied upon because of their expertise may feel that each admitted gap chips away at the very thing that justifies their position, and so they learn to hide their uncertainty, or to resolve it quietly after hours rather than asking a colleague in the moment.

The difficulty is that this behaviour is contagious. When the most experienced people in a team never appear to be learning, everyone else draws the obvious conclusion: that learning is something one grows out of, and that the mark of seniority is to have stopped needing it. It seems to me that this gets the matter almost exactly backwards. The engineers I would most want to work alongside are not those who know the most at any given moment but those who remain genuinely curious, who treat the limits of their knowledge as a place to explore rather than a secret to protect. Real competence, on this view, is not a fixed stock of answers but a continuing capacity to learn, and it shows itself most clearly in the willingness to be seen doing so.

If that is right, then making learning acceptable is less a matter of training programmes than of example. A single senior engineer asking a plain question in a meeting may do more for a team's culture than any number of policies, because it demonstrates that curiosity is not a weakness to be concealed. Perhaps the simplest thing any of us can do is to ask the question we would rather not be seen asking.
