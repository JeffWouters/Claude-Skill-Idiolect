There is a particular kind of unease that settles over experienced engineers when they are asked to adopt a new technology. It rarely announces itself as unease. More often it takes the form of reasoned objection: the old tool works perfectly well, the new one is immature, the migration will cost more than it saves. Some of these objections are entirely sound. But I suspect that beneath many of them lies something less comfortable to say aloud, namely the fear of looking unskilled. Someone who has spent years becoming fluent in one way of working is being asked, in effect, to become a beginner again, and to do so in front of colleagues who may have come to rely on their expertise.

Organisations, for their part, tend to speak warmly about growth. Learning is praised in values statements and performance reviews; curiosity is encouraged, at least in principle. What is less often provided is time. The new technology is expected to be adopted alongside existing commitments, fitted into the gaps between deadlines, and the implicit message is that learning is something one should manage to do without it appearing anywhere on the plan. It is hardly surprising, under these conditions, that people approach it with some reluctance.

The result is a peculiar style of learning, in which engineers acquire only the fragments they need to complete the task in front of them. A command is copied from somewhere, a configuration is adjusted until it works, and the underlying model of how the thing actually behaves is never quite formed. Knowledge gathered this way is fragile. It serves well enough until something unexpected happens, at which point it becomes clear that nobody really understands the system they have been operating. Perhaps the more honest approach would be to admit that learning takes time and discomfort, and to budget for both, rather than pretending that fluency can be assembled from scraps.
