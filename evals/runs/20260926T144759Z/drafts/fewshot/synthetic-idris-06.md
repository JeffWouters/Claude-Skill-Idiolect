When a team finally notices that its standing meetings have become more habit than help, the temptation is to sweep them all away at once. There is a certain appeal in the clean calendar, the sense of reclaiming hours that had been quietly lost, and I understand why the gesture is so often made. I am less convinced that it is wise. Repeating meetings, whatever their faults, provide something that is easy to overlook until it disappears: a regular point of contact, a moment each week in which the people who share a piece of work are reliably in the same place. Much of what holds a team together happens in the margins of such meetings, in the brief exchanges before the agenda begins or the question someone would not otherwise have thought to ask. Scrap the meetings entirely and that contact tends to go with them, often without anyone noticing until the team has drifted apart.

The better course, it seems to me, is neither to abolish these meetings nor to leave them untouched, but to ask each one, from time to time, to justify itself again. What is this meeting for now, as opposed to when it began? Who needs to be here, and for how long? Would anything important be lost if it happened less often, or ended altogether? These are simple questions, and yet they are rarely put, perhaps because the meeting has become so familiar that it no longer seems like something that could be otherwise.

I should admit that the review is not a comfortable exercise. Proposing that a meeting shrink or stop can feel like a criticism of whoever started it, or of the people who value it, and there is always someone for whom a particular gathering matters more than the others realise. That awkwardness is, I think, part of its usefulness, since it forces into the open a conversation that would otherwise never happen. In practice, the result is seldom dramatic. Many meetings turn out to deserve a shorter slot or a smaller audience; a few, on reflection, have genuinely run their course and can be allowed to end.
