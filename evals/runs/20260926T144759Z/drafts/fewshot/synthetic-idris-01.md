Most teams, if pressed, could not say exactly why their weekly meeting exists. It is simply there, occupying the same hour on the same day, as fixed a feature of the calendar as the weekend. I have come to think that repeating meetings behave less like tools than like rituals, and that, like many rituals, they have a remarkable capacity to outlive whatever first gave them meaning. The words are still spoken and the chairs are still filled; what has quietly departed is the reason anyone gathered in the first place.

It is worth remembering that such meetings rarely begin as empty gestures. Almost always they start as a sensible answer to a real problem: work that kept falling between two people, a decision that needed a regular place to be made, a group that had drifted out of touch and needed somewhere to find each other again. In those early weeks the meeting earns its hour without difficulty, because everyone in the room can feel the problem it was invented to solve. The trouble is that a successful meeting tends to dissolve its own justification. Once the problem has been addressed, the meeting continues, carried along by habit and by a vague sense that stopping it would be a kind of negligence.

What makes this so difficult to notice is that the decline is gradual rather than sudden. There is no single week in which the meeting becomes useless; there is only a slow thinning, in which the agenda shortens, the updates grow more ceremonial and the conversation drifts towards whatever happens to be on people's minds. Nobody is responsible for observing this, and so nobody does. Perhaps the more honest question to ask of any standing meeting is not whether it is useful today but whether we would invent it now, knowing what we know. I suspect that the answer, surprisingly often, would be no, and that we continue less out of conviction than out of a reluctance to be the one who asks.
