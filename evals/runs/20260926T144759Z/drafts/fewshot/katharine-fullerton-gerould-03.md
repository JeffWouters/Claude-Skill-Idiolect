We are all, I suppose, acquainted with the person who "never tells a lie." She will say so herself, and more than once. You have only to show her your new hat, or read her the little verses you wrote last winter, and she will let you know--with the air of one doing a public service--exactly what she thinks of them. She calls this candour. I have another name for it, but I will keep it to myself; for I, at least, believe in the polite falsehood.

Let us be honest about the polite falsehood, since honesty is in question. When I tell my hostess that I have had a charming evening, I am not always reporting the facts. When I say that the baby is a beautiful baby, I may be stretching a point. But nobody is deceived to any purpose, and somebody is spared. These small courtesies are not really statements at all; they are the oil that keeps the social machine from shrieking. To refuse them is not to be more truthful than one's neighbours. It is only to be more uncomfortable to live with.

For there is a kind of truth-telling that is nothing but self-indulgence. The blunt person enjoys her bluntness; she enjoys it far more than any one else does. She has the pleasure of speaking her mind, and her friend has the pain of hearing it--and, frankly, I do not see that the arrangement is fair. It is a luxury she pays for, sooner or later, with her friendships. One by one they fall away, not because they could not bear the truth, but because they could not bear being made the occasion for her virtue. [example needed]

The candid critic has, of course, her defence ready. Her praise, she tells us, is worth having precisely because it is rare. When she says a thing is good, you may be sure it is good. I used to believe this; I believe it no longer. Praise does not gain in value by being hoarded, any more than a smile does. And the habit of criticism is a habit like any other--it grows on what it feeds on. The woman who begins by withholding praise from things that do not deserve it ends by withholding it from everything. She has trained her eye to find the flaw, and presently she cannot see anything else. I have known such people (and not the unkindest, either) who could not, at the last, have praised anything at all without a "but."
