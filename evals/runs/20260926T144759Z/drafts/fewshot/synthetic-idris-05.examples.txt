It seems to me that the best onboarding treats the newcomer not merely as someone to be taught but as someone who can teach the team something about itself. This means inviting their observations early, before they have learned to stop noticing; it means treating their confusion as information about where the team's knowledge is poorly recorded; it means, perhaps, appointing someone whose explicit responsibility is to make the newcomer feel welcome rather than leaving this to whoever happens to have time. None of these practices is especially elaborate. They require only a shift in perspective, from seeing onboarding as a transfer of knowledge in one direction to seeing it as an exchange from which both parties can learn. The consequence, in my experience, is not only faster integration but a team that remains more aware of its own assumptions.

---

The postmortem, in the end, is one of the few occasions on which organisations formally set aside time to reflect on their own fallibility. That is a remarkable thing, when you consider how much of working life is organised around the projection of competence and control. The practice asks us to look directly at the moments when our systems and our judgement failed, and to do so in the company of others, without the protective distance that usually allows us to avoid such reflection. Whether it succeeds depends less on the format than on the willingness of those involved to be genuinely curious about what happened. Perhaps that is the deepest lesson the postmortem has to offer: that learning from failure is not primarily a technical skill but a moral one, requiring a kind of courage that no template can supply.

---

What would it mean to approach tools more thoughtfully? It seems to me that the essential step is to separate the conversation about tools from the conversation about practices, and to have the second conversation first. Before asking which system we should use, we might ask what we are actually trying to do, what is preventing us from doing it, and whether the obstacle lies in the software or in ourselves. Sometimes the answer will genuinely point to a tool, in which case the migration can proceed with a clearer sense of purpose; sometimes it will point elsewhere, to decisions that need to be made or conversations that need to be had, and no amount of new software will substitute for them. This is slower and less satisfying than choosing something new, since it denies us the pleasure of a fresh start. It is also, I suspect, considerably more likely to change anything.
