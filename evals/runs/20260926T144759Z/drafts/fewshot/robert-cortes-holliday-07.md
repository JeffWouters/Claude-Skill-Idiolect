If you wish to know what a person is really like, ask the person who has sold him something.  I do not mean his friends.  His friends see him at his best, or at least at his most careful.  I mean the girl behind the glove counter, the man at the ticket window, the clerk in the bookshop.  These people see him when he does not think it matters how he behaves.

It is part of the business of selling things to be spoken to rudely.  You are expected to take it.  You do take it.  You are spoken to sharply, and you are spoken to impatiently, and you are spoken to as if you were not quite there, as if you were a part of the counter that happened to be able to fetch things down from a shelf.  All this you take, day in and day out, with a pleasant face.  And after a while, taking it, you come to have a pretty clear notion of people.  You come to know who is a gentleman and who is not, and you find, oftener than you might think, that it is not always the one you would have picked from his clothes.

So when I say that the novelist was courteous to me I do not say it lightly.  He spoke to me as one man speaks to another.  There was nothing of the great man about him.  There was nothing, either, of that other manner, worse in its way, of the great man who is being very kind to the clerk.  He was simply in earnest.  He asked me questions, and he waited for my answers, and he listened to them, as if they were answers that it was worth a man's while to hear.  He was, in the plain sense of the word, polite.  I do not know that I have met with anything quite like it before or since, across a counter.

What he wanted to know about was the trade.  How much of what we sold, he asked, was British fiction?  What share of the stock?  He wanted, it seemed, to get at the figures.  I told him what I could, which was [example needed].  He took it in thoughtfully, and nodded, and seemed to turn it over.

Then I mentioned, I forget in what connection, a certain very popular novelist, a writer whose books at that time we could hardly keep upon the tables.  I thought he would be interested.  He was not interested.  He dismissed him.  He did it quite without heat, in a sentence, as a man might brush a crumb off his sleeve.  It was not jealousy; I am sure of that.  It was simply that, so far as he was concerned, there was nothing there to talk about.  And then he went back, very earnestly, to the question of British fiction.
