I once had a long talk with a man who had been a forger, and who had, as he assured me, given up the profession. I had supposed that a man who had lived outside the law would have no very nice distinctions as to what was right and what was wrong. I was mistaken. He had a code, and he held to it with a strictness that would have done credit to a deacon.

I asked him, in the course of our conversation, whether there was anything he had considered really wrong in the days of his activity. He answered promptly that there was. He had always despised petty theft. The man who took a purse or a watch was, in his eyes, a low fellow, beneath the notice of a gentleman.

I ventured to suggest that, after all, there might be some resemblance between the pickpocket and the forger. Both, I said, were engaged in getting other people's money without their consent. He would not hear of it. The comparison, he said, was entirely unfair. There was no more likeness between the two than between a sneak and a soldier.

Then he explained his point of view. The pickpocket took advantage of the unwary. He preyed upon people who were not expecting him and could not defend themselves. But the forger was engaged in a contest of skill. On the other side were the banks, with all their resources, and the detectives, who were well paid to catch him. It was a fair fight, and the odds were against him. If he won, it was because he was the cleverer man.

I confess that I found the argument hard to answer on its own ground. He had simply taken the moral judgment that belongs to honest work and transferred it to his own trade. It was the pride of the artist, and it kept him, in his own estimation, a respectable member of society.
