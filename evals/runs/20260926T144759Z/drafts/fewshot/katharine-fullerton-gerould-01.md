There is a certain kind of well-meaning person--we all know her, and some of us have been her--who has decided that the surest road to equality lies through the kitchen door. She will keep no servant, because to keep a servant is to hold another human being beneath one; she will have no fine linen, no old silver, no tea served otherwise than out of the pot at the table, because refinement is a wall, and walls divide. I respect her motive. I do not think anything could be more honourable than her wish. But I am bound to say that she is mistaken, and mistaken on every count.

Let us begin with her clothes. She has put off her good frocks, very likely, and goes about in something plain and serviceable, so that no one shall feel the difference between them. But the difference is there, and every one feels it. Class is not a matter of what one wears; it is a matter of how one speaks, what one has read, what one takes for granted at breakfast. You cannot dress it away. The shabby coat on the educated woman deceives nobody--least of all the people it is meant to reassure. They know perfectly well that she could buy another to-morrow, and that she has chosen not to; and I am not sure they like her the better for the choice.

Then there is the matter of service. It is the fashion to speak as if domestic service were the last refuge of the downtrodden, and as if the girl behind the counter were a free woman by comparison. I wonder. The girl at the counter (and she is always held up as the lucky one!) stands all day under the eye of a floorwalker, at wages that must cover her room and her board; the cook in a decent household has both, and a kitchen of her own, and a mistress who--if she is worth anything--is a human being and not a system. I do not say that every place is a good place. I say that a good place may be a good deal freer than a good shop, and that the woman who refuses to employ anyone, on principle, has not freed a single girl from anything.

But what I find hardest to forgive is the talk. It is one thing to give up pretty things because one cannot afford them, or because one has not the time to care for them. It is quite another to give them up and then to announce that the bare table is "nicer," that the thick cup is "more honest," that one really prefers things so. One does not. Nobody does. The absence of a lovely thing is an absence, and to call it a presence is a small dishonesty that--frankly--I would rather not have at my table.
