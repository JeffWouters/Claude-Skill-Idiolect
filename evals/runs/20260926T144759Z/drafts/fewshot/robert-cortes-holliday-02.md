Most people who are looking for rooms are unhappy.  I have observed this.  You will see them upon the steps of houses in the side streets, with a newspaper folded back to the advertisements in one hand and a pencil in the other, and in their faces a look of settled grievance.  The trouble is not with the rooms.  The trouble is that the person looking for rooms wants a flat, and not merely a flat but a grand flat, with a man in a uniform at the door, a lift, a view, and a bath with the water hot at all hours.  He has seen these places.  He has walked past them.  And after that the third floor back with the gas ring does not look quite what it did.

How the grand flats are paid for is a mystery to me.  The rents are something tremendous.  I have looked at the figures, and the figures are [example needed] a year, and more.  Now, who are the people who live in them?  You never hear of them.  They are not in the newspapers.  They are not famous for anything.  Nobody knows who they are.  And yet there they are, by the hundred, by the thousand, row above row, lights in every window, paying these rents, quarter day in and quarter day out, without, apparently, the smallest inconvenience.  I have given a good deal of thought to it, and I cannot make it out.

I, for my part, looked at a room the other day in a house off a little lane in Greenwich Village.  It was a shabby house.  The front of it had been painted at one time, but not lately.  The landlady took me up.  The room had been, she said, "just vacated."  It had.  It had been vacated so recently that the last tenant, in a manner of speaking, was still there.  He had been, it appeared, a man of habits.  He had smoked, and not in moderation; the curtains knew it.  He had not cared for the waste-paper basket; the floor knew it.  He had, it would seem, [example needed].  The landlady said he was "a very quiet gentleman."  I dare say he was.  I did not take the room.
