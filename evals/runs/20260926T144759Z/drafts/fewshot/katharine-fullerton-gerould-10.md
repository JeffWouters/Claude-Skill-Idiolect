I have never understood why the simple life should be called simple. It is, so far as I have been able to observe, the most complicated life there is. The people who lead it are forever busy--baking their own bread, mending their own shoes, weeding their own gardens and teaching their own children--and they have not a moment left for anything else. They set out to escape the burdens of civilization, and they have only exchanged a few large burdens for a great many small ones.

For if you do everything yourself, you do nothing else. That is the whole of the matter. There are only so many hours in the day, and the woman who spends them all on the necessities of life will have none to spend on the luxuries of it--on reading, on talk, on the leisurely friendships that were, I had supposed, the point of living at all. She has simplified her possessions, very likely. She has not simplified her days.

The remedy, it seems to me, lies in exactly the opposite direction. What we need is not less specialization, but more: more people doing the one thing they can do well, and fewer of us doing badly the things we were never meant to do. Let us have more servants, by all means, and not fewer--and let us pay them properly and treat them decently, and let them be as expert in their work as we should like to be in ours. That is not a luxury. It is the only arrangement under which any one has time to think.

Oh, I know the answer. It is not right, we are told, that some should serve while others are served; and so the high-minded give up their servants and do their own work, by way of protest. But whom does the protest help? Not the servant, who has lost a place. Not the high-minded, who has lost her leisure. Voluntary self-denial is a very comfortable thing to the conscience, but I have never seen that it put a single extra loaf on any one else's table.
