It is, I suppose, a small vulgarity to tell tales of the customers.  Still, I do not see that I am worse than my betters in this.  Coachmen have done it.  The man who drove for years for [person] has told, I believe, what that gentleman said on the box and off it.  Waiters have done it.  The waiter who brought the soup to [person] at [place] has let it be known how that great man took his soup, and what he said of it.  Nobody appears to have thought any the less of the coachman or of the waiter.  And a clerk in a bookshop, who sees people when they are thinking of nothing so much as themselves, is, I venture to say, in quite as good a position as a coachman.  So I shall gossip a little.

Our acquaintance with the novelist was--merely professional.  I had admired him for a long time.  I had read him, I think, pretty nearly all.  The evening before I saw him I had been talking about him.  We had gone into the matter at some length, at [place], a friend and I, and had settled, as one does settle such things in the evening, pretty much everything there was to be settled about him: what he meant, what he did not mean, and what he would be like if one met him.  Then, the next morning, there he was.  It gave me a queer feeling.  It was, you might say, a little uncanny, as if talking of a man had called him up out of the air, and he had walked in off the street to see what all the fuss was about.

He was not alone.  With him there was a companion, a very well-dressed person, of the sort who is well dressed rather thoroughly, from the hat down.  This companion did not want a book.  This companion did not appear to want anything.  He stood about.  He looked at nothing in particular, idly, with the air of a man who is waiting in a shop because somebody else has business there, and who is prepared to go on waiting, patiently, for as long as it may take.
