# Judge packet synthetic-noor-08

## The passage (by the author)

A plastic bucket. Warm water, about four litres. 200 millilitres of white vinegar. A soft cloth for the frames. A chamois for the sills. A stack of old newspaper, torn into half sheets. The short stepladder from the shed.

I pick a grey day. That is the first rule. Sun dries the glass before you can wipe it, and then you get streaks. Grey and still is best. A little wind is fine. Rain is not.

I start upstairs and work down. Drips run down, never up. If you do the ground floor first, you do it twice.

First the frames. I wipe the sills and the frames with the cloth and plain water. Dead flies. Old spider silk. A grit of dust in the corners. If you skip this, the dirt from the frame ends up on the glass.

So then the glass. I dip the cloth in the vinegar water and wet the whole pane. Not dripping. Just wet. Then I take a half sheet of newspaper and crumple it into a loose ball. About the size of a fist.

You wipe in circles first. Then top to bottom in straight lines. The paper squeaks when the glass is clean. It is a dry, thin sound, like a finger on a balloon. When it stops squeaking, the paper is too wet. It goes in the bag and I take a new one.

The ink does no harm. People expect grey hands and grey glass. The hands, yes. The glass comes up clear. I think the ink helps it polish. I have never proved it.

The outside is the hard part. The upstairs windows open outward on a hinge. From inside I can reach most of the outer pane. Most, not all. The bottom corner of the back bedroom I cannot reach at all. I lean out as far as is sensible and leave a strip about the width of a hand.

## Draft C

Vinegar and newspaper. That is the whole method. It is cheap and it works.

The kit. A bucket of warm water with a good splash of white vinegar in it. [ratio needed]. A cloth for the frames. A stack of old newspaper. A step ladder. And an overcast day.

That last one matters. Do not clean windows in the sun. The sun dries the glass before you get to it and leaves streaks. You wipe, it dries, you see the lines. You wipe again. More lines. On a grey day the glass stays wet long enough for you to finish. It is also easier to see what you are doing without the glare.

Start at the top floor. Always. Work down. Anything that drips, drips onto glass you have yet to clean. Do it the other way round and you clean the downstairs windows twice.

Frames before glass. Wipe the frames and the sills with the cloth first. They hold dust and dead insects and bits of cobweb. If you do the glass first, you knock all that onto the clean glass when you do the frames. So frames, then glass.

## Draft D

Cleaning windows with vinegar and old newspaper is one of those household methods that sounds old-fashioned until you try it. The equipment is simple: a bucket or spray bottle of vinegar solution, [ratio of vinegar to water needed], a cloth for the frames, and a stack of old newspaper. A step ladder helps for the higher panes.

Pick an overcast day if you can. In bright sunshine the solution dries on the glass before you can wipe it, leaving streaks. Under cloud it stays wet long enough to work, and you can see the smears more clearly without glare.

Start on the top floor and work down. Drips run downward, so cleaning the upper windows first means anything that falls lands on glass you have not yet done, rather than spoiling work already finished. For the same reason, clean the frames before the glass. Wipe down the frames and sills first, clearing dust and cobwebs, so that dirt from the frame does not end up smeared across a clean pane.

Then the glass. Wet it with the solution, crumple a sheet of newspaper into a loose ball, and wipe. Use firm circular movements to lift the dirt, then finish with straight strokes, top to bottom, to leave the surface even. The paper absorbs the moisture without leaving lint behind, which is the whole point of using it instead of a cloth.
