# Judge packet synthetic-idris-02

## The passage (by the author)

There is also the question of to whom a handover is made, which is often decided less by thought than by availability. The work tends to pass to whoever has the most room in their week or the least ability to refuse, and that person inherits not only the systems but the half-finished promises and unspoken expectations of everyone who used to rely on someone else. I do not wish to suggest that the usual remedies are wrong; a written runbook, a list of owned services and a few recorded walkthroughs are genuinely better than nothing, and a team that insists on them is behaving responsibly. Yet they rest on an assumption that knowledge is a parcel, something that can be wrapped by one person and unwrapped by another, when in practice it behaves rather more like a relationship, which must be built slowly and in some sense cannot be delegated at all. I suspect most of us know this privately; we simply lack a format that admits it.

What might a more honest handover look like? It seems to me that it would begin by acknowledging what cannot be passed on, and by spending less effort on completeness and more on introductions—to the people who know the history, to the customers who will notice first, to the corners of the system where the departing engineer was always a little afraid. It might mean choosing the recipient deliberately, perhaps even choosing several, so that understanding is spread across the team rather than settled on a single new pair of shoulders. It might also mean that the person leaving remains reachable for a while, not as an obligation but as an ordinary courtesy between colleagues. None of this requires a new template or a new tool, only a willingness to treat the handover as the start of a slower process rather than an event that finishes on a particular afternoon. It is less tidy, however careful you may be; it asks the one leaving to admit how much they carried without ever saying so.

## Draft A

When an engineer leaves, their responsibilities have to go somewhere, and the way this is usually decided is less deliberate than it ought to be. The work tends to pass to whoever seems to have some spare capacity, which is a reasonable criterion for scheduling but a poor one for continuity, since the person with the most room in their week is rarely the person best placed to understand what they are receiving, which is often more than anyone has written down. Alongside the systems and the tickets come a set of implicit commitments: promises made in passing to another team, an understanding about which alerts can safely be ignored, a sense of who needs to be warned before anything changes. The recipient inherits these without knowing they exist, and usually discovers them only when one is broken.

The customary remedy is documentation, and it would be foolish to argue against it. A good handover document is valuable; it records decisions, points to the places where things are kept, and gives the successor somewhere to begin. It seems to me, however, that documents can only carry part of what matters, because much of what the departing engineer knows is not information so much as relationship: knowing whom to ask, how far to trust a particular estimate, which colleague will quietly fix a problem and which will need to be persuaded. That kind of understanding is built between people, and it is rather difficult to transfer on paper.

What might work better is to treat the last weeks as a period of introductions rather than a period of writing. The leaver's time is perhaps best spent connecting their successors with the people they depend on, and walking them through the areas that are fragile, where a small misunderstanding could have large consequences. It also helps to spread ownership across several colleagues instead of concentrating it in one, so that no single person carries the whole inheritance. Where it can be arranged, keeping the leaver contactable for a while after they go allows the questions nobody thought to ask to be answered after all.

## Draft B

When an engineer leaves, who inherits what they did? In most teams the answer is decided less by fit than by availability: the work goes to whoever appears to have spare capacity, which is to say whoever happens to be least visibly busy in the week the resignation is announced. That person receives not only the systems and tickets that were formally assigned to the leaver but a quantity of implicit commitments that nobody wrote down, such as the promise to review an integration for another team next quarter or the understanding that a particular service would be rewritten before it caused trouble. It may be that these unrecorded obligations are the larger part of the inheritance.

The customary response is to ask the departing engineer for documentation, and documents do help; a clear account of how a system is deployed is worth having. Understanding, however, is relational rather than textual. Much of what the leaver knows lives in their acquaintance with the people who depend on their work, in their sense of which colleague to ask about which oddity, and in a feeling for where the system is fragile that they would struggle to articulate even if asked. A handover document captures the parts that were already easy to explain.

It seems to me that the final weeks would be better spent on introductions than on writing. The leaver might take their successors to meet the people who rely on the work, and walk them through the areas they themselves regard as fragile, since these are the places where an inheritor is most likely to be surprised. Ownership, too, might be spread across several colleagues rather than concentrated in one, which reduces the chance that the same problem simply recurs when the next person leaves. The leaver, where willing, could also remain contactable for a while after their departure, not as a permanent consultant but as someone who can answer the questions nobody knew to ask. Perhaps the real handover is not the transfer of knowledge but the transfer of relationships, which no document can perform on behalf of anyone.

## Draft C

When an engineer leaves, their responsibilities are usually handed to whoever appears to have the most room in their schedule. This is understandable, since someone has to take the work and the person with spare capacity is the obvious candidate. But spare capacity is a curious basis on which to choose an heir. It says nothing about whether the recipient understands the systems in question, or knows the people who depend on them, or is aware of the promises that the departing colleague made along the way. And there are always such promises: the informal undertaking to look at a particular problem next quarter, the understanding with another team about how an interface will evolve, the quiet habit of checking something each week that nobody ever wrote down. The recipient inherits these commitments without knowing they exist, and discovers them, as a rule, only when one of them is broken.

The customary response is to ask the leaver to write things down, and documents certainly help. Yet understanding of this kind is not primarily a matter of recorded facts. It is relational; it lives in the knowledge of whom to ask, whose judgement to trust, which parts of a system are sturdy and which are held together by attention. A document can gesture at this, but it cannot transmit it.

What might a better handover look like? The first priority, I would suggest, is introductions rather than inventories: the leaver's remaining time is better spent connecting successors to the people they will need than in producing another page of notes. The second is to spend what attention remains on the fragile areas, the places where a small misunderstanding could cause a serious failure, rather than distributing it evenly across everything. The third is to resist handing the whole portfolio to a single person, and instead to spread ownership across several colleagues, so that no one becomes the new sole point of failure. And finally, where it can be arranged, the leaver might remain contactable for a while after departure. None of this removes the loss, but it makes the loss considerably easier to absorb.

## Draft D

When an engineer leaves, their work has to go somewhere, and it usually goes to whoever has room for it. That sounds sensible, but spare capacity is a poor way to choose an owner. The person with the emptiest calendar is not necessarily the person who understands the system, the customers or the history behind the decisions, and they are rarely the one best placed to carry it forward.

What they inherit is also larger than it looks. Alongside the services and the tickets come the implicit commitments: the promise to look at another team's integration next month, the understanding that a certain alert always gets a quick reply, the favour half-agreed in a hallway conversation. None of this is written down, because the person who made those promises never needed it to be. The new owner discovers them one surprise at a time.

The usual response is documentation. Write it all down before the last day, the thinking goes, and the knowledge will survive. Documents do help, and a leaver who writes a good handover note is doing everyone a favour. But understanding is mostly relational. Knowing a system means knowing who to ask, who depends on it, who gets nervous when it changes and why. A document can list those people; it cannot introduce you to them.

So the handover should put introductions first. Use the remaining time to connect the new owners with the people on the other side of the work, the stakeholders, the neighbouring teams, the one colleague who remembers why things are the way they are. Alongside that, point at the fragile areas: the parts that break in strange ways, the workarounds that nobody would guess, the places where a careful change still goes wrong.

It also helps to spread ownership across several colleagues rather than dropping everything on one person. That shares the load and means no single inheritor becomes the new point of failure.

Finally, keep the departing engineer reachable for a while, if they are willing. A short question answered in a few minutes can save days of guessing. The handover does not end on the last day; it ends when the new owners stop needing to ask.
