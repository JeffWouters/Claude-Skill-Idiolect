# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-03

## The passage (by the author)

This place of which I speak has, particularly in the evenings, much the air of a club, where choice spirits of the locality foregather to discuss politics, it may be, and the more engrossing forms of sport, such as boxing. And perhaps relish a little game at cards. I often pass this place at night and feel a warmth of spirit at the hum of jovial social contact within.

I like, too, the way the undertakers' shops of the humble and obscure carry on cheek by jowl with the familiar, homely, friendly things of life. This gives Death a neighborly sort of air. On my walks in that quarter I always give a friendly glance to the windows of a "Cremation Ass'n" on Eighth Avenue, on one side of it a delicatessen shop, on the other a "loan office," in the basement below a plumber.

Attractive, too, is it to consider how founders of tidy undertaking houses have become personages and are held in revered esteem. For they are not, it would seem, like unto those who have established just ordinary businesses. This I will show you:

At a corner of Twenty-third Street, over a telegraph office, is an establishment of some caste. Window legend reads: "Undertakers—Cremations—Night and Day—Interments in all Cemeteries." The last phrase reminds me of the way my old friend James Huneker used to date his letters to me from Brooklyn. They began, "Flatbush by the C—emeteries." But that's not the point. It's a pity the alert English writer who recently visited us and discovered a statue of General Grant in Grant Park, overlooking the Blackstone (where nobody had ever seen one before), and that the huge bust of Washington Irving in Bryant Park, behind the New York Public Library, was an effigy of Father George Washington—it's a rotten shame E. V. Lucas missed this corner while here.

## Draft A

In the more modest neighbourhoods of Manhattan the undertaker is not a remote and solemn figure. He is a neighbour, and his shop is a neighbourhood institution. I know of one [detail needed: which shop, and where] which, of an evening, serves very much the purpose of a village store. The lights are on, the door is open, and the men of the block drift in to sit and smoke and exchange the news of the day. Nobody appears to find anything peculiar in this. Why should they? The proprietor is a sociable man, the chairs are comfortable, and the business, after all, is one that everybody will require sooner or later.

It is the same with the cremation companies. You come upon them, in these streets, sandwiched between a delicatessen and a pawnshop, or next door to a plumber, with nothing in particular to distinguish them from their neighbours but the lettering in the window. I have come to think this arrangement rather wholesome. Death, so situated, loses a good deal of its pomp and something of its terror. It becomes an ordinary transaction of the street, like buying a pound of ham, pawning a watch, or having the pipes attended to; familiar, practical, even a little homely.

## Draft B

In the more modest neighbourhoods of Manhattan the undertaker is not, as he is elsewhere, a person one avoids. He is a neighbour. His shop is on the corner, or a few doors from it, and of an evening it becomes, so to speak, a kind of club. Men drop in to smoke and to talk. They discuss the weather, the price of things, the affairs of the block; they do not, so far as I have been able to discover, discuss death at all. And why should they? Death is not a stranger here. It keeps shop in the same street as everybody else. You will find an establishment which offers cremation next door to a delicatessen, and across the way a pawnshop, and a little further on a plumber. One goes in for a pound of ham, and one comes out past the urns. I do not know that anybody finds this morbid. It is simply one of the businesses of the neighbourhood, and after a while it becomes as familiar, and nearly as homely, as the others.

## Draft C

The undertaking business, in the humbler parts of Manhattan, has a character of its own, and nobody seems to have noticed it. In the fashionable quarters death is a thing kept out of sight. In the modest neighbourhoods it keeps shop, like anybody else, and it keeps its lights on in the evening.

There is such a shop not far from me. By day it is a place of business; you pass it and think no more about it. But of an evening it is something else again. It is a sort of club. The men of the block drop in. They sit about, they smoke, they talk over the affairs of the neighbourhood. The proprietor presides, very genial. Nobody appears to find anything odd in the arrangement. Why should they? Where else is there to go?

Walk a little further and you will come upon a cremation firm. It is next door to a delicatessen. A few doors along, one of the same kind stands between a pawnshop and a plumber. The plumber mends your pipes; the pawnbroker lends you a little on your watch; the delicatessen sells you a pound of ham; and the cremation people will, when the time comes, attend to you. It is all one street, and all one life, and death, set down among these homely neighbours, becomes quite a familiar and everyday sort of thing. There is, I think, a good deal to be said for this.

Of course there are grander establishments. There is a funeral firm on Twenty-third Street which does things in a very large way indeed, very solemn, very handsome, with [example needed]. It is the undertaking business in its Sunday clothes.

And speaking of monuments, I am reminded of a certain English writer who visited us not long ago and wrote about our monuments, and got them, one after another, wrong. I will not go into his blunders. Perhaps it is enough that he made them.

## Draft D

In the plainer neighbourhoods of Manhattan, the undertaker is not a figure apart. He is a shopkeeper among shopkeepers, and his premises sit on the same block as everyone else's. In one street I know, the local funeral shop does more than bury people. In the evenings it becomes a kind of social club. Men drop in after work, stand about in the front room, talk over the day's news and the neighbourhood's quarrels, and go home again. Nobody seems to find it strange. The place is warm and lit, the proprietor is a neighbour, and there is always somewhere to sit.

Walk a few more blocks and you will find cremation firms wedged in beside delicatessens, pawnshops and plumbers. One window shows sausages, the next shows watches and wedding rings left in pledge, the next shows lengths of pipe, and the next quietly offers to reduce you to ashes. I have come to think this arrangement does the neighbourhood a kindness. Death here is not hidden away behind hushed doors in a distant district. It is simply one more trade on the street, as ordinary as having a sink repaired, and people pass it on the way to buy their lunch.

There are grander establishments, of course. On 23rd Street stands a funeral firm of a very different sort, with [details needed: its appearance and manner], where death is conducted with all the ceremony money can buy. It is impressive, and it is a little chilly.

I was reminded of all this by a visiting English writer, [name needed], who has lately been telling us what he thinks of our monuments. His opinions were confident. His facts were less so, and he managed to get several of them wrong [example needed]. I would suggest that before he lectures us again on how Americans honour their dead, he spend an evening in one of these neighbourhood shops. He might learn something, and at the very least he would be offered a chair.
