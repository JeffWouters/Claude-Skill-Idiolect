# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-03

## The passage (by the author)

This adaptation to nature is easy for us, for we are rustics by inheritance. Our ancestors lived in the open, and kept their flocks and were mighty hunters long before towns were ever thought of. So when we go into the woods in the spring, our self-consciousness leaves us and we speedily make ourselves at home. We take things for granted, and are not careful about trifles. A great many things are going on, but the multiplicity does not distract us. We do not need to understand.

For we have primal sympathies which are very good substitutes for intelligence. We do not worry because nature does not get on faster with her work. When we go out on the hills on a spring morning, as our forbears did ten thousand years ago, it does not fret us to consider that things are going on very much as they did then. The sap is mounting in the trees; the wild flowers are pushing out of the sod; the free citizens of the woods are pursuing their vocations without regard to our moralities. A great deal is going on, but nothing has come to a dramatic culmination.

Our innate rusticity makes us accept all this in the spirit in which it is offered to us. It is nature's way and we like it, because we are used to it. We take what is set before us and ask no questions. It is spring. We do not stop to inquire as to whether this spring is an improvement on last spring or on the spring of the year 400 B.C. There is a timelessness about our enjoyment. We are not thinking of events set in a chronological order, but of a process which loses nothing by reason of repetition.

Our attitude toward a city is usually quite different. We are not at our ease. We are querulous and anxious, and our interest takes a feverish turn. For the cities of our Western world are new-fangled contrivances which we are not used to, and we are worried as we try to find out whether they will work. These aggregations of humanity have not existed long enough to seem to belong to the nature of things. It is exciting to be invited to "see Seattle grow," but the exhibition does not yield a "harvest of a quiet eye." If Seattle should cease to grow while we are looking at it, what should we do then?

## Draft A

Walk into a wood in spring and something in you relaxes almost at once. You do not need to know the names of the trees or the birds. You do not need to understand what is going on in the soil or why the leaves are coming out this week rather than last. You simply enjoy it. I think this is because the instincts that let us feel at home there were handed down to us. For most of our history people lived close to fields and woods, and whatever we have forgotten, our nerves still remember how to be comfortable in them.

One of the things those instincts accept without complaint is that nothing much happens. Nature does not please by surprising us. It pleases by doing the same thing again. Spring comes as it came last year and the year before, and the pleasure lies precisely in its coming back. There is no plot, no climax, no sense that we must keep watching to find out how it ends. A wood does not need to be going anywhere. We do not ask whether it is making progress, and we would find the question a little absurd. Nobody measures a meadow against its performance last season.

Cities are a different matter. When we look at a city we cannot help asking how it is doing. Is it growing? Is it growing fast enough? Will it keep growing, or has it already peaked? We watch it the way we watch a business or a patient, looking for signs. The easy, unmeasuring enjoyment we bring to the countryside simply is not available.

This is especially true of the young cities of the West. They have no long past for us to lean on, and we have no inherited feeling for them. Their streets and habits are unfamiliar. A town like [example needed] seems to have appeared almost out of nowhere, and that very speed makes us uneasy, because what arrived so quickly might leave just as quickly. So we keep an anxious eye on it. Every new building is read as evidence, every slack year as a warning. We fret over whether the growth will continue because growth is the only story the place has told us.

Perhaps, in time, these cities will become familiar enough to be enjoyed the way a wood is enjoyed, for simply being there and doing what they did last year. Until then we will go on watching them nervously, and going out to the countryside to rest.

## Draft B

Most of us go into the country to rest, and we come back from the city tired. The difference does not lie altogether in the scenery. It lies in ourselves, and in the long habits of mind we have inherited from ancestors who knew the fields and the woods far better than they knew any street.

When I walk in the spring woods, I do not feel called upon to understand what is going on about me. I have no notion of measuring it. The buds open, the birds return, and the brook runs as it ran last year; nobody asks me whether the season is an improvement upon the one before, and I do not ask myself. Something older than my own opinions is at ease there. My forefathers made their peace with the woods a great while ago, and I have come into the inheritance without any effort of my own.

Nature pleases us largely because she repeats herself. She has no taste for novelty, and she never works up to a climax. A dramatist who brought on the same scene every year would be hissed from the stage, but we go out each spring to see the old performance with undiminished delight. We know how it will end. That is precisely the charm of it. There is no suspense and therefore no anxiety, and the mind, relieved of the burden of expectation, is free to enjoy what is before it.

The city is another matter, and a young Western city most of all. Here nothing is familiar. There are no inherited instincts to tell us what to feel, for our ancestors never saw such a place, and so we look at it with a kind of nervous attention. We are always asking questions of it. Is it growing? Will it continue to grow, or has it already reached its limit? Every new building is a piece of evidence, and every vacant lot a cause for doubt.

This anxious watching is natural enough. A city of this sort is a promise and not yet an accomplishment, and a promise must be kept before one can rest in it with any confidence. We cannot sit down comfortably in a town whose future is still being argued, as we sit under a tree whose future was settled long before we were born. Our grandchildren will find in these streets, it may be, the repose we find in the woods. But that will require a considerable number of springs.

## Draft C

Why is it that a man who wanders through the woods in spring comes home refreshed, while the same man, walking through a city, comes home with a furrowed brow? The woods are not simpler than the city. They are, if anything, considerably more intricate, and a botanist might devote his whole life to a single acre without exhausting its curiosities; but the ordinary walker is not a botanist, and nobody expects him to become one.

The explanation, I suspect, is that we are countrymen by inheritance. Our instincts were formed in the fields many generations before our opinions were formed in the towns, and when we enter a wood we are on familiar ground in a sense that goes deeper than personal recollection. We feel no obligation to understand what we see. We do not inquire whether the oaks are making satisfactory progress, or whether this particular spring represents an improvement upon the last, or what the whole enterprise is likely to amount to in the end. We accept the performance as our ancestors accepted it. And we are content.

For nature does not please by novelty. She has been doing the same things for a very long time and shows no inclination to do anything different, bringing out the leaves in the same order and returning the same birds to sing the same songs. There is no climax toward which it all moves, no concluding act in which the plot is finally resolved and the audience dismissed. If there were, we should grow restless waiting for it. It is precisely the endless repetition that soothes us, since we know the story perfectly and like nothing better than to hear it told again.

A city is another matter. Our young Western cities have no such hold upon our instincts, for they are new, and we have not had time to grow accustomed to their manners. We cannot simply enjoy them, as we enjoy the woods, because we never know what they are going to do next. Every such city is an experiment, and we watch it as one watches any experiment whose outcome is uncertain, with a persistent and not altogether comfortable anxiety, asking whether its growth is healthy and whether it will continue.

This is not the temper of rest. The countryside asks nothing of us except that we look at it. The city asks that we believe in it, and belief, while the evidence is still coming in, is an exhausting occupation.

## Draft D

When I go into the woods in the spring, I am at ease. I do not ask what the woods are coming to. I do not inquire whether this year's leaves are an improvement on last year's, or whether the brook is making satisfactory progress toward the sea. I do not measure anything. I am content to look and to listen, and to let the season do what it pleases.

This is not because I understand the woods. I understand them very little. It is because I have inherited, from a long line of country-bred ancestors, a set of instincts which make me feel at home there. They knew the ways of the fields and the forests, and they have passed on to me, if not their knowledge, at least their confidence. I do not need to know why the buds open. I know that they will.

The charm of nature is that it does not try to surprise us. It has no novelties to offer, and it does not work up to a climax. It says the same thing over and over again, and we are never tired of hearing it. The robin that sings this morning sings the song that robins have always sung. If he were to strike out in a new direction and attempt something original, we should be disturbed. We do not go to the country for news. We go there to be told what we have heard before.

It is quite otherwise when I visit one of the young cities of the West. There I am not at ease at all. I find myself watching it anxiously, as one might watch a child who has grown very fast and may or may not keep on growing. I want to know how many people it had last year and how many it has now. I look at its new buildings and wonder whether there will be more of them. Every vacant lot is a question.

The reason, I suppose, is that I have no instincts to guide me here. My ancestors did not live in such places, and they left me no inherited assurance about them. The city is unfamiliar, and what is unfamiliar must be understood before it can be enjoyed. So I cannot simply take it as it comes. I must reckon its progress and estimate its prospects. I must be told that all is going well.

The woods do not need my good opinion. The city, I fear, is waiting for it. And that is a heavy responsibility for a man who only came out for a walk.
