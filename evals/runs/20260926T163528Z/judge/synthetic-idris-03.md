# Judge packet synthetic-idris-03

## The passage (by the author)

What is a technical interview really for? The official answer is simple enough: an organisation needs to know whether a stranger can do the work, and the interview is the most efficient instrument it has found for finding out. Yet those of us who have sat on either side of the table know that the hour rarely feels like a measurement, and that both parties leave it with a curious sense of having taken part in something neither of them quite designed. The candidate performs competence for people who are, in some sense, performing judgement; the interviewers, for their part, perform a confidence in their own process that most of them privately doubt. It may be that the interview survives, despite a long and rather weary literature on its shortcomings, precisely because it does something other than predict who will do the work well. I suspect it is less an examination than a ritual of mutual performance, in which two groups of strangers agree to pretend, for an hour, that they already understand one another.

Consider the experience from the candidate's chair. You are asked to solve a problem you will almost certainly not meet in the job, on a whiteboard or a shared screen, while several people you have just met watch you think and take notes you cannot read. The skills the situation rewards—composure under observation, fluency in thinking aloud, the knack of seeming unhurried when you are not—are real skills, perhaps, but they are not obviously the ones the work will ask of you, which is mostly to sit quietly with a difficulty for a long time and then ask a colleague for help. Is it any wonder that so many capable engineers come away feeling that they have been assessed on their nerve rather than their judgement? The interviewers, meanwhile, are having an experience of their own; they have been pulled from their ordinary work, handed a set of questions they did not write and asked to reach a verdict about a person on the strength of a single, artificial afternoon.

## Draft A

The technical interview presents itself as a test of ability. A candidate is set a problem, asked to solve it under observation, and judged on the result, and the whole arrangement carries the air of an examination. But it seems to me that this description, though accurate as far as it goes, misses what the occasion actually is. Both parties are playing roles. The applicant performs the part of someone who is composed, capable and unhurried; the interviewer performs the part of someone who can tell, from a single conversation, whether that performance is genuine. Each knows the other is performing, and each proceeds as though the exercise were straightforwardly what it claims to be.

If the interview measured what it purports to measure, this would matter less. The difficulty is that it rewards a quality only loosely related to the work. What the format favours is calm under scrutiny: the capacity to think clearly while being watched, to speak fluently about a half-formed idea, to recover gracefully from a false start with a stranger taking notes. These are real abilities, and in some roles they are valuable. Yet the work itself mostly asks for something different. It asks for patience with problems that do not yield in an hour, for the willingness to read and reread, for collaboration with colleagues whose trust has been built over months rather than minutes. Someone may excel at all of this and still falter in the interview room, and the reverse is equally possible.

The interviewer's position is hardly easier. They must reach a consequential judgement on the basis of one contrived session, knowing that the conditions bear little resemblance to those in which the candidate would actually work. Whatever confidence they feel in the verdict is, I suspect, borrowed largely from the ritual itself.

Why, then, does the practice persist? Perhaps because it serves purposes other than the one it names: it offers a shared procedure, a way of making a difficult decision feel orderly, and a defence against the charge of arbitrariness. Those are not trivial needs. But we might at least be honest that they are the needs being met, rather than the one we claim.

## Draft B

A technical job interview is supposed to measure whether someone can do the work. That is the official story, and everyone involved repeats it. The candidate prepares to demonstrate ability; the interviewer prepares to assess it. Questions are chosen, scores are recorded, and a decision follows as though a measurement had been taken.

Look more closely and it resembles something else: a piece of mutual role-play. The candidate plays a version of themselves who is quick, confident and never stuck. The interviewer plays someone who can tell, within the space of a single conversation, whether a stranger will be a good colleague. Both know the roles are partly performance, and both play along, because the ritual persists for reasons that have little to do with how well it predicts anything. It is familiar, it feels fair, it gives a process something to point to, and it spares everyone the harder question of what a better method would look like.

What the format rewards is telling. Applicants do well when they stay calm under scrutiny, think out loud without faltering and produce an answer while someone watches and judges. Those are real skills, but they are not the ones most engineering jobs depend on. The daily work is slower and more collaborative: reading unfamiliar code, asking colleagues for context, trying something, being wrong, trying again, and taking the time to get it right. A person who excels at that patient, shared kind of work may freeze in a room where every hesitation is noted, while someone who performs well under pressure may be less good at the quiet, sustained effort that follows.

The interviewer's position is no easier. They must form a judgement from one contrived session, under artificial conditions, with problems that have been stripped of the context that real problems carry. Whatever they conclude, they conclude from a sample that is small and distorted by the setting itself.

None of this means interviews are useless. It means they measure something narrower than they claim, and that both sides would do well to remember which part is the test and which part is the play.

## Draft C

The technical interview presents itself as a test of ability, and both parties generally speak of it in those terms. The candidate is there to demonstrate what they can do; the interviewer is there to find out. It seems to me, however, that this description flatters the occasion. What actually takes place is something closer to a piece of mutual role-play, in which each side performs a version of itself that it believes the other expects to see, and both agree, more or less tacitly, not to mention how artificial the whole arrangement is. That the practice persists is not, I suspect, because anyone is convinced it measures what it claims to measure, but because it serves other purposes: it gives the process a recognisable shape, it allows decisions to be defended afterwards, and it spares everyone the harder question of what a better alternative would look like.

If we consider what the format actually rewards, it becomes clear that an applicant who remains calm under scrutiny, who can think aloud without faltering while a stranger watches and takes notes, will generally do well, and there is nothing wrong with that quality in itself. It is, however, rather different from what most of the work actually requires. The job, in most cases, is patient and collaborative: it involves returning to a problem over several days, asking colleagues for help, reading what others have written, and changing their minds without anyone keeping score. Very little of this can be observed in an hour, and some of it might even count against a candidate who displayed it too openly in the interview room.

The position of the interviewer is hardly easier, since they are asked to form a judgement about the likely conduct of another person over months or years from a single session that has been deliberately contrived, under conditions that neither of them would choose in ordinary working life. Perhaps the most honest thing to say is that interviewers do the best they can with evidence that is thin by design, and that the confidence with which their conclusions are later reported is not always matched by the evidence on which they rest.

## Draft D

What is a technical job interview actually for? Nominally it is a test of ability: the candidate is asked to solve problems, the interviewers observe how well they do so, and a decision follows from the evidence. Anyone who has sat on either side of the table will recognise that this description is somewhat generous. It may be that the interview is better understood as a piece of mutual role-play, in which the applicant performs the part of the confident engineer and the interviewers perform the part of impartial judges, and in which both sides know that the performance is only loosely connected to the work it claims to assess.

Why, then, does the practice persist? Partly, I suspect, because it serves purposes other than the one it announces. It gives an organisation a ritual through which a hiring decision can be made to look rigorous; it gives interviewers a sense of having exercised judgement; it gives applicants, perhaps, a recognisable set of hoops through which they can prepare to jump. None of these purposes is contemptible, but none of them is the measurement of ability.

The difficulty is that the format rewards a quality that the job rarely requires. What an applicant is really being assessed on is the capacity to remain calm under scrutiny, to think aloud fluently while a stranger watches and takes notes. The work itself asks for something rather different: patience with a problem that will not yield in an afternoon, a willingness to ask colleagues for help, the collaborative habit of improving an idea by letting others criticise it. A candidate who possesses these qualities in abundance may well appear hesitant in an interview room, while one who performs brilliantly there may find the slower, shared work of a team considerably less congenial.

The interviewers, meanwhile, are asked to judge from a single contrived session a person they have never seen work. It would be surprising if they managed this reliably. Perhaps the interview tells us less about whether someone can do the job than about whether they can pretend, convincingly and for as long as the session lasts, that the job resembles an interview.
