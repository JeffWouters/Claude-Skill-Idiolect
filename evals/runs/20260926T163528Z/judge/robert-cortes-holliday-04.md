# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-04

## The passage (by the author)

Across the way, at the corner of Eighth Street, the mellow white hotel maintains the distinguished name, and touches "the Avenue" with a very aromatic French flavor. Famous for its cuisine, largely patronized by the transient French population of the city, a habitual port of call of many painters and writers, the scene of the annual Illustrators' Ball, and so on.

I like within the frequent spectacle of gentlemen of magnificent bulk and huge black beards, in general effect impressively suggesting the probability of their all being Academicians. I like the fact (or the hypothesis) that all the waiters are Looeys and Sharses and Gastongs. I like the little marble-top tables with wire spindle legs. I like the lady patrons (Oh! immensely) who are frequently very chic (and with exquisite ankles). I like the young gentlemen customers, who (many of them) look exactly as though their faces were modelled in wax, and who wear the sort of delicate moustaches that are advertised in Vanity Fair.

But even more I like the quaintness of the scene without doors. There along the curb, you recall, stand (in summer beneath the pleasant greenery of drooping trees), awaiting hire, a succession of those delightful, open, low-swung, horse-drawn vehicles, victorias, which were the fashionable thing at the period named by Mrs. Wharton "The Age of Innocence." The romantically leisurely drivers of these unbelievably leisurely craft are perfectly turned out to be, so to say, in the picture. They affect coachmen's coats (piquantly tempered by age) with large silver buttons and, in mild weather, top hats constructed of straw, painted black. In some instances these coachmen are "colored"—which is a very pleasant thing, too, I think.

This hotel, naturally, has figured in a number of pieces of fiction. In Samuel Merwin's novel "The Trufflers" it is the Parisian, where Greenwich Village, when in funds, dines, lunches, breakfasts in the little rooms which you enter from the Avenue, directly under the wide front steps, or from the side street through the bar, and where Upper West Side, when seeking the quaintly foreign dissociated from squalor, goes up the steps into the airy eating rooms with full length hinged windows to dine. And where (in this book) the young lady whose blooming presence in the barber shop in the basement invites you to manicure attentions gives rise to some very dramatic occurrences. The place, this shop, of Marius (as called in the story), "the one barber in New York who does not ask 'Wet or dry.'"

## Draft A

There is a hotel on lower Fifth Avenue which is, in the manner of its living, French. It is not, you understand, in France; it is very much in New York. But once inside its doors you are, for the price of a dinner, somewhere else. And the dinner, it should be said at once, is the thing. The cooking is French cooking, the real article, not the kind which is French only on the bill of fare. People who know about these things speak of it with a certain lowering of the voice.

It is a place of artists. They come to it as to their own. You see them at the little tables, talking, gesturing, disputing, in no hurry whatever to be gone. A good many of the diners wear beards—not the neat, businesslike beard of the bank director, but the other kind, the kind that has opinions. The waiters are of a piece with the cooking. They glide; they bow; they murmur; they appear to regard your choice of a sauce as a matter of some moment, which, in that house, it is. And, among the beards and the paint-stained sleeves, sit the fashionable. Very fashionably gowned ladies, very correctly tailored gentlemen, who have come down from uptown to look at the artists, and who are themselves, to be sure, looked at in return. The whole room has an air, pleasant and a little foreign, which you feel before you can name it.

Outside, at the curb, stand the old horse carriages. You might think that the motor-car had never been invented. There they wait, the horses dozing, and on the boxes sit the drivers, costumed, in hats and coats of another day, patient as the horses. They wait for the fashionable, who will presently come out, well dined, and be driven away uptown in the manner of their grandparents. It is a picture, and I suspect that everybody concerned knows it.

The place has got into a book. A novel, [title needed], depicts it under another name, but nobody who has dined there could mistake it. There it all is: the cooking, the artists, the beards, the waiters, the ladies from uptown. And the novelist has not forgotten the barbershop in the basement. I confess I had not myself thought much about the barbershop in the basement. One goes to the hotel, after all, to dine, not to be shaved. But it is there, in the book, as it is there in fact, down below, going quietly about its business while the artists dispute and the fashionable dine overhead. It takes a novelist to go down into the basement.

## Draft B

On lower Fifth Avenue there is a hotel that has always seemed to me less a New York building than a piece of France set down by mistake on the wrong side of the ocean. [name needed] It keeps French hours, speaks a good deal of French, and above all cooks in the French manner. People who would never think of staying the night there come for dinner, and they come back.

The cooking is the first reason. It is careful, unhurried food, served by people who appear to believe that a meal is worth taking seriously. The dining room has long been a favourite of artists, and on any evening you will see a fair number of them at the tables, talking with their hands and arguing over their wine. There are more beards in that room than anywhere else I know in the city. Some belong to painters, some to men who would like to be taken for painters, and a few, I suspect, to men who simply find the beard suits the food.

The waiters deserve a paragraph to themselves. They move through the room with the air of men who have seen everything and approve of very little of it, and yet they are unfailingly attentive. [example needed] They seem to know which guests want to be left alone and which want to be recognised, and they manage both without appearing to notice either.

Among the artists sit the fashionable guests, who come partly for the cooking and partly, I think, to be seen in a place that has a reputation for being slightly bohemian. The two groups mix without much friction. Each seems to enjoy the other as part of the scenery.

Outside, the scene is older still. Along the kerb wait a row of old horse carriages, and their drivers are got up in costume, [details needed: the style of the costume], as if the hotel had hired them from another century to match its menu. In a city that is steadily giving itself over to the motor car, they look faintly unreal, and they are all the more charming for it.

The hotel has even found its way into fiction. A novel, [title and author needed], puts it into its pages under another name, and anyone who knows the place will recognise it at once. The book is thorough. It goes down past the dining room and the lobby into the basement, where there is a barbershop, and it describes that too. I have always taken this as the surest sign of the hotel's standing: a novelist who wanted to catch something of New York thought the place belonged in the book, right down to the barber's chair.

## Draft C

There is a hotel on lower Fifth Avenue [detail needed: its name] which has never quite made up its mind to be American. It is French. It is French in its cooking, which is excellent, and French in its waiters, who are deliberate, courteous and profoundly unimpressed by anybody. It is French, above all, in the air of the place, a pleasant, unhurried, slightly worn air, as of a house where good dinners have been eaten for a great many years and nobody has seen any reason to change the furniture. You do not go there to be seen. You go there, or you used to, to eat.

The company is mixed. At one table sit the artists, a shabby and animated group, arguing about something—always about something—over the remains of an omelette. At another there is a man with a beard. At a third there is another man with a beard. I do not know why the place should attract beards, but it does; a room of this kind seems to draw them as a lamp draws moths. And among these, perfectly at ease, you will see the fashionable, the very well-dressed women and their correct escorts, who have come down from uptown with the agreeable sense of doing something slightly daring. The waiters treat them all alike. That is to say, with a grave and courteous indifference.

Outside, at the curb, stand the old horse carriages. They are relics. The drivers wear a sort of costume—[detail needed: what the costume is]—which has been preserved, one gathers, on purpose for the pleasure of the visitor. They wait there patiently through the evening. Now and then somebody engages one. More often they are simply looked at, admired, and left standing, which is their real function; they are part of the scenery, like the lamp-posts of the avenue.

The hotel has, of course, got into a novel. [detail needed: the novel and its author] It appears there under another name, but nobody who knows the place could mistake it. The novelist has put in everything: the dining room, the waiters, the artists, the beards. He has even gone down into the basement, where there is a barbershop, and described that too. It is a thorough piece of work. Whether the barbers have read it, I cannot say.

## Draft D

There is a hotel on lower Fifth Avenue which is not, strictly speaking, in New York at all. It is in France. It shows, I believe, no disposition whatever to come home. You go in from the Avenue, and the Avenue is gone. The cooking is French—not French as the menus of our larger establishments are French, in the spelling only, but French in the sauce. I am no authority upon sauces. I merely know when I have met a good one, and I have met a number of them there.

The company is at least as interesting as the food. A great many of the diners are artists, or look as though they would like to be taken for artists, which in this neighbourhood amounts to much the same thing. A remarkable proportion of them wear beards. I do not mean the trim, apologetic beard of the Wall Street man who has been abroad; I mean the real article, full and unashamed, the kind of beard behind which a man may think in comfort. The waiters, too, are of the old school. They move among the tables with an air of having known your father, and of not having thought much of him. [example needed: a waiter's manner or remark] And among all these, at the better tables, sit the fashionable people, who have come down from uptown to look at the artists, precisely as the artists have come in to look at one another. Nobody, so far as I could see, was in the least displeased with the arrangement.

Outside, at the curb, there is a sight you will hardly find elsewhere in the city to-day. The old horse carriages wait there—cabs of a pattern one had supposed extinct—and on the boxes sit drivers in costume, with the patience of men who have outlived their century and do not intend to hurry about leaving it. I have never been sure whether they are waiting for anybody in particular. Perhaps they are simply waiting, as it were, for the past to come out after dinner.

The place has found its way into fiction. A novel describes it under another name [example needed: the novel and its name for the hotel], and describes it, I think, pretty faithfully. The novelist has not overlooked even the barbershop in the basement. That, it seems to me, is the true test of a writer's acquaintance with a hotel. Anybody can describe the dining-room. It takes a regular guest to know where one goes to be shaved.
