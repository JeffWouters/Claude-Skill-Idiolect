# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-01

## The passage (by the author)

But meanwhile let us—those of us who can—see to it that the pre-eminent brains of other ages shall not have passed away in vain. M. Anatole France, in La Révolte des Anges, has a good deal to say about the absurdity of a Jehovah who still believes in the Ptolemaïc system. Well, the Ptolemaïc system did not prevent the ancient world from giving us Greek theatres and Roman law, or England from giving us Magna Charta. We are still imitating Greek theatres (rather badly, I admit) in our stadia; Roman law is still, by and large, good enough for such an enlightened country as France; and Magna Charta—or its equivalent—had to be there before we could have a Declaration of Independence. Our superior scientific knowledge has not given us our standards of beauty or justice or liberty. Let us take what the present offers—airplanes and all. But let us not throw away what other men, in other ages, have died for the sake of discovering. If the lore of the past is useless, there is every chance—one must be very overweening indeed not to admit it—that the lore of our generation will be useless, too. Culture—whether you use the word itself or find another term—means only a decent economy of human experience. You cannot improve on things without keeping those things pretty steadily in mind. Otherwise you run the risk of wasting a lot of time doing something that has already been done. Any one, I think, will admit that. And it is not a far step to the realization that on the whole it is wise not to lose the past out of our minds. There is no glory in being wiser than the original savage; there is glory in being wiser than the original sage. But in order to be wiser than he, we must have a shrewd suspicion of how wise he was. By and large, without culture, that shrewd suspicion will never be ours.

## Draft A

It is easy, standing on the shoulders of modern science, to look back at earlier ages and see mostly error. Their astronomers had the heavens wrong, their physicians misread the body, and their natural philosophers explained the world with notions we now teach only as curiosities. From there it is a short step to the conclusion that what they thought is no longer worth our time. That conclusion is a mistake.

Consider what those same civilisations managed to build while holding their mistaken science. They produced art that still moves people who know nothing of the beliefs behind it. They worked out systems of law that we continue to draw on, and ideas of liberty that we still defend. None of this depended on getting the orbit of a planet right. Wisdom about how people should live together, what is beautiful, and what is just is not cancelled by a wrong theory of matter. A society can be deeply mistaken about nature and still see clearly about human beings.

This is what culture is for. Culture, properly understood, means keeping the accumulated experience of the past in view, so that each generation does not have to start from nothing. Progress that forgets where it came from tends to repeat itself: it spends its energy rediscovering what was already known, making old mistakes in new clothes, and congratulating itself on insights that earlier thinkers had and wrote down. Keeping the record open is not nostalgia. It is simply efficient.

There is a further point. We like to say that we have surpassed the thinkers of earlier ages, and in many fields we have. But surpassing someone requires knowing what they understood. You cannot go beyond a position you have never grasped; you can only ignore it, and ignoring is not the same as outgrowing. The person who dismisses the past without studying it has not moved past it at all. He has merely stopped looking, and is more likely than anyone to walk back into the very ground he believes he left behind.

## Draft B

It is the fashion to-day to speak of the older ages as if they had merely been wrong. They were wrong, of course, about a great many things; their notions of nature were, many of them, quite absurd, and [example needed: a mistaken belief of an earlier age] is only one of a long list. But I do not know that being wrong about the stars ever kept a people from building well. The men who were so mistaken about the heavens made an art we cannot equal, framed laws we still live under, and won, at great cost, a liberty we have grown careless of. Their science has gone the way of all science; their art and their law remain. One may smile at their physics, if one likes. It is harder to smile at their works.

What, then, is culture? I take it to be nothing more mysterious than this: the habit of keeping in view what men have already learned, so that we need not learn it all over again. Progress is not a matter of throwing things away. The man who knows nothing of what went before him is not free of the past; he is simply condemned to repeat it, and generally to repeat it badly. He discovers, with a great flourish, some truth that was old when [example needed: an ancient thinker] was young, and he calls it new. We have all met him (he is very common just now!) and we have all been a little tired by him. Every generation that forgets its inheritance must spend its strength in doing over the work its fathers had done—and it has so much less strength to spare for the work that is really its own.

Nor is it enough to say that the moderns have surpassed the ancients. Perhaps they have, in some things; but one cannot surpass what one has never measured. To go beyond a thinker, you must first know how far he went. The young man who tells me he has outgrown [example needed: an earlier thinker] has usually never read him; and that is not outgrowing, it is only missing. We shall go further than our forebears only by standing where they stood.

## Draft C

It is the fashion, just now, to look back on the earlier ages with a sort of indulgent pity. They were wrong about nearly everything a schoolboy now knows; and we are right. Granted. But it is a curious thing that those same benighted people made art we cannot equal, framed laws we still live under, and won for us a liberty we have done very little to enlarge. Their science was wrong; their art, their justice and their freedom were not. One does not need a correct theory of nature to know what a just judge is, or what a free man owes his city. The two kinds of knowledge, in short, are not the same kind, and the second does not go out of date, as the first does, with each new discovery.

What, then, is culture? It is not—as one sometimes hears—a polite acquaintance with old books for the sake of seeming well read. It is simply the habit of keeping the whole of our accumulated experience in view, so that we need not learn every lesson over again, at our own expense and with a great deal of unnecessary pain. A people without it is like a man without a memory; he may be very clever, but he will spend half his life rediscovering what his grandfather could have told him. Progress that forgets the past is not progress at all; it is merely going round in a circle rather fast. I fancy that a good deal of what passes for novelty to-day is of this sort. The point is that no one can surpass the earlier thinkers without first knowing what they understood. Otherwise, being ignorant of the ground they covered, we shall merely walk it again and call it new country. You cannot climb past a man on the ladder unless you have found the ladder. And the ladder, one must admit, was built by them.

## Draft D

Nobody, I suppose, will pretend that the ancients knew as much as we do about the heavens, or the body, or the causes of disease. They were wrong about a great many things; some of them were wrong about nearly everything that can be weighed and measured. And yet they built. They made works of art we still stand before in silence; they framed laws that our own courts have not wholly improved upon; they worked out, at no small cost, the notion that a man may be free. It is a curious thing, when one comes to think of it, that people who had the whole order of nature upside down should have left us so much that is still standing upright.

I do not say this to belittle the laboratory. Heaven forbid! The laboratory has done admirably, and I am as grateful as any one for its gifts. But I confess I grow a little weary of the eager young person (it is nearly always a young person!) who assures me that nothing written before the age of science is worth an evening. He has mistaken one kind of knowledge for the whole of it.

For what, after all, is culture? It is not a polite acquaintance with the classics. It is simply the habit of keeping in view what the race has already learned—slowly, and often painfully—so that we need not learn it all over again. A society that forgets its own experience is like a man who burns his account-books every New Year: he may feel very free and very modern, but he will certainly be cheated. We have not so much time, any of us, that we can afford to rediscover, generation after generation, what our grandfathers could have told us.

And there is another point, which the ardent progressive seldom stops to consider. If we mean to go beyond the old thinkers—and I hope we do—we must first find out where they stood. One cannot very well pass a man on the road without knowing which road he took. To surpass is, before anything else, to understand; the rest is merely running about.
