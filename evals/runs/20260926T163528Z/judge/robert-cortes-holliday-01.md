# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-01

## The passage (by the author)

Of course, some may complain more or less at the place about the "service." Or swank round outside about the address, saying carelessly: "Oh! yes: at the Blackstone, you know." Or again, if it's a rather inexpensive place, remark to friends: "Isn't it a funny hole! But the cuisine is excellent. You'd be surprised! That's why I stop there. And then it's much more homey, too, than those garish places."

Now I myself am a fan for hotels.

If I was a rich man I'd do like an aristocratic and restless young man I know, who used to go to one New York hotel about twelve at night (after the evening's entertainment) and leave a call for ten in the morning, when he would get up and drive to another hotel, check in, eat lunch and dinner there, and move on to a third New York hotel that night. A cheerful way he had of adding variety to his life.

He was a highly agreeable youth, this chap. Always "wore" a silver-headed cane. I'm sorry to have to say that he is now in jail. Yep! You see, he had many attractive qualities, but dependability was not a feature of his equipment. However, his is a resilient nature, and, fortunately, he is an epicure by temperament. I was rather distressed, myself, when I heard that he was in jail; and other of his friends that I met also were decidedly disturbed about him. One day one of them got a letter from him (it was in France, you know, that he was then in jail), a bubbling, delightful letter (just like the youth), in which he declared with much gusto that the jail he was in had the best menu of any jail in France.

But about hotels. Oh, yes!

## Draft A

I like hotels. I like them more, I suspect, than most of the people who inhabit them. Listen to the conversation of any lobby and you will hear one of two things. Either the guest is complaining of the service, the deliberation of the elevator, the indifference of the clerk, the coffee that arrived cold; or he is boasting of his hotel as though he had personally constructed it. It is the finest in town. The beds are the most comfortable in the country. Nobody of any discernment stops anywhere else. I have never been able to do either. A hotel seems to me an agreeable, somewhat theatrical arrangement, where one is attended by strangers and asked no questions, and I am grateful for it without feeling any obligation to defend it.

I once knew a young man, extremely wealthy, who carried this appreciation much further than I have ever ventured. He was not attached to any particular hotel. He was devoted to hotels in general, and he could not endure being bored by one of them. So he moved every day. He would breakfast in one, lunch somewhere about the city, and by evening his trunks would be following him into another lobby, with another clerk, another prospect of another street. Variety, he explained, was the entire point. Why should a man with the means of living everywhere be content to live in one place? I did not have an answer for him; I doubt there is one.

Some years afterward I heard that he was in prison in France. [detail needed: how he came to be there] It was, naturally, a considerable shock to his friends. But the letters he wrote them were not what anybody had expected. He did not complain of his cell. He wrote, instead, in warm and particular praise of the food. He had discovered, at last, an establishment that satisfied him—and one he couldn't exchange every morning.

## Draft B

I like hotels. I like them, I suppose, a good deal better than most of the people who inhabit them. Listen to the guests in any lobby of an evening. Half of them are grumbling about the service; the soup was cold, the bell-boy was dilatory, the clerk was insolent, the chambermaid was nowhere to be discovered. The other half are boasting about their hotel, which is incomparably the finest in the city, and which they appear to have constructed with their own hands. Neither party, so far as I can observe, is having a particularly agreeable time. It seems to me a pity. A hotel is a delightful institution, if you will only let it alone and permit it to entertain you.

I once knew a young man, very wealthy, who carried this principle rather further than anybody else I have heard of. He had discovered that he enjoyed hotels—and he did not see why he should confine himself to one. So he changed his hotel every day. He inhabited, as it were, the whole city at once. He'd pack in the morning, and by evening he would be unpacking somewhere else, among unfamiliar carpets and strange waiters, with a different view from a different window, and with the pleasant sensation, which I imagine never quite deserted him, of a traveller who has arrived without the tiresome necessity of having gone anywhere. It was variety he wanted, and he got it. I don't know that I ever met a more contented man. Some time afterwards he was put in jail in France. Here, you would say, his experiment came to an end. Not at all. He wrote to his friends, and what he wrote about was the food. The prison food, he informed them, was excellent. I have never been able to decide whether this was the soundest criticism of hotels ever delivered, or merely the final triumph of a thoroughly good-natured traveller.

## Draft C

I like hotels. I like them very much, and I do not know that I have ever heard anybody say so before. It is the custom, you will have observed, to speak of a hotel in one of two ways. Either the guest grumbles, or he boasts. He grumbles about the service. The bell-boy was slow; the soup was cold; the man at the desk had a manner. Or else he boasts about his hotel, as if he had built it himself, and laid the carpets, and personally trained the head waiter. Now, I do neither. I simply enjoy the thing, which seems to be regarded as not quite the proper attitude. A hotel is a place where you are nobody in particular, and where nobody in particular expects anything of you, and I find that restful.

I had an acquaintance once who carried this taste a good deal further than I. He was a young man, and he was rich, very rich, rich in that easy, careless, unconcerned way which belongs only to the young. He lived in hotels. But he did not live in a hotel. That, to him, would have been monotonous. Every day he moved to a different hotel in the city. Monday here, Tuesday there, Wednesday somewhere else. He liked, he said, a little variety. One could, he explained, get into a rut. Such was his theory of life, and I do not say that it was a bad one.

Afterward, as sometimes happens to rich young men who dislike ruts, he found himself in France, and in France he found himself in jail. Just how he got there does not concern us. What concerns us is that he wrote home to his friends, and what he wrote was in praise of the food. The prison fare, he said, was excellent. He could recommend it. There speaks, I think, the true connoisseur of hotels.

## Draft D

I have always liked hotels, and I have never quite understood the people who stay in them only to complain. Sit in any lobby for an hour and you will hear the two kinds of guest. The first kind grumbles. The coffee came late, the porter was slow, the towels were thin, the man at the desk did not remember his name. The second kind boasts. His hotel is the best in town, his room has the best view, the head waiter knows him and keeps a table for him by the window. Neither of them, as far as I can tell, is enjoying himself.

My own pleasure in a hotel is simpler. I like the anonymity of it, the clean room that asks nothing of you, the sense that for a night or two the world has agreed to look after you and not to ask questions.

I once knew a rich young man who took this pleasure further than anyone I have met. He lived in [city needed], and he did not keep a house there. Instead he moved from one hotel to another, a new one every day. When I asked him why, he said he liked the variety. He never had to grow tired of a wallpaper or a waiter. Each morning his bags went ahead of him, and each evening he signed a new register and learned the way to a new dining room.

Some years later he ended up in prison in France. I never learned the whole story, and I will not guess at it here; [details needed]. What I do know is that he wrote to his friends from his cell, and that his letters were full of praise for the prison food. I have thought about that often. A man who had tried every hotel in the city had at last found one he could not leave, and he was still, it seems, judging the kitchen.
