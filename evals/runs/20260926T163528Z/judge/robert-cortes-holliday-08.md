# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-08

## The passage (by the author)

That word, "undertaker," has long, long ago been discarded by the élite of the profession. What a queer word as a business title it was, anyway! How did it originally ever come to be used in its mortuary relation? No one in the business that I have asked has been able to tell me. And why in the dim past when names were being given to trades did not this word, undertaker, seem to be equally descriptive of the career of physician or attorney? Indeed, does not he that sets himself the highly hazardous task of saving a living fellow being from disease or the gallows undertake to do more than he who merely performs the quiet office of laying us away?

And then, oddly enough for its tragic associations, the word acquired in our minds something of a ludicrous turn. It was reminiscent of Dickens, of hired professional "mourners," and that sort of thing. At mention of the word, a picture popped into our mind of a grotesquely angular being, of sallow, elongated features and lugubrious manner, garbed in a rusty frock coat and "stove pipe" hat, who put together before him the tips of black-gloved fingers and spoke with a hollow sound. We would say to our friends when they were feeling blue: "What's the matter with you? You act like an undertaker."

Well, as doubtless you have noticed, the term "funeral director" more or less recently pretty well superseded the word undertaker among progressive concerns. It is a phrase much more in the modern spirit, like "domestic science" for (what used to be) "household work," "modiste" for "dressmaker," "maid" for "hired-girl," "psychic" for "fortune teller," "publicity engineer" for "press agent," and so on. And it has a good, business-like, efficient sound.

Still (I discovered) to be buried by a funeral director is not the very latest, the most fashionable thing. The really smart way nowadays of bidding good-bye to the world is to go to the establishment of a "mortician."

## Draft A

Consider the undertaker. I have lately given some thought to this word, and the more I think about it the less I understand it. Why the undertaker should have been the one to undertake is not at all clear. Everybody undertakes something. The doctor undertakes to cure you, and the lawyer undertakes to get you off, and either of them, it would seem, had quite as good a claim to the title. But somehow, by what accident of history I do not know, the word came to rest upon the man who undertakes to bury you, and there it stayed.

And having stayed, it took on a character. The word grew long in the face. It put on black gloves and a tall hat. It came to mean, in the popular mind, a thin, pale, solemn person, rubbing his hands and speaking in a low voice, a figure out of a comic paper, at once gloomy and faintly ridiculous. [example needed] You could not say "undertaker" in company without somebody's smiling, and you could not say it in a sickroom without somebody's shuddering. This is a hard thing for a man in trade.

So the word was retired. The undertaker became a funeral director. He was not the first man to be promoted in this manner, nor will he be the last. [example needed] In the same way, I imagine, a great many callings have gone up in the world by the simple process of going up in the dictionary. A funeral director directs. There is authority in it, and efficiency, and nothing whatever of the tall hat.

But fashion does not stand still, even in this sober business. Funeral director, it appears, is already a little behind the times. The word now is mortician. Mortician has a learned sound, rather like physician, and it is, so they tell me, very much the thing. So the old undertaker is gone. You are not buried; you are, I suppose, morticed.

## Draft B

Nobody seems quite sure how the undertaker came to be the man who buries us. The word itself promises nothing of the kind. To undertake is simply to take something on, and a great many people take things on for a living. A doctor undertakes to cure you; a lawyer undertakes your case. Either of them could have been called an undertaker with as much reason, and perhaps more. Yet somewhere along the line the word settled on the trade that deals with the dead, and there it stayed. Once it had settled, it began to collect associations, and very few of them were flattering. The undertaker became a stock figure, long faced and dressed in black, rubbing his hands and measuring the company with his eye. He was gloomy, and because he was gloomy he was funny. Writers and comedians found him irresistible. The word could hardly be said without a certain smile, and a trade that asks people to trust it at the worst moment of their lives cannot afford to be smiled at.

So the trade did what many trades have done, and gave itself a better name. The undertaker became the funeral director. It is the same move that turned other ordinary occupations into something that sounds grander on a business card [example needed: other upgraded job titles]. The new title does its job. It is calm, it is respectable, and it suggests someone who organizes and manages rather than someone who carries and digs. Nobody has yet found a way to make it comic.

Even that, it seems, is not the end of it. "Mortician" is now the fashionable word, with its faint ring of the laboratory and the profession, as though the work were a branch of medicine. Whether it will last, or whether it too will gather its own jokes and have to be replaced, remains to be seen. The work itself has not changed much. Only the name keeps moving, a step ahead of the laughter.

## Draft C

Why the man who buries us should ever have been called an undertaker is a question to which I have never found a satisfactory answer. An undertaker, by the plain sense of the word, is one who undertakes something. Everybody undertakes something. The physician undertakes to cure you, the lawyer undertakes to keep you out of gaol, or at any rate to get you out again; either of them has as good a claim to the title as the gentleman in the black gloves. Yet the word went, by some obscure process of language, to the last of these, and there it stuck. [example needed: when or how the word took on this meaning]

Having stuck, it gathered about itself a curious set of associations. The undertaker became a figure of fun, and a gloomy figure of fun at that. He was the long face at the foot of the stairs, the man with the professional sigh, the stock character of comic sketches. Nobody, I imagine, was particularly anxious to be one at dinner.

The trade then did what trades will do when their name has grown shabby. It got a new one. The undertaker became the funeral director, very much as other callings have promoted themselves with a grander title. [example needed: other upgraded job titles the writer has in mind] "Funeral director" has a pleasant, executive sound; it suggests a desk, a telephone, an office. It suggests that somebody is in charge, which on such occasions is a comfort.

Already, however, the funeral director is perhaps a little out of date. The fashionable word now is mortician. It has a rather scientific air, and I suppose that is the point of it. Whether it will last is another matter. Words of this kind wear out quickly; the fresh paint soon has the old associations showing through it. And I should not be surprised if, in a generation or so, somebody in the trade proposes, as a bold and refreshing novelty, that we go back to calling it undertaking.

## Draft D

It has never been altogether clear to me why the word "undertaker" should have come to mean what it does. An undertaker, one would suppose, is simply a person who undertakes something, and nearly everybody undertakes something or other in the course of a working day. The doctor undertakes to cure you; the lawyer undertakes to get you out of your difficulty, or (as the case may be) into a larger one. Either of them might, with perfect propriety, have claimed the title. Neither did. By some obscure process of which the dictionaries give no satisfactory account, the word settled upon the gentleman who conducts burials, and there it has stayed. [example needed: the history of the word]

Having settled there, it began to gather associations, and not agreeable ones. The undertaker became a stock figure of comedy, long of face and black of coat, rubbing his hands in a manner not quite suited to the occasion. At the same time he became a figure of gloom. The very word, pronounced, was apt to cast a chill over a dinner table. It was, so to speak, both a joke and a shudder, and a man cannot conduct a dignified business under a name which makes people either laugh or shiver.

So the name was changed. The undertaker became a "funeral director," which sounds considerably more substantial, and rather suggests a board meeting. In this he was only following a general movement. All about us, occupations have been quietly promoted in title without any noticeable alteration in duties; [example needed: an upgraded job title] and the funeral trade, perceiving which way the wind blew, did not intend to be left behind. Now, I understand, even "funeral director" is losing its lustre. The fashionable word is "mortician." It has a pleasantly scientific ring, like physician or electrician, and it contains nothing whatever that anybody could laugh at. Whether it will keep its dignity longer than its predecessors did remains to be seen.
