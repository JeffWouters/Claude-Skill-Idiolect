# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-02

## The passage (by the author)

I had the flu bad enough, when that was the big thing going; but somehow so far I myself have escaped being caught in the Crime Wave. This gives me the great advantage over most people of being a detached spectator of the rollicking game.

I have a friend, though, who was caught up just a few days ago. He has been telling me all about it. Murder case.

This fellow is a sort of author. He had served a time or two as a juror in the Supreme Court of New York County. In that building down by the City Hall. But he says those cases bored him terribly. They were chicken-feed sort of rows, generally concerned with the question of how many dollars and fractions thereof X had occasioned the loss of to Z by reason of his failure to deliver such and such a quantity of (say) beeswax before the drop in the market of 39.7-1/4 cents, as called for by telephone agreement, possibly. The "Court" (a nice, pink and grey old fellow) would go to sleep, with his mouth open, during the drone of the legal argument, and be awakened automatically (apparently by some change in atmospheric conditions) at the moment required for him to begin his charge to the jury. Occasionally, he would come semi-to for an instant before this, and indistinctly utter the words, "Objection sustained."

My friend's chief impression of these proceedings is his recollection of one phenomenon which he observed. Not long after the opening of the presentation of X's side of the case he saw very clearly that Z hadn't a leg to stand on. It was ridiculous that he had the face to come into court with an attempt to question the truth of facts which were as apparent to the naked eye as the Woolworth Building. My friend felt it needless to pay any further attention to the foolish formalities of the argument. If he had not had an uneasy feeling that he might get pinched for this, he would have gone to sleep, like the Judge.

## Draft A

A friend of mine is at present engaged in the administration of justice. He is a writer by trade, and a writer, one would suppose, is a man with nothing much to do; so they have got him. He sits on a murder jury. He tells me that he is "very well, thank you," and that the case is "extremely interesting," and I gather from the general tone of the thing that he is enjoying himself more than he would care to admit.

Meanwhile, I am out of it. The city, I read, is in the midst of what the newspapers call a crime wave. Every morning there is something fresh. [example needed], and so on, one morning after another. I read about it all with the calm of one who is well away from it. I have escaped. There is a great deal to be said for escaping.

It was not always murder with my friend. His earlier experience of the law, he tells me, was in the civil courts, and there it was all money. So-and-so owed so-and-so a sum; so-and-so said he did not. A contract had been broken, or it had not been broken, according to which side you were listening to. Hour after hour, day after day, the thing went on. Dull? He says that dull is not the word for it. He says there is no word for it.

The judge, it appears, felt much the same. He dozed. Not all the time, you understand; but now and then, in the warm part of the afternoon, his head would sink gently forward, and the court would go on without him, as courts, I suppose, are well able to do. My friend observed this with a good deal of sympathy. And he confesses to me that he had himself made up his mind about the verdict quite early in the proceedings, and that after that he did not trouble to listen any further. It saved, he says, a great deal of wear and tear. I do not know that this is how justice is supposed to work. But I do not know that it is not, either.

## Draft B

A friend of mine is on a jury. He writes for a living, and I suspect he regards the whole business as material, though he is too polite to say so to the court. This time it is a murder trial, which is a step up for him. The last time he was called, he sat through civil cases, and he tells me they were the dullest days of his life. Every one of them was about money: who owed it, who had been promised it, who had signed a paper and then forgotten what the paper said. He listened to lawyers argue for hours over sums that meant nothing to him and a great deal to the people in the room, and he came home each evening too bored even to complain properly.

A murder is different, or ought to be. There is a life at stake on one side and a death already on the other. And yet, from what he tells me, the atmosphere in the courtroom is not so very different from the money cases. The judge dozes. Not all the time, my friend is careful to say, but often enough that the jurors have noticed, and one or two of them have begun to watch the bench rather than the witness box, waiting to see whether his head will drop.

My friend confesses that he made up his mind early. Somewhere in the first days of testimony he decided what the verdict should be, and once he had decided it, he stopped listening. He still sits in his chair and looks attentive. He still nods at the right moments. But the case, for him, is over, and he is only waiting for the others to catch up. ## Draft C

I have been out of town a good deal of late, and I cannot say that I regret it. The newspapers tell me that the city is in the midst of an extraordinary wave of crime. [example needed: a particular crime reported] Holdups, it appears, are as common as traffic signals, and a respectable citizen hardly ventures to the corner for a paper without first considering his will. I read these accounts at a comfortable distance, with the particular satisfaction of a man who has left a burning building by the side door.

A friend of mine has not been so fortunate. He is a writer, a quiet and industrious person with no special interest in the machinery of the law. The law, however, has taken an interest in him. He is now sitting on a jury in a murder trial. He has served before, on civil cases, and he described them to me at the time with a kind of weary disgust, as though he had been made to sit through the same tedious play. They were all about money: who owed whom, and how much, and whether a certain agreement had or had not been, in the eyes of the law, an agreement at all. He said that he had never in his life been so thoroughly bored. He had expected, I think, something of the drama of the courtroom as the novelists present it, and he got instead an interminable argument over a bill.

A murder, you would suppose, would be different. And so, in a manner, it has been. He tells me that the judge dozes. Not continually, you understand, but with a certain comfortable regularity, in the warm and heavy part of the afternoon, when the windows are shut and the evidence is technical, his chin descending by degrees toward his robe. Nobody seems to mind. The lawyers proceed; the witnesses proceed; the judge wakes, and looks about him with dignity, and the trial goes on. My friend, for his part, made up his mind early. He will not say exactly when, but it was well before the testimony was finished. Having decided the verdict to his own satisfaction, he stopped listening. He sits in the box now as composedly as the judge, and rather more awake, thinking, I imagine, about his next article.

## Draft D

The newspapers, which pursue me even here, inform me that the city is in the midst of what they are pleased to describe as a crime wave. I read of it with the placid satisfaction of a man who has got out of the rain. I am, for the present, beyond the reach of the burglar and the gunman; the worst that threatens me is tedium. But a friend of mine has not been so fortunate. He has not been robbed, nor shot at, nor anything of that character. He has been summoned for a jury. And it is, if you please, a murder jury.

My friend is a writer, and a writer, I think, ought to regard such an appointment as a professional windfall. He did not always regard it so. He has served before, on civil cases, and he assures me that these were the dullest hours of an existence not otherwise conspicuous for excitement. One man declared that another man owed him money; the other man declared that he didn't. Documents were produced. Accountants were examined. Attorneys argued, with a solemnity worthy of a nobler cause, about sums which neither side, so far as my friend could discover, particularly needed. He came home, he says, feeling somewhat as though he had been read to from a ledger.

A murder, one would suppose, is another matter. Here, surely, is life and death, and the study of human nature in its extremity. Perhaps it is. But my friend's account of the proceedings is a little disconcerting. The judge, it appears, dozed. Not continuously—he would rouse himself occasionally, with the expression of a man who has been attending all along—but with a certain regularity which my friend came to admire. And my friend himself, I regret to say, determined the verdict rather early in the trial. Having determined it, he saw no particular reason to go on attending to the testimony, and so he stopped. I do not know that I blame him. I merely observe that justice, as it is administered to-day, appears to be conducted by a considerable number of people who are not listening, and that I am exceedingly comfortable where I am.
