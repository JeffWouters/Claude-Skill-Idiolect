# Judge packet synthetic-noor-06

## The passage (by the author)

Last Tuesday it came at about two. I woke at six to the quiet. Snow makes the street go still. No tyres, no bus. Just the tick of the boiler and a blackbird somewhere in the hedge.

The shovel lives in the shed with the handle up. It is a wide aluminium one, 50 centimetres across the blade. I bought it at a car boot sale for eight euros. Before that I used my grandfather's coal shovel. It is narrow and heavy and good for nothing but coal.

Here is the method.

First, boots and two pairs of socks. Then gloves with a grip on the palm. Wool gloves are wet in ten minutes. Your hands go stiff before the path is half done.

I start at the step and not at the gate. That way I am always standing on cleared ground. If you start at the gate you tread the snow flat on the way down. Flat snow turns to ice by evening.

So then the first cut. I push the blade straight down the middle, one width, all the way to the gate. That opens a line. Then I work outwards from it, left side first, then right. Push, lift, turn, throw. Push, lift, turn, throw.

You throw to the side the wind is going. Here that is east, mostly. I learned this the first winter in the house. I threw everything onto the west border. The wind brought half of it back across the path by lunch.

I never lift a full blade. A full blade of wet snow weighs about seven kilos. I weighed it once on the kitchen scales, a bucket at a time. Half a blade is enough. Your back knows the difference in the morning.

The last centimetre is the part that matters. The shovel leaves a thin skin that packs down under feet. So I go over the whole path again with the stiff yard broom. Then grit, a handful every metre or so, from the tin by the back door. The tin says SALT on the lid in my father's hand. It has held grit for years.

Left side, then right side. Short strokes, pushing the snow to the edge.

Throw downwind. Check the wind before the first throw, not after. Otherwise it comes straight back at you, in your face and down your collar and back onto the bit you just cleared.

And do not fill the shovel. This is the big one. Half a load, sometimes less. Snow is heavier than it looks, and wet snow is heavier still. A full shovel lifted and twisted is how backs go. Half loads take longer. They also mean I can stand up straight the next day.

When the path is clear I sweep it. A stiff broom, down to the paving. The thin layer the shovel leaves is the layer that freezes. Then the grit. A thin scatter, edge to edge, and a bit extra on the step. Then back inside.

There is a particular silence after snow falls overnight. The street is muffled, the cars are still, and the first sound of the morning is often the scrape of a shovel on someone's path. This morning it was mine.

The shovel I use is [shovel type needed]. There is an older alternative in the shed, inherited from [whom needed], which [description needed]. It still works, but for most snowfalls I reach for the newer one.

Start at the house end. It is tempting to begin at the gate, but clearing outward from the door means you are never trudging back over snow you have already packed down, and the most used stretch is done first if you are interrupted. Cut a single line down the centre of the path first, one shovel wide. That gives you a firm footing and a clear edge to work from. Then work outward from that line towards each side, pushing the snow away from the middle.

When you throw snow, throw it downwind. Throwing into the wind just brings it back into your face and onto the path you have cleared. Pay attention to where it lands, too: not onto the neighbour's path, and not in a heap that will melt and refreeze across your own.

Take partial loads. A full shovel of wet snow is far heavier than it looks, and it is the lifting and twisting that hurts the back. Half a blade at a time, bending the knees and turning the whole body rather than the waist, is slower in the moment and much kinder the next day.

When the bulk is gone, sweep the path with a stiff broom to take off the thin layer the shovel leaves behind. That last film is what turns to ice overnight. Finally, spread grit along the cleared path, a little more on any slope or step. Then go inside, and listen to the quiet again.
