## The passage (by the author)

There is also the matter of power, which the format tends to acknowledge only obliquely. However kind the manager may be, and however informal the setting, the person across the table has some say over the report's pay, reputation and future, and both of them know it. The standard advice—that managers should listen more than they speak, that the time belongs to the report, that the agenda should be theirs to set—is correct as far as it goes, and I do not wish to dismiss it; it has made a great many of these meetings considerably more humane than they once were. The difficulty is that no rule about who speaks first can dissolve an asymmetry that exists precisely because of the relationship itself. Arguably the most honest one-to-ones are those in which the imbalance is named rather than concealed, in which the manager admits, in some sense, that they are asking for candour from someone who has good reason to withhold it.

What might it mean to approach the one-to-one differently? It seems to me that the most valuable thing a manager can bring to it is not a better template but a willingness to be surprised, to treat the conversation as a place where their own assumptions about the work, the team and the person in front of them might be quietly corrected. This might mean, perhaps, tolerating silences that feel faintly awkward, or returning to a remark made weeks earlier to show that it was genuinely heard, or admitting uncertainty about a decision rather than defending it. None of this requires new tools or an elaborate process; it requires patience, and a certain readiness to hear things that are uncomfortable to hear. It is also slower, since trust of this kind accumulates only through many small occasions on which something risky was said and nothing bad followed, and there is no way to hurry it.

## Draft A

What is a one-on-one meeting actually for, when one of the two people in the room decides the other's pay, prospects and daily work? The usual advice is that the manager should let the report lead, setting the agenda, choosing the topics and doing most of the talking, and that advice is sound as far as it goes, since a meeting conducted this way is considerably better than one in which the manager simply works through a list of status questions. It cannot, however, remove the imbalance built into the relationship. The report knows who writes the review, and no amount of attentive listening will make them forget it.

It may be that the more honest course is to name the gap openly rather than pretend that a change of format has dissolved it. A manager who acknowledges plainly that the difference in authority exists, and that it probably makes certain things harder to say, has at least given the report permission to notice it too. This is not a confession so much as a description of the conditions under which both people are speaking; it treats the report as someone perfectly capable of understanding their own situation.

It seems to me that the rest follows from how the manager behaves across many meetings rather than from anything said in one. They should welcome being corrected, and show that they mean it, since a correction received with visible irritation is unlikely to be offered twice. They should tolerate pauses instead of hurrying to fill them, because the thing a report most needs to say frequently arrives only after a silence. They might return to something mentioned weeks earlier, which demonstrates that it was heard; and they might admit doubt about their own decisions, which is the clearest signal available that disagreement is safe. None of this is quick. Trust in this relationship grows slowly, I suspect, and only when the manager continues to behave in the same way long after the gesture has stopped feeling novel to either of them.

## Draft B

Much of the advice on one-on-one meetings urges the manager to let the report set the agenda, and there is real wisdom in it. A conversation that begins with the report's concerns is more likely to reach them than one that begins with the manager's. Yet the advice, however sincerely followed, cannot dissolve the imbalance on which the meeting rests. One person in the room will write the other's review, influence their pay and shape their prospects; the other knows this, and knows that the first knows it too. Handing over the agenda does not change who holds the pen. It may even obscure the matter, by suggesting an equality that neither party quite believes in.

I think it is better, on the whole, to name the imbalance than to pretend it away. To say plainly that the relationship is unequal, and that this makes certain things harder to say, is not an admission of failure but an acknowledgement of the conditions under which honesty has to be earned. Having said it, the manager can then do the slower work of making the imbalance matter less. That means welcoming correction rather than merely permitting it, so that a report who points out an error is thanked rather than tolerated; it means allowing pauses to run their course, since the thing that most needs saying often arrives only after an uncomfortable silence; it means returning to remarks made in earlier meetings, which shows that they were heard and that they counted; and it means being willing to admit doubt, to say that one does not know, or has not decided, or may have been wrong.

None of these gestures will produce trust on its own, and certainly not quickly. Trust in this setting accumulates in small deposits over many conversations, and a single careless response can withdraw a good deal of it. But the alternative, a meeting in which both people perform an openness that neither feels, seems to me a poor substitute. Better, surely, to begin from the truth about the relationship and build from there.

## Draft C

Most advice about one-on-one meetings says the same thing: let the report set the agenda. It is good advice. When the other person decides what gets discussed, the meeting stops being a status check dressed up as a conversation, and the manager learns what actually occupies the report's mind rather than what the manager assumed it would be.

But handing over the agenda does not hand over the power. The manager still writes the review, still shapes the next assignment, still has a say in pay and promotion. The report knows this, and so every sentence they choose to say is filtered through it. A report can lead the meeting and still be careful, still leave out the doubt or the complaint that would be most useful to hear. The imbalance is built into the relationship, and no seating arrangement or agenda template removes it.

The better move is to stop pretending it isn't there. A manager who says plainly that the difference in position exists, that it makes honesty harder, and that they would rather hear the awkward thing than not, has at least put the problem on the table. Naming it does not dissolve it, but it gives both people permission to talk about it instead of working around it in silence.

The rest is behaviour, and it has to be consistent. When a report corrects you, welcome it, and let them see that nothing bad followed. When a question produces a long pause, sit in it rather than filling it with your own answer. Come back to something they said weeks ago, so they know it was heard and not just noted. And admit when you are unsure, because a manager who never doubts gives nobody else room to doubt either.

None of this works quickly. Trust in a one-on-one builds slowly, one meeting at a time, and it is spent much faster than it is earned. The gap will always be there; what changes is whether both people can see it.

## Draft D

Most advice about one-on-one meetings tells the manager to step back and let the report set the agenda. It is good advice, as far as it goes, and I would not want to discourage anyone from following it, yet it rests on an assumption that seems to me rather optimistic: that the imbalance between the two people in the room can be dissolved by a change of procedure. It cannot be, because one of them writes the other's review, shapes their assignments and has some say in their future; the other knows this, and no arrangement of the agenda will make them forget it. Letting the report lead helps, but it helps in the way that good manners help at a formal dinner, easing the occasion without changing what kind of occasion it is.

What might work better is simply to name the gap. A manager who says openly that the relationship is unequal, and that this probably makes certain things harder to say, has not solved anything; they have, however, made the difficulty something the two people can look at together rather than something each of them must quietly manage alone. That is a modest shift, but it tends to change the character of the conversations that follow.

The rest depends less on technique than on conduct sustained over many meetings. It means welcoming correction rather than merely tolerating it, and letting it be seen that being wrong in front of a report is not an injury; it means allowing pauses to run a little longer than is comfortable, since the thing someone most needs to say is often the thing they take longest to reach; it means returning, weeks later, to a remark that seemed to pass unnoticed. It also means admitting doubt, which managers are perhaps least inclined to do. None of this produces trust quickly, since trust of this kind accumulates gradually, through evidence rather than assurance, and a manager who expects it sooner may be mistaking politeness for candour.
