## The passage (by the author)

There is also the question of who gets to name things, and what that right quietly confers. The person who proposes the name that sticks acquires a small but durable authorship over the concept, since every later conversation will take place in the vocabulary they chose; the person whose suggestion was set aside must now speak, for as long as the thing exists, in a colleague's language. I do not wish to suggest that this is a contest most of us consciously enter, and yet the faint discomfort of these discussions arguably owes something to it. It is tempting to conclude that the remedy is a naming convention, a written guideline that removes judgement from the matter altogether, and such conventions do genuine good; they spare us a great many small arguments and make a codebase easier for strangers to read. The difficulty is that a convention can tell us how to spell a name but not what the thing is for, which is precisely the question the argument was trying, clumsily, to settle.

What might it look like to name things more honestly? It seems to me that the most useful change is to treat the argument over a name as a legitimate conversation about scope rather than as a distraction from the real work, and to let it run a little longer than feels comfortable. We might ask, when a name is proposed, what it would forbid as well as what it describes, and whether everyone in the room would recognise the same boundary. We could, perhaps, be more willing to rename things when they have genuinely changed, accepting the considerable cost of the change as the price of a vocabulary that still tells the truth; the old name, after all, is not a monument but a tool. None of this requires a new process or a new tool, only a shift in how we regard a discussion that we are accustomed to treating as faintly embarrassing. It will make some meetings slower, and it will occasionally force a team to admit that it does not yet agree about what it is building.

## Draft A

Few disagreements on a software team are conducted with quite as much quiet intensity as the argument over what to call something. To an outsider the stakes can look trivial—a service, a module, a field in a database, and a handful of people who cannot agree on a word for it—and those involved will often say, a little sheepishly, that it does not really matter. Yet the persistence of these disputes suggests otherwise. A name, once adopted, tends to outlive the conversation that produced it; it shapes how newcomers understand the thing, how its boundaries are drawn, and what gets added to it later. Whoever's name is chosen gains a kind of lasting influence over the work, and it is perhaps not surprising that people sense this even when they cannot quite articulate it.

The usual remedy is a naming convention, and conventions do help. They settle the smaller questions of casing and prefixes and pluralisation, and in doing so they remove a good deal of pointless squabbling. What they cannot do is settle the question that most naming disputes are really about, which is what the thing is for. No style guide can tell us whether a component handles billing or payments or invoicing, because that is not a question about words at all; it is a question about purpose, and the words are merely where the disagreement becomes visible.

It may be more useful, then, to treat a naming debate as a discussion of scope in disguise. Rather than asking which name sounds better, we might ask what each candidate excludes—what a reader would reasonably assume does not belong under it—and whether we are comfortable with that exclusion. We might check, too, whether the people in the room share an understanding of the thing at all, since a dispute that refuses to resolve is often a sign that they do not. And we might accept that names should change when the things they describe change, even though renaming is tedious and occasionally disruptive. The cost of a rename is real, but it is paid once; the cost of a name that no longer fits is paid every time someone reads it and is misled.

## Draft B

Few arguments in a software team last as long, or feel as petty from the outside, as arguments about names. What should this service be called? Is it an account, a customer or a tenant? Is the thing we ship a feature, a module or a product? Onlookers roll their eyes, but the people involved rarely feel they are arguing about nothing, and they are right.

A name is not just a label. Whoever's name is adopted gains lasting influence. The word they chose shapes how others think about the thing, what gets added to it, what seems to belong elsewhere, and who is consulted when it changes. Months later, new colleagues learn the concept through that word and inherit the frame that came with it. Winning a naming debate can quietly mean winning a design debate that nobody explicitly held.

Teams often try to escape these fights with conventions. Style guides say how to case identifiers, which prefixes to use, whether to prefer nouns or verbs. Conventions help. They curb the squabbles about form and let people spend their energy elsewhere. What they cannot do is settle purpose. No rule about casing will tell you whether the thing you are naming is meant to handle one job or three.

That is why I find it more useful to treat naming debates as scope discussions. When two people disagree about a name, they usually disagree about what the thing is for. So ask a different question: what does this name exclude? A good name draws a boundary. If a proposed name would comfortably cover responsibilities the team does not want the component to take on, that is worth knowing before the code grows.

Then check shared understanding. Ask a few people to describe, in their own words, what the name means. If the answers differ, the problem is not the word but the missing agreement behind it, and choosing a word will not fix that on its own.

Finally, accept that names age. When the thing changes, rename it, even though renaming costs effort, touches many files and irritates people. A name that no longer fits misleads every reader who trusts it. Paying the cost once is usually cheaper than paying for the confusion over and over.

## Draft C

Anyone who has spent time in a software team will have witnessed, and probably taken part in, an argument about what something should be called. These disputes are often dismissed as trivial, the kind of distraction that consumes an afternoon while the real work waits, and there is some justice in that view. It seems to me, however, that they persist precisely because they are not trivial at all. Whoever's name is adopted gains a quiet and lasting influence over how everyone else thinks about the thing, since a name carries with it a particular account of what the thing is for and where its boundaries lie. The person who named the service, the module or the concept has, in a sense, written the first chapter of its history, and later contributors will tend to read everything that follows in the light of that chapter.

Conventions help, up to a point, and a shared agreement about casing, prefixes and the order of words can remove a whole category of squabbles; teams that have one are generally spared the most tedious of these debates. What a convention cannot do is settle purpose. It can tell us how to write a name once we have decided what the thing is; it cannot tell us what the thing is, and that, I suspect, is usually what the argument was about in the first place.

Perhaps the more useful approach is to treat naming debates as what they often are: discussions of scope in disguise. Rather than asking which name sounds better, we might ask what each candidate excludes, since a good name draws a line as much as it describes what lies inside it. We might also check, before anyone commits to a choice, whether the people in the room actually share an understanding of the thing being named, because a disagreement about words is sometimes the first visible sign of a disagreement about meaning. When the thing itself changes, as things in software generally do, it may be worth renaming it despite the cost, which is real but rarely as great as the cost of a name that has quietly stopped telling the truth.

## Draft D

Why should a question as small as what to call something provoke such stubborn disagreement among people who otherwise work together quite happily? Anyone who has sat through a long argument about the name of a service or a field will recognise the peculiar intensity of it, the sense that far more is at stake than the words themselves, and it may be that far more usually is. A name, once adopted, shapes how everyone else thinks and talks about the thing it describes, so whoever's name is adopted gains a quiet and lasting influence over the conversations that follow. Naming conventions help, and teams that have agreed on them tend to squabble less over case and prefixes and plurals; but a convention can only govern the form of a name, not the question of what the thing is for, and it is that second question which most naming disputes are really about.

It seems to me that the more useful approach is to treat a naming debate as what it often is, a discussion of scope conducted in disguise. Rather than asking which name sounds best, we might ask what each candidate excludes: which responsibilities it would place outside the thing, and which requests it would make easier to decline. We might also check, perhaps more often than feels necessary, whether everyone involved actually shares the same understanding of what is being named, since two people can agree on a word while imagining quite different things behind it. When the thing itself changes, as things in software tend to do, there is a case for renaming it despite the cost—the broken references, the migration, the period in which both names are in circulation—because a name that no longer describes its object will mislead everyone who reads it. None of this is likely to make the conversation any shorter, though it does, I suspect, make it a conversation about the right thing.

Perhaps the argument over a name is never really about the name at all, but about what we are willing to agree the thing should be.
