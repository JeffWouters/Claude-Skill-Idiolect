# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-06

## The passage (by the author)

I inquired about the house and was told that it was not far away on the hillside, and was yet unfinished. I was pleased with this, and meant to go up and see it when the spell of bad weather of which Landor complained had passed by.

Beside Landor there was only one other historic association which one could enjoy without getting drenched—that was St. David. In wading across the barnyard, I encountered "Boots," an intelligent young man though unduly respectful. He informed me that the old building just across from the stable was the cell of St. David.

I was not prepared for this. All I knew was that St. David was the patron saint of Wales and had a cathedral and a number of other churches dedicated to him. Without too grossly admitting my ignorance, I tried to draw out from my mentor some further biographical facts that my imagination might work on during my stay. He thought that St. David was some relation to King Arthur, but just what the relation was, and whether he was only a relative by marriage, he didn't know. It wasn't very much information, but I was profoundly grateful to him.

I have since read a long article on St. David in the "Cambrian Plutarch." The author goes into the question of the family relations between King Arthur and St. David with great thoroughness, but what conclusion he comes to is not quite evident. He thinks that the people are wrong who say that St. David was a nephew, because he was fifty years older than Arthur. That would make him more likely his uncle. But as he admits that King Arthur may possibly be another name for the constellation Ursa Major, it is difficult to fix the dates exactly. At any rate, the "Cambrian Plutarch" is sure that King Arthur was a Welshman and a credit to the country—and so was St. David. The author was as accurate in regard to the dates as the nature of his subject would allow. He adds apologetically, "It will appear that the life of St. David is rather misplaced with respect to chronological order. But as he was contemporary with all those whose lives have already been given, the anachronism, if such it may be called, can be of no great importance."

## Draft A

It was raining in Wales, and I had given up the hope of seeing anything but the inside of the hotel. The traveler who is kept indoors by the weather becomes grateful for small historical mercies, and is disposed to accept whatever antiquity is offered him without asking too closely for its credentials. So when a servant of the house, observing my disconsolate attitude at the window, pointed across the wet street to an unremarkable building and informed me that it had been the cell of St. David, I received the information with more enthusiasm than it perhaps deserved. I asked what else was known of the saint. He considered the question with the gravity of a man upon whom the reputation of his country depended, and replied that St. David was some relation of King Arthur. Beyond that he would not go.

I did not press him. A vague kinship is often the most satisfactory kind, for it cannot be disproved and it confers a certain dignity upon both parties.

Some time afterward I fell in with a Welsh biographical compendium, [title needed: the compendium], and turned to it for a more exact account. The compiler was a conscientious person who approached the problem in a thoroughly scholarly manner. Was St. David the nephew of Arthur, or was he his uncle? The authorities, it appeared, were divided, and he weighed their claims with an impartiality that did him credit, setting nephew against uncle with as much care as though the honor of both houses hung upon the result. Then he made a concession which I thought extraordinarily handsome. It was possible, he admitted, that Arthur was not a man at all, but a constellation. This would have daunted a less resolute biographer. It did not daunt him. As for the objection that the dates would not agree, he disposed of it in a sentence; chronology, he intimated, was a pedantic consideration which need not detain a serious inquirer into the lives of saints.

I closed the book with a new respect for the servant. He had said that the saint was some relation of the king, and he had said no more; the compendium, after much labor and a good deal of astronomy, had arrived at precisely the same conclusion, with the added uncertainty as to whether the king was in the sky. On the whole I preferred the servant's version. It was shorter.

## Draft B

It rained in Wales, as it has a way of doing, and I was glad of any excuse for standing under a roof. The excuse was furnished by a servant at the hotel, who, seeing me at the window, pointed across the way to a low stone building and informed me that it was the cell of St. David. I asked him what he knew of the saint. He knew that he was a very holy man, and that he was some relation to King Arthur. When I pressed him as to the degree of kinship he became vague. He thought it was on the mother's side, but he would not commit himself. It was enough for him that the two were in the family, and I have since come to think that it was enough for anybody.

For later, in a more studious mood, I turned to [title of Welsh biographical compendium], which I had supposed would settle the matter. It did nothing of the kind. It did something much better. It treated the question with that large hospitality which is the charm of the older kind of learning. Some authorities, it appeared, made St. David the nephew of Arthur; others, with equal confidence, made him the uncle. The compiler weighed the evidence on both sides with great fairness and did not presume to decide between them. He was, moreover, candid enough to admit that there were scholars who held that Arthur was not a king at all, but a constellation. This did not seem to him to be a fatal objection. A saint might very well be related to a constellation; there are families in which stranger things have happened.

As for the dates, which in the case of a nephew and an uncle might be thought to have some bearing on the matter, he dismissed them with a gesture. Chronology, he intimated, was a pedantic science, and not to be allowed to interfere with the affections of a people.

I confess that I closed the book with a feeling of gratitude. There is a kind of history which tells us precisely what happened, and leaves us nothing to do but to believe it. There is another kind which leaves the doors open and the windows unlatched, so that the imagination may come and go as it pleases. In a country where it rains so much, one needs a history of the second kind. The servant at the hotel had been, after all, a very sound historian. He had told me all that was known, and he had not spoiled it by knowing more.

## Draft C

It rained in Wales. I had been told that it would, and I had not believed it, in the way one does not believe any warning about weather that has not yet arrived. It arrived. It came down on the hills and the slate roofs and on me, and it went on coming down with a patience I have never seen in English rain, which always seems to have somewhere else to be.

On the second wet afternoon I stood at the window of the hotel and looked out at nothing in particular, and a servant of the house, perhaps taking pity on me, came and stood beside me and pointed through the rain at a low building across the way. That, he said, was St. David's cell. I asked him who St. David was, not because I did not know, but because I wanted to hear what he would say. He thought about it for some time. St. David, he said at last, was a relation of King Arthur's. I asked him what relation. He did not know. He was a relation, and that seemed to him quite enough, and he went away to attend to something else.

I did not think it quite enough, and when I got home I looked the matter up in a Welsh biographical compendium, a large and learned work which I had supposed would settle it. It did not settle it. It considered, with great seriousness, whether St. David had been Arthur's nephew or Arthur's uncle, and it laid out the case for each, and it declined to choose between them. It then observed, in the same even tone, that there were scholars who held that Arthur was not a man at all but a constellation, and that this view could not be lightly set aside. As to the question of dates, which I had innocently thought might help, the compendium said that chronology was of small account in such matters, and passed on.

I closed the book with a new respect for the hotel servant. He had told me that St. David was some relation of Arthur's, and had not pretended to know more. The learned work had told me the same thing at much greater length, and had added the possibility that one of the two parties was a group of stars. Of the two authorities, I think I prefer the one who stood with me at the window, in the rain, and did not guess.

## Draft D

It rained the whole of the day on which I came into that part of Wales, and it was still raining when I sat at the window of the hotel and looked out upon a building across the way. A servant of the establishment observed my curiosity and informed me that it was the cell of St. David. I asked him who St. David was. He answered, with the air of one conferring a distinction upon his questioner, that he was a relation of King Arthur.

I pressed him for particulars. Was the saint a brother, a cousin, a son? Here his knowledge failed him, though his confidence did not. He was sure of the kinship; the precise degree of it was a matter he was content to leave to antiquarians. It was sufficient for him that the saint and the king belonged to the same distinguished family.

I did not at the time consider the answer satisfactory. Afterward I came upon a Welsh compendium of biography, and I turned to it in the expectation that scholarship would supply what the hotel had left indefinite. The learned author took the question seriously. He set out the evidence that St. David was the nephew of Arthur, and then the evidence that he was his uncle, and weighed the one against the other with admirable impartiality and no conclusion. He went further. He was willing to admit that Arthur himself might be a constellation, and that the whole matter might belong to the heavens rather than to the history of Wales. As for the difficulty that the dates would not agree, he disposed of it in a sentence; chronology, he intimated, was not a consideration that a reasonable man would allow to stand in the way of a good genealogy.

I closed the book with a new respect for the servant. He had said less than the scholar, but he had said nothing that the scholar was prepared to deny. A relation of King Arthur the saint remained, whether as nephew or uncle, whether to a king or to a group of stars. Perhaps the vagueness was not ignorance after all. It may have been the only honest position open to a man who lived across the way from the cell, and who was obliged to answer the question for every traveler the weather kept indoors.
