## The passage (by the author)

But in the middle of the track was an indubitable fact. By all the rules of argumentation it had no business to be there, trespassing on the right of way. But there it was! We trembled to think of the impending collision.

But the collision between the argument and the fact never happened. The "humanly speaking" was the switch that turned the argument safely on a parallel track, where it went whizzing by the fact without the least injury to either. Many things which are humanly speaking impossible are of the most common occurrence and the theologian knew it.

It is only by the use of this saving clause that one may safely moralize or generalize or indulge in the mildest form of prediction. Strictly speaking, no one has a right to express any opinion about such complex and incomprehensible aggregations of humanity as the United States of America or the British Empire. Humanly speaking, they both are impossible. Antecedently to experience the Constitution of Utopia as expounded by Sir Thomas More would be much more probable. It has a certain rational coherence. If it existed at all it would hang together, being made out of whole cloth. But how does the British Empire hold together? It seems to be made of shreds and patches. It is full of anomalies and temporary makeshifts. Why millions of people, who do not know each other, should be willing to die rather than to be separated from each other, is something not easily explained. Nevertheless the British Empire exists, and, through all the changes which threaten it, grows in strength.

The perils that threaten the United States of America are so obvious that anybody can see them. So far as one can see, the Republic ought to have been destroyed long ago by political corruption, race prejudice, unrestricted immigration and the growth of monopolies. The only way to account for its present existence is that there is something about it that is not so easily seen. Disease is often more easily diagnosed than health. But we should remember that the Republic is not out of danger. It is a very salutary thing to bring its perils to the attention of the too easy-going citizens. It is well to have a Jeremiah, now and then, to speak unwelcome truths.

## Draft A

There was once a theologian whose reasoning I have always admired, not for its conclusions but for its manners. He built his argument with great care, one proposition resting upon another, and at the critical point he added a small qualifying clause. It was a modest thing, a few words of caution tucked into the middle of a sentence. Yet it saved him. Whenever a stubborn fact came down the road, his logic stepped politely aside and let it pass. The two never met, and so neither was the worse for the encounter.

I have thought of that caveat when listening to those who reason about great nations. The sprawling empire and the continental republic are, on paper, very irrational things. They are too large to be governed, too various to agree, and too scattered to be defended. The imagined commonwealth of the philosophers is a much tidier affair. It is small enough to be seen from a hilltop; its citizens know one another and share the same opinions, and its laws follow from first principles as neatly as the steps of a proof. By every rule of right reasoning it ought to last, and the unwieldy giant ought to fall apart.

But the facts have a way of declining the invitation. The tidy commonwealth remains where it was born, in a book. The improbable nation goes on, and not only goes on but grows stronger, as if it had never been told how improbable it was. Perhaps we should add a caveat of our own and say that large nations are irrational, as a rule.

I would apply this to America. Her dangers are plain enough, and nobody need hunt for them. They are printed every morning. A people who have survived so many obvious perils must have strengths that are not so obvious, and it is these hidden resources that the gloomy reasoner leaves out of his account. That the house has not fallen is itself a kind of evidence about the foundations.

I would not, however, silence the prophets. Their warnings are valuable even when their predictions fail, and perhaps most valuable then, for it may be that the warning is the reason for the failure. A nation that is told it is in danger has at least a chance to look about it.

## Draft B

There was once a theologian who had a way of reasoning that never came to grief. His logic was of the strictest sort, and yet it never ran into a fact that it could not get round. The secret lay in a single cautious phrase. Whenever he had proved a thing beyond all doubt, he added, "so far as appears." That was enough. The facts might go their way and the argument its own, and neither need be embarrassed by the meeting.

I have often wished that our political philosophers had learned the trick. They reason about nations as if a nation ought to behave like a well-made syllogism. Give them a small commonwealth, neatly bounded, where every citizen knows his neighbor and the whole business of government can be taken in at a glance, and they will show you that it is rational. Give them a sprawling empire, or a republic that stretches across a continent, and they will show you with equal clearness that it cannot last. It is too large to be governed and too various to agree. It has no good reason for existing.

But the sprawling nations do not read the philosophers. They go on existing. They grow stronger in the very ways in which they were proved to be weak, while the tidy commonwealth, which had everything in its favor except the facts, remains a pleasant thing to imagine. Here the theologian's caveat would have saved a good deal of trouble. The large state is improbable, so far as appears; it is irrational, so far as appears. What does not appear is the reason it holds together.

I think this is the case with America. Her dangers are plain to every one. They have been set forth often enough, and a man need not be a prophet to see them. Yet a country with so many obvious reasons for failing, which somehow does not fail, must have reasons for succeeding that are not so obvious. The strength is hidden, perhaps, precisely because the weakness is so conspicuous.

I would not for that reason silence the prophets. A nation is none the worse for being told of its perils, even by those who are sure it will perish of them. Only I should like them to add, at the end of each warning, the theologian's modest phrase.

## Draft C

There was once a theologian, [name needed], whose reasoning I have always admired for its prudence. He did not ask his logic to do more than logic can safely do. Having proved a thing to his own satisfaction, he was careful to add, "so far as the nature of the case permits." It was a modest phrase, and it saved him from no end of embarrassment. For whenever his argument came to the edge of a stubborn fact, it did not dash itself against it. It simply bowed, murmured its little caveat, and went round.

I would recommend the same courtesy to those who reason about great nations. By all the rules of political science, a very large state ought not to exist. It is too sprawling to be governed and too various to agree. The philosopher sits down and draws the plan of a commonwealth as it should be. It is small and compact. Its citizens know one another by sight. They meet in one place and settle their affairs by the light of Right Reason. Everything about it is tidy, and everything about it is probable. The only thing to be said against it is that it does not happen.

Meanwhile the improbable empires and the unreasonable republics go on. They stretch across mountains and deserts that were never meant to be crossed by a single law. They take in peoples who have nothing in common but a flag and a grievance against the tax collector. They ought to fall apart, and it must be confessed that now and then they look as if they would. Yet somehow they endure, and not only endure but grow stronger. The logician, finding his conclusions contradicted, would do well to fall back on the theologian's phrase.

So with America. Its dangers are not hidden; they are plain to every one who passes by. Any one can make a list of them, and a good many people have. But I am disposed to think that a nation which has so many obvious reasons for failing, and has not yet failed, must have some reasons for succeeding that are not so obvious.

This is not to say that we should stop our ears to the prophets. A prophet of evil is a useful person to have about the house. He keeps us from growing too comfortable. Only we need not believe that his warnings are the whole of the truth, so far as the nature of the case permits.

## Draft D

There is an old theologian, [name needed], whose reasoning I have always admired for a single word. He would lay out his argument with great confidence, step by careful step, and then, at the very point where it seemed bound to crash into some stubborn fact of the world, he would add "probably." That small caveat did a great deal of work. It let the logic run on its own track while reality ran on another, and the two never had to meet. Nobody could quite accuse him of being wrong, because he had never quite claimed to be right.

I find myself wanting to borrow his word whenever I think about large nations. By every tidy standard, a sprawling empire or a continental republic ought not to work. Sit down and design a commonwealth from scratch and you will draw something small and orderly: a few thousand citizens who know one another, share a language and a set of habits, and can gather in one place to settle their affairs. That imagined state is rational. It is easy to reason about. The great nations, by contrast, look irrational and improbable. They stretch across climates and tongues and interests that seem to have no reason to hang together. On paper they should fly apart.

And yet they endure. More than that, they tend to grow stronger. The argument says they are impossible; the facts keep turning up to say otherwise. The honest thinker, faced with this, has two choices. He can insist the argument is sound and wait for the facts to come round, or he can add his cautious "probably" and admit that the world is under no obligation to follow his reasoning.

America is the case that tests this most. Its dangers are plain, and nobody needs to hunt for them: [example needed]. A nation that carries weaknesses so openly and still holds together must have strengths that are harder to see. What is visible is the strain; what is hidden is whatever keeps absorbing it. I suspect the hidden part is larger than the critics allow.

None of this means the prophets of decline should be ignored. Their warnings are valuable, and a nation that stops listening to them is in real trouble. But it would be wise to read their forecasts the way we read the theologian, with the qualifier in mind. The collapse they predict will come, probably.
