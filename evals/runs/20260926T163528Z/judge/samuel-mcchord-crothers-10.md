# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-10

## The passage (by the author)

I remember, some years ago, how merrily you used to laugh about the "calamity-howler," whose habitat at that time was Kansas. The farmers of Kansas were not then as prosperous as they are now. When several bad years came together they didn't like it, and began to make complaints. Their raucous cries you found very amusing.

The calamity-howler, being ignorant of the laws of political economy and of the conditions of progress, did not take his calamities in the spirit in which they were offered to him by the rest of the country. He did not find satisfaction in the thought that other people were prosperous though he was not. Instead of acting reasonably and voting the straight ticket from motives of party loyalty, he raised all sorts of irrelevant issues. He treated Prosperity as if it were a local issue, instead of a plank in the National Platform.

Now, all this was opposed to your good-natured philosophy of progress. You were eminently practical, and it was a part of your creed never to "go behind the returns." As to Prosperity, it was "first come, first served." In this land of opportunity the person who first sees an opportunity should take it, asking no questions as to why he came by it. It is his by right of discovery.

You were always a great believer in the good old American doctrine of Manifest Destiny. This was a big country and destined to grow bigger. To you bigness was its own excuse for being. Optimism was as natural as breathing. It was manifest destiny that cities and corporations and locomotives and armies and navies and national debts and daily newspapers, with their Sunday supplements, and bank clearances and tariffs and insurance companies and the price of living should go up. It was all according to a beautiful natural law, "as fire ascending seeks the sun." Besides these things, it was manifest destiny that other things not so good should grow bigger also,—graft and slums and foolish luxury. They were all involved in the increasing bigness of things.

## Draft A

He was an optimist, and he never let anyone forget it. I remember how he talked about the farmers out in Kansas, the ones who wrote letters to the papers and stood up at meetings to complain about their mortgages and their crops and the price they got for them. He had no patience for any of it. To him they were simply men who had failed to see the bright side.

What puzzled him, or what he pretended puzzled him, was that they would not take comfort in the good fortune around them. The country was doing well, he said. Other men were getting rich. Towns were rising, railroads were running, money was moving. Why should a farmer sit on his porch and grumble about his own bad year when the nation as a whole was prospering? He seemed honestly to believe that another man's prosperity ought to be consolation enough, and he mocked the farmers for being too small-minded to accept it.

His view of opportunity was just as simple. It belonged to whoever grabbed it first. There was no use asking whether the arrangement was fair, because fairness had nothing to do with it. The land, the contracts, the good positions were all out there, and the quick man took them while the slow man stood about talking. If you came late, that was your affair. He said this without any cruelty that I could hear. He said it the way a man states the weather.

And he believed, above all, that everything would keep growing. Cities would grow. Debts would grow. Prices would grow. He would add, with a kind of cheerful shrug, that corruption would grow too, and so would the slums. None of this troubled him, because none of it could be helped. It was all part of the same great movement, the expansion of the country, and to object to one piece of it was to object to the whole. You might as well complain about the tide.

I think of him now whenever I hear someone say that progress is inevitable. He had that same easy certainty, and it left no room at all for the man on the losing end.

## Draft B

I remember an optimist of the old school who had no patience whatever with the farmers of Kansas. They were always complaining, he said. He could not see what they had to complain about, when the country as a whole had never been so prosperous and there was more wealth changing hands in a single season than their grandfathers had seen in a lifetime. Why should a man sit on his farm and grumble when his neighbors were doing so well? It seemed to him a mean and envious disposition that would not accept the general prosperity of others as a sufficient consolation for its own private losses.

His philosophy was a simple one, and he held it with the serenity of a man who has never been troubled by a doubt. Opportunity, he said, belongs to whoever grabs it first. It is not handed round like the plate at a church supper, so that each one in the pew gets his modest share; it lies in the road, and the man who is quick enough picks it up and goes on his way. If the farmer was not quick enough, that was unfortunate. It was not a grievance.

What impressed me most was his faith in national expansion. He believed that everything in the country was bound to grow, and he did not greatly care what it was. The cities would grow, and the debts would grow along with them. Prices would rise. The corruption of politics would increase, for there would be more to be corrupt about, and the slums would spread as steadily and as naturally as the cities that contained them. He spoke of all this without the slightest alarm. It was inevitable; and what was inevitable could hardly be wrong.

I confess that I rather envied him. It must be a comfortable thing to look upon a growing slum and see in it nothing but an additional proof of national vigor. Yet I could not help thinking of the Kansas farmer, who was also a part of the nation and who was not growing at all, and who might have found the optimist's cheerfulness a good deal easier to share if some small portion of all that inevitable growth had happened to come his way.

## Draft C

I once knew an optimist of the robust and uncomplaining sort, a man who had made up his mind about the universe and found it very much to his liking. He had no patience with the farmers of Kansas. They were, he said, a discontented people who complained of their hardships [example needed: what the farmers complained of] and would not be comforted by any consideration that a reasonable man could offer them. Were not other men growing rich all about them, and was not the country as a whole more prosperous than it had ever been? That the prosperity happened to belong to somebody else seemed to him a very small objection. A farmer who could not rejoice in the good fortune of his neighbours was lacking in public spirit.

His philosophy was simple, and he held it with an untroubled conscience. Opportunity, he believed, belongs to whoever grabs it first. It is not distributed; it is seized. The man who arrived late and found the best of everything already taken had only himself to blame, while the man who arrived early had nothing whatever to apologise for and might reasonably expect the gratitude of those who came after. He did not think of this as a hard doctrine. To him it was merely the way of the world, and he would have been astonished to hear it described as a creed at all.

But what I chiefly remember is his faith in expansion. Everything was growing, and everything ought to grow. The cities were growing, and so were the debts, and the prices of things went steadily upward in a manner which he took to be an unmistakable sign of national health. He admitted, when pressed, that corruption was growing too, and that the slums were spreading along with everything else. He was not disturbed. These things, he explained, belonged together, and it was childish to wish for the one without the other. A nation that expands must expect to bulge in unexpected places.

I have sometimes wondered what he would have said had the growing ever stopped. I suspect he would have found a reason to go on believing. There is a kind of optimism that nothing can refute, because it has never troubled to look.

## Draft D

I remember him well, for he was the most cheerful man I ever knew, and his cheerfulness was of the kind that is not easily disturbed by the misfortunes of others. He had been out among the farmers of Kansas at a time when they were having a hard struggle of it, and he came back with no great sympathy for them. They were, he said, a complaining lot.

What vexed him was not that they were poor, but that they would not take comfort in the prosperity of their neighbours. Here was the country growing rich all about them, and they sat on their fences and grumbled because none of the riches had come their way. He thought this a very narrow view. A man with a proper public spirit, he held, ought to be able to look across the state line and rejoice. If the harvest had failed at home, there was a boom somewhere else, and it seemed to him mere churlishness to refuse to be consoled by it.

His philosophy of opportunity was simple and, as he stated it, entirely satisfactory. Opportunity belonged to whoever grabbed it first. It was not handed round like bread at a table, a slice to each; it lay in the road, and the quick man picked it up. That the slow man should afterward complain of being hungry struck him as a confession rather than a grievance.

But his faith was larger than any doctrine of the individual. He believed in growth, and he believed in it without discrimination. Everything was growing, and everything ought to grow. The cities were growing, and that was well. The debts were growing, and that was well too, for a debt was only a sign of confidence in the future. Prices were going up; so, he admitted, was corruption, and the slums were spreading at the edges of the towns. He did not deny any of it. He only smiled and said it was inevitable. It was all part of the same great movement, and one could not expect to have the expansion without the things that expanded with it.

I used to envy him a little. It is a fine thing to have a creed which no fact can shake, because every fact is taken as fresh evidence in its favour.
