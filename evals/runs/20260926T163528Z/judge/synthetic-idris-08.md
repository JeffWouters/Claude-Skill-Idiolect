## The passage (by the author)

There is also the matter of tone, which suffers considerably when every exchange must carry its whole meaning on its own. In a room, a blunt remark is softened by a smile or corrected at once by a look of puzzlement; in writing, left overnight, it has hours in which to be misread, and the reader has no one to ask. The standard remedy is to write more carefully and more completely, and I do not wish to suggest that this is wrong, since a well-constructed message that anticipates the obvious questions is a genuine kindness to a reader who cannot interrupt. The difficulty is that thoroughness has a cost of its own. A long, careful message can feel less like an invitation than a verdict, arriving fully formed with its reasoning already settled, and the colleague who receives it may reasonably wonder whether there is still room to disagree or only room to comply. Arguably, the more polished our asynchronous writing becomes, the more it conceals the uncertainty that would, in a shared room, have drawn other people in.

What might a more humane etiquette of distance look like? It seems to me that it would begin with a willingness to leave some things deliberately unfinished, to write not only what we have concluded but what we are unsure of and where we would welcome a different view. It might mean that a decision reached while half the team is asleep is treated as provisional until they have had a working day to respond, which is slower and occasionally frustrating but which acknowledges that their judgement is not a formality to be collected afterwards. It might also mean sharing the discomfort of the inconvenient meeting, so that the same people are not always the ones speaking at the edge of their evening, tired and a little less articulate than their colleagues. I suspect none of this requires a new tool or an elaborate policy; it asks only that we remember, each time we write, that the reader is a person who will meet our words alone.

## Draft A

What becomes of tone when nobody is in the room? In a conversation held face to face, much of what we mean is carried by cues we hardly notice ourselves giving: a pause, a raised eyebrow, the softening of a voice when a suggestion is still only a suggestion. Asynchronous writing strips almost all of this away, and a distributed team that relies on it must find other ways to signal warmth, uncertainty and openness. The usual advice is to write more thoroughly, to anticipate questions and supply context, and this advice is sound as far as it goes. It may be, however, that a very thorough message carries a risk of its own, since the more complete it appears, the more easily it can read as a decision already taken rather than an invitation to think together.

It seems to me that a few habits can soften this without sacrificing clarity. The first is to share open questions explicitly, naming what the writer does not yet know and would genuinely like others to weigh in on, so that the reader can see where the thinking is still unfinished. The second is to treat decisions made while colleagues elsewhere are asleep as tentative until those colleagues have had a chance to respond; a decision reached by whoever happened to be awake is not quite the same as a decision reached by the team. There is also the matter of meetings, which a distributed team can rarely avoid entirely, and whose awkward hours might be rotated rather than always falling on the same people.

The most important thing to remember, I suspect, is that each reader is alone when they read. They cannot see the writer's face or hear the tone that was intended, and whatever the message leaves ambiguous they will resolve by themselves, sometimes generously and sometimes not. Perhaps the kindest thing a writer can do is to write as though that solitude were always in view.

## Draft B

Writing for a distributed team is, in some respects, an exercise in writing without a face. In a room we rely on a great many small signals to soften what we say—a tone of voice, a pause, the expression that tells a colleague we are thinking aloud rather than delivering a verdict—and we rarely notice how much work those signals do until they are taken away. An asynchronous message has none of them. It arrives stripped of its context and is read, often hours later, by someone who must supply the tone for themselves.

The natural response is to write more thoroughly, anticipating questions and setting out the reasoning in full, and this does help. A careful message spares its readers a great deal of guesswork. Yet thoroughness has a curious side effect: the more complete a message appears, the more it tends to read as a decision already taken. Readers who were not present for the thinking may reasonably conclude that there is nothing left for them to contribute, and so they say nothing, and the silence is then mistaken for agreement.

What might we do differently? It seems worth sharing open questions explicitly rather than leaving them implied—saying plainly which parts of a proposal are settled and which are still genuinely uncertain. Decisions made while part of the team is asleep might be treated as tentative until those colleagues have had a chance to respond, since otherwise the working hours of one location quietly become the hours in which everything is decided. Where meetings are unavoidable, the awkward times could rotate, so that the burden of the early call or the late one does not always fall on the same people.

Underneath all of this lies a simpler observation, and perhaps the most important one. However many people a message is addressed to, each of them reads it alone. There is no one beside them to catch their eye or to say that the phrasing sounded harsher than it was meant. If we could keep that solitary reader in mind as we write, I suspect a good deal of the rest would follow.

## Draft C

Distributed teams run on writing. Decisions, questions, updates and disagreements all travel through messages that someone reads hours after they were sent, often in another time zone. That shift changes how tone lands and who gets to take part, and both deserve more attention than they usually get.

In person, we soften our words without thinking. A smile, a pause, a raised eyebrow tells the listener whether we are joking, unsure or firm. Written messages lack all of these cues. A short reply can read as curt, a question as criticism, a neutral statement as displeasure. Readers fill the gap with their own mood, and not always generously.

The common advice is to write more thoroughly: give context, explain reasoning, anticipate questions. That helps. But thorough messages carry their own risk. A long, well-structured proposal can read as a closed decision. The more complete it looks, the less room readers feel they have to push back, especially if they are junior, new or working in a second language.

A few habits help keep writing inclusive. First, share open questions explicitly. Say what you are unsure about and where you want input, so readers know the door is still open. Second, treat decisions made while others sleep as tentative. If a discussion concluded during hours when part of the team was offline, mark the outcome as provisional until they have had a chance to respond. Otherwise, the same people are always presented with conclusions instead of invited into choices.

Third, rotate awkward meeting hours. When synchronous time is unavoidable, the inconvenience of early mornings or late evenings should not always fall on the same people. Sharing the burden is a small, visible signal that everyone's time counts equally.

Finally, remember that each reader is alone. They open your message without you there to clarify, without colleagues nearby to ask what you meant. Write for that person: someone who wants to understand you, has only your words to go on, and may be reading at the end of a long day. A little warmth and a clear invitation go a long way when the page is all they have.

## Draft D

Much of the work of a distributed team is carried out in writing, and it is easy to underestimate how much of our ordinary tact depends on things that writing cannot carry. In a room, a proposal arrives with a tone of voice, a pause, a glance around the table to see whether anyone looks uneasy; in a message, it arrives alone, and the reader must supply all of that for themselves. The usual advice is to write more thoroughly, to anticipate objections and explain the reasoning in full, and this is sound as far as it goes. What it tends to overlook is that a thorough message can read as a closed decision. The more complete the argument, the less room it seems to leave for anyone else, and a colleague reading it some hours later may reasonably conclude that the matter has already been settled without them.

It seems to me that the remedy lies less in the length of our messages than in what we choose to leave visibly unfinished. We might share the questions we have not yet answered alongside the conclusions we have reached, so that others can see where their contribution would actually count. Decisions made while part of the team is asleep could be treated as tentative by default, open to revision once everyone has had a chance to read them, rather than presented as settled simply because the people who happened to be awake agreed. Where a meeting is genuinely necessary, the awkward hours might be rotated, so that the inconvenience does not always fall on the same people, who would otherwise have every reason to suspect that their time matters rather less than that of their colleagues.

Perhaps the most useful thing to keep in mind, though, is simply that each reader is alone when they read. There is nobody beside them to soften a curt phrase or to reassure them that a question was not meant as criticism; there is only the text, and whatever mood they bring to it. Writing with that solitary reader in view is, I suspect, the better part of what inclusion in a distributed team actually requires.
