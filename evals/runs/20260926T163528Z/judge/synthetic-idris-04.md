# Judge packet synthetic-idris-04

## The passage (by the author)

What does it mean to decline a request that is entirely reasonable, made by a colleague whose judgement we trust and whose work we admire? Most of the advice on the subject treats refusal as a problem of assertiveness, as though the difficulty lay chiefly in finding the nerve to say the word and the phrasing with which to soften it. That advice is not wrong, and a considerable number of us could stand to hear it; yet it seems to address the easier case, in which the request is unwelcome and the person making it is someone we would rather not oblige in any event. The harder case is the one in which we would genuinely like to help, in which the request is sensible and the colleague deserves a yes, and in which we decline anyway because the hours are already spoken for. I suspect that what makes this kind of refusal so uncomfortable is not the loss of the task but the small injury it seems to do to a relationship we value.

Consider the experience of being asked, late in the afternoon, whether you might look over a colleague's design before it goes to the wider group. The request is modest, the colleague is someone who has helped you more times than you could easily count, and the review would, in some sense, be a pleasure; yet you already know that the evening is promised to work you have been deferring for several days. There follows a curious interior negotiation—a rehearsal of reasons, a calculation of how much sleep might be spared, a search for some arrangement in which nothing has to be refused at all. Is it any wonder that so many of us simply say yes and then do both things badly? The yes costs nothing at the moment it is given; the cost arrives later, spread thinly across the review, the deferred work and the person who has to live with the consequences of both, who is very often ourselves.

## Draft A

Most advice about saying no is really advice about confidence. Be clear, be firm, don't over-explain, don't apologise. That works well enough for the requests you never wanted in the first place: the meeting with no purpose, the task that belongs to someone else, the favour from someone who asks too often. In those cases the difficulty is nerve, and nerve can be practised.

The harder case gets far less attention. It is the request that is perfectly sensible, from a colleague you respect, for help you would genuinely like to give, arriving at a moment when you simply do not have the time. Confidence is not the problem here. You are not unsure whether you are allowed to refuse; you are unsure whether you want to, because part of you would rather say yes.

Picture it. Late in the day, a trusted peer asks whether you could review their design before tomorrow. The design matters, their judgement is good, and your opinion would be useful. But the evening is already committed, and you knew that before they asked. Nothing about the request is unreasonable. That is exactly what makes it hard to decline.

So most people agree anyway. It feels generous, and in the moment it costs nothing but a small rearrangement. The cost comes later. The evening you had promised elsewhere gets squeezed, or the review gets done tired and half-attentive, which helps nobody. Often the person who pays is you: the sleep you lose, the work you postpone, the quiet resentment that builds when helpfulness keeps overriding your own plans. A rushed review can even be worse for the colleague than an honest delay.

Refusing well in this situation is less about firmness and more about honesty. Say that you would like to help, that you cannot do it tonight, and what you could offer instead, if anything. [example needed] A respected colleague is usually the person most likely to understand, precisely because they would not want you to pretend.

## Draft B

Most of what is written about saying no concerns confidence. We are encouraged to be firm, to stop apologising, and to recognise that requests from other people do not automatically become our obligations. This is useful advice when the request is one we never wanted in the first place, and there are certainly situations in which it applies. It seems to me, though, that it addresses the easier case. The harder one is the request that is entirely reasonable, from someone we respect, which we would genuinely like to help with and simply do not have the time for.

Suppose a colleague whose work you trust asks late in the day whether you might review a design they are about to share more widely. It is a sensible thing to ask; you are well placed to help, and you would probably enjoy doing it. The difficulty is that the evening is already committed, and not to anything that could easily be moved. No amount of confidence resolves this, because the problem is not that you lack the nerve to refuse but that refusing feels like a small betrayal of something you value: the colleague, the work, and perhaps your own sense of being the kind of person who helps.

Agreeing therefore seems the generous choice, and in the moment it usually is the easier one. The cost of agreeing, however, does not disappear; it is merely deferred, and it tends to fall later on someone, quite often on yourself. The other commitment of the evening is kept badly or not at all, the review is done with less care than it deserved, or the tiredness carries into the following day and is paid for there. Perhaps the more honest kindness is to say plainly that you would like to help and cannot do so tonight, and to offer what you genuinely can, even if that is rather less than was asked. A colleague worth respecting will usually understand, and the refusal might in the end serve the work better than a reluctant yes.

## Draft C

Why is it so much harder to refuse a good request than a bad one? Most of the advice written about saying no concerns the unwanted ask: the meeting that should never have been scheduled, the favour requested by someone who asks for favours rather too readily. For these cases the prescription is confidence, and it works well enough, since there is little to lose by declining something one never wished to do. It may be that the harder case, and the more common one among conscientious people, is the request that is entirely reasonable, made by someone one respects, at a moment when one would genuinely like to help but simply has no time.

Consider a trusted colleague who asks, late in the day, whether you could review their design that evening. The request is sensible; the design matters; the colleague is someone whose judgement you value and whose work you would be glad to improve. Your evening, however, has already been promised to something else. No amount of confidence helps here, because the difficulty was never a lack of nerve. It lies instead in the fact that both the request and the prior commitment are legitimate, and that refusing one of them feels like a small betrayal of the kind of colleague you would prefer to be.

One agrees, then, and the review is done, perhaps a little hurriedly, and the colleague is grateful. The cost does not disappear, though; it is merely deferred, and it tends to fall later on whoever has the least power to object, which is frequently oneself. The commitment that was displaced must still be honoured somehow, whether by an evening lost to catching up or by a promise quietly broken to someone who was not in the room when the decision was made.

It seems to me that a considered refusal, offered with an honest explanation and perhaps an alternative time, is usually the more generous answer to a respected colleague. Perhaps the kindest yes is sometimes a no that has been properly explained.

## Draft D

Most of what is written about saying no concerns requests we would rather not accept. The advice is to be confident, to decline without excessive apology, to recognise that our time is our own. This is reasonable counsel, and for the unwanted request it is probably sufficient. But it leaves untouched a harder and, I think, more common case: the request we would genuinely like to accept, from someone we respect, for something that is plainly worth doing, at a moment when we simply do not have the time. Confidence is of little help here, since the difficulty is not that we lack the nerve to refuse but that we do not really want to.

Consider a colleague whose judgement you trust, who asks late in the day whether you might review their design before a decision is taken. The request is sensible; the design matters; your view would probably improve it. And the evening is already committed, perhaps to other work, perhaps to something outside work altogether. Nothing about the situation invites a firm refusal. Everything about it invites a quiet yes, followed by the private rearrangement of whatever had been planned.

The trouble is that the cost of agreeing does not disappear; it is merely deferred, and it tends to fall somewhere we did not intend. Sometimes it falls on the work we had already promised, which now receives less attention than it deserved. Sometimes it falls on the people who were expecting our evening. Most often, I suspect, it falls on ourselves, in the form of tiredness, of resentment we would prefer not to feel towards a colleague we like, and of a gradual erosion of the boundaries that allow us to be useful at all. The review itself may be done less well than it would have been on another day.

What would it mean to refuse such a request well? Perhaps it means acknowledging openly that we want to help, explaining that we cannot tonight, and offering whatever we honestly can instead. That is not a lack of generosity. It is, arguably, the more respectful answer, since it treats the colleague as someone who would rather have a truthful no than a grudging yes.
