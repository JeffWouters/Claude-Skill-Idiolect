# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-05

## The passage (by the author)

A couple of our recent commentators upon Mr. Chesterton have taken a fling at the matter of his not being as huge as, it seems to them, he has been made out to be. I remember that when I saw him before I was even startled to find him more monstrous than even he had appeared in his pictures. He appears to take part a good deal in pageants in England; and recent photographs of him as Falstaff, or Tony Weller, or Mr. Pickwick, or somebody like that, have not altogether squared up with my recollection of him. True, he has not quite the bulk he had before; but it is a captious critic, I should say, who would not consider him sufficiently elephantine for all ordinary purposes.

He was saying (much to the delight of the house) when I became one of the audience, that he would "not regard this as the time or the occasion for him to comment upon the lid on liquor." A bit later in the course of his answer to the question he had propounded, "Shall We Abolish the Inevitable," he got an especially good hand when he remarked: "People nowadays do not like statements having authority—but they will accept any statement without authority." He concluded his denunciation of the idea of fatalism with the declaration: "Whatever man is, he is not in one sense a part of nature." "He has committed crimes, Crimes," he repeated—with gusto in the use of the word,—"and performed heroisms which no animal ever tried to do. Let us hold ourselves free from the boundary of the material order of things, for so shall we have a chance in the future to do things far too historic for prophecy."

I darted back toward Room Three, ran into Mr. Widdecombe, we wheeled, and saw the mountain approaching. Whereas before, this off-stage place had been deserted, now the scene was populous—with the figures of agitated young women. Mr. Widdecombe, however, with much valiance secured Mr. Chesterton. "Yes, yes," he said, and (remarkable remark!), "I had the pleasure of meeting you in England." He glanced about rather nervously at the dancing figures seeking to obtain him, and led the way for me into the dressing room. Mr. Widdecombe pulled the door to from without.

## Draft A

He has been cut down to size, we are told. The critics have been at him for some time now, with the small, sharp instruments of their trade, and the word has gone round that the great man is not, after all, so very great. Well, we went to hear him. We sat among the American multitude in [lecture hall needed] and waited, somewhat in the spirit of one who goes to see whether a famous mountain has actually been lowered by the surveyors. It had not. He came out upon the platform, and he was enormous. He was enormous in the plain physical sense, which everybody knows about, and he was enormous in some other sense, difficult to denote, which fills a hall as a tide fills a harbour. The critics, you perceive, had been measuring the wrong thing.

His subject, as nearly as we could keep hold of it, was fatalism, and he would have none of it. Man, he said (and he said it in a good many delightful ways), is not simply a part of nature, carried along with the leaves and the weather. Man stands apart. He looks at nature; nature does not look at him. [example needed] It was the old doctrine, stoutly argued, and the audience, which had perhaps come to see a celebrity, found itself, somewhat to its surprise, listening to a philosopher.

Afterwards we went round behind. There is always a crowd behind, and this one consisted very largely of young women, eager young women, earnest young women, young women with programmes and young women with fountain pens, all pressing in upon the great man in the narrow passage. A host, a brisk and capable person evidently practised in this sort of navigation, took him by the arm. "This way, this way," he said, very nicely, very firmly. And through the press of admirers, as a tug brings a liner through the small craft of the harbour, he steered the enormous figure, gently but without pause, past the programmes, past the fountain pens, into the dressing room; and the door shut. We followed, having, as it happened, some slight claim to be admitted.

## Draft B

I went to hear G. K. Chesterton lecture when he came to America, half expecting to be disappointed. The critics had been at him for some time. They said he had repeated himself, that the paradoxes had worn thin, that the great man had shrunk into a habit. I had read enough of them to arrive a little wary, the way one goes to see a famous building after being told it is smaller than the postcards.

He was not smaller than the postcards. Whatever the critics had taken away from him, they had not managed to take any of the size. He filled the platform the way weather fills a sky, and when he moved it seemed less a man crossing a stage than a landscape shifting its position. I found I had stopped thinking about the reviews within a minute of his arrival.

The talk itself was an argument against fatalism. He would not have it that men are simply one more product of nature, pushed along by the same forces that push the tides and the seasons. Man, he said, stands apart. He is the one creature that looks at nature from the outside, judges it, and refuses it when he likes. The rest of the world goes where it is driven; man chooses, and the choosing is the whole point of him. He said it with a good deal of laughter, his own included, but underneath the jokes the position was perfectly serious and perfectly firm. [example needed: a line or image from the lecture]

Afterward I was taken backstage. Our host led the way, and the way was not clear. A cluster of eager young women had gathered near the door, the sort who come to lectures as much to be near the lecturer as to hear him, and they closed in the moment he appeared. The host dealt with them with the practised calm of a man steering a large ship through small boats. He murmured, he smiled, he kept moving, and he brought Chesterton through them and into a dressing room without once appearing to hurry.

Then the door shut, the noise fell away, and there he was, still enormous in a room built for ordinary people. [example needed: what was said in the dressing room]

## Draft C

It has become something of a fashion among the critics to announce that Mr. Chesterton has diminished. He has repeated himself too frequently, they explain; the paradox has worn threadbare, the conjuring trick is understood. Very well. I went to hear him lecture, and I am obliged to report that he does not appear diminished. He appears, if anything, considerably larger than the critics have ever been. He occupies the platform somewhat as a thunderhead occupies a summer afternoon. One suspected that the lectern had been provided principally so that he might have something to lean upon, and that the lectern was not altogether confident of the arrangement. [example needed: where and when the lecture was given]

His subject, as nearly as I could make it out, was a quarrel with fatalism. There is a notion abroad, he said in effect, that humanity is merely one more item in nature, carried along like a leaf upon a stream, and that it had better make its peace with the current. Mr. Chesterton will have none of it. Man, he insisted, stands apart from nature; he is the solitary creature in it who regards the stream and demands to know where it is going. The cow does not argue with the pasture. I do not know that the audience was converted, but it was certainly entertained, and perhaps entertainment is the beginning of conversion. He laughed a good deal at his own jokes, and so did everybody else.

Afterwards I made my way round to the back of the hall. There the difficulty was not to discover Mr. Chesterton, whom it would be impossible to overlook, but to approach him. Between him and the remainder of the world stood a considerable company of eager young women, each of whom, I gathered, had something of the first importance to communicate. I don't know what it was; I never got near enough to hear. Presently a host appeared, a quiet and capable gentleman, and took the celebrated visitor in charge, much as a tugboat takes charge of an ocean liner. Gently, firmly, with a word here and a smile there, he navigated him through the assembly and into a dressing room. The door closed upon them, and the young women looked at one another. ## Draft D

There has been, of late, a considerable amount of talk to the effect that G. K. Chesterton is not what he was. The critics have taken his measurements and pronounced him diminished. We went to hear him lecture with this solemn verdict in mind, half expecting to find a smaller man. We did not. Whatever the critics may have accomplished against his reputation, they have accomplished nothing whatever against his person, which still seems enormous. He fills the platform as a ship fills a dock; he moves about it, when he moves at all, with the leisurely deliberation of something which understands that it cannot be hurried and sees no particular reason why it should be. The voice, too, is large, though it is not loud. It comes out of him easily, somewhat breathlessly, and appears to be enjoying itself enormously. And the audience, which had come partly to discover whether the critics were right, very soon forgot the critics altogether.

His subject was fatalism, and he was against it. The fashionable doctrine that man is merely one more product of nature, pushed about by invisible forces he can neither understand nor resist, he would have none of. Man, he held, stands apart from nature. He is in it, but he is not of it in the manner in which the tree and the beast are of it. He can contemplate it, pass judgment upon it, and (what is more to the point) refuse it. This was argued with a generous allowance of paradox and a generous allowance of laughter, some of it his own. [example needed: a line from the talk] It is not easy to reconstruct afterward exactly how the argument proceeded; it is entirely easy to report that one came away persuaded, for the evening at any rate.

Afterward we went round behind. In the passage leading to the stage door there was a formidable congregation of young women, extremely eager, some carrying books and some carrying nothing but expectation. Our host, who evidently had experience of these occasions, took the great man by the elbow, as it were, and navigated him through them, very much as one might pilot a liner through a flotilla of small boats. The young women gave way reluctantly, and closed in again behind. Then a door opened, we were in a dressing room, and the door was shut upon the flotilla. It was, one felt, the only room in the building sufficiently spacious for the purpose.
