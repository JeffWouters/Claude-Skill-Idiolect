# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-02

## The passage (by the author)

In these days some of the best people I know are in this predicament in regard to their moral and social affairs. These friends of mine have this peculiarity, that they are anxious to do their duty. Now, in all generations, there have been persons who did their duty, according to their lights. But in these days it happens that a new set of lights has been turned on suddenly, and we all see more duties than we had bargained for. In the glare we see an army of creditors, each with an overdue bill in hand. Each demands immediate payment, and shakes his head when we suggest that he call again next week. We realize that our moral cash in hand is not sufficient for the crisis. If all our obligations must be met at once, there will be a panic in which most of our securities will be sacrificed.

We are accustomed to grumble over the increase in the cost of living. But the enhancement of price in the necessities of physical life is nothing compared to the increase in the cost of the higher life.

There are those now living who can remember when almost any one could have the satisfaction of being considered a good citizen and neighbor. All one had to do was to attend to one's own affairs and keep within the law. He would then be respected by all, and would deserve the most eulogistic epitaph when he came to die. By working for private profit he could have the satisfaction of knowing that all sorts of public benefits came as by-products of his activity.

But now all such satisfactions are denied. To be a good citizen you must put your mind on the job, and it is no easy one. You must be up and doing. And when you are doing one good thing there will be keen-eyed critics who will ask why you have not been doing other things which are much more important; and they will sternly demand of you, "What do you mean by such criminal negligence?"

## Draft A

It is not that we have grown wicked. It is rather that we have grown conscientious, and that the conscientious person now finds himself in the position of a man whose debts have all fallen due on the same morning. He does not deny any of them. He acknowledges each obligation as it is presented, and he would honestly like to discharge it. But he has not the means to settle them all at once, and the creditors are not disposed to wait.

The duties themselves are not new in the sense of being invented yesterday. What is new is that we see them. A good many of them existed before, but they lay comfortably out of sight, and a man who could not perceive them could hardly be blamed for neglecting them. Now there is no such shelter, for every morning brings intelligence of some wrong that he might help to right, some cause that has a claim upon his time and his purse, some neighbor near or distant whose welfare has somehow become his particular responsibility.

The result is that ethical living has become an expensive business. We used to hear a great deal about the high cost of the necessities of life. These are dear enough, but they are within the reach of a prudent man. The moral necessities are dearer. A person may maintain a roof over his head on a modest income; he will find it considerably harder to maintain a clear conscience on one.

There was a time when the terms were easier. If a man obeyed the law, paid what he owed and minded his own business, he was accounted a good citizen, and nobody thought of asking more of him. He was treated with respect. That was the whole of the bargain.

It is not so to-day. The critics of our society do not inquire what a man has done; they inquire what he has left undone, and whatever he has neglected, through ignorance or indolence or mere preoccupation with his own affairs, is laid to his charge. Minding one's own business, which was once a civic virtue, is now very nearly the chief of the vices. The good citizen of the old dispensation has become the respectable offender of the new.

## Draft B

The trouble with the conscientious person of to-day is not that he has lost his sense of duty, but that he has found too many duties. They did not come upon him one at a time, as duties used to do. They have all come at once. He is in the position of a man whose debts, contracted at various times and in various places, have all fallen due on the same morning. He does not doubt that he owes them. What troubles him is that he cannot see how to pay.

It is commonly said that the cost of living has gone up. I should not care to dispute it. But I am persuaded that the cost of right living has gone up a good deal faster. A man may still manage to provide himself with food and shelter and decent clothing. It is when he tries to provide himself with a good conscience that he finds the prices beyond him. The ethical necessities have become more expensive than the material ones, and there is no bargain counter where they can be had at a reduction.

There was a time when it was not so hard to be a good citizen. If a man obeyed the law and minded his own business, he was thought to have done very well. His neighbors respected him. He paid his taxes and went to bed with an easy mind.

Ah, but minding one's own business is no longer accounted a virtue. It is the very thing that is held against us. For now our business is everybody's business, and everybody's business is ours. The critics are abroad, and they are not interested in what we have done. They want to know what we have left undone. Whatever we have neglected, and there is always something, is laid at our door. We are blamed not for our sins but for our omissions, and the list of omissions has no end.

I would not be too hard on the critics. They are, for the most part, right. Only I would have them remember that a man who is asked to pay all his debts at once may be forgiven if he pays some of them late.

## Draft C

Being a good citizen used to be a fairly modest undertaking. You obeyed the law, paid what you owed, and minded your own business. Do that and your neighbours thought well of you. Nobody expected you to have opinions about everything, still less to act on all of them. Respectability was within reach of anyone willing to keep their head down and their accounts in order.

That arrangement has quietly come to an end. The conscientious person today looks around and finds a whole crowd of new duties waiting. There are obligations to [example needed], to [example needed], to causes and communities that previous generations never had to think about. Each one on its own is reasonable. Most of them are probably right. The trouble is that they have arrived all at once, like a pile of debts that happen to fall due in the same week. You could settle any one of them. You cannot settle all of them, and every one you leave unpaid is noted.

What strikes me most is the cost. We talk a great deal about how expensive it has become to live: the price of housing, of food, of the plain necessities. But I suspect ethical living has grown costlier still. Being good now takes time, attention, money and a kind of constant vigilance that the old model of citizenship never demanded. The material necessities at least have a price you can look up. The moral ones keep expanding, and there is no receipt that says you have done enough.

And that is where the change really bites. Under the old rules, you were judged on what you did. If you kept the law and did no harm, you had passed. Under the new rules you are judged on what you neglect. There is always a critic ready to point out the duty you overlooked, the cause you did not support, the obligation you failed to see. The standard is no longer whether you behaved well but whether you did everything, and nobody does everything.

I do not think the new duties are false. Many of them were overdue. But we should be honest that we have made good citizenship much harder than it used to be, and that the people most likely to feel the weight are the conscientious ones who take it seriously.

## Draft D

It is not easy to be a good citizen in these days. Time was when a man who obeyed the laws, paid his taxes and minded his own business could hold up his head among his neighbors and expect their respect without further question. Nobody demanded more of him. If he did not set the world right, neither did he add to its disorder, and that was considered a creditable showing. He went to bed with a comfortable conscience and slept until morning.

That easy standard has passed. The conscientious person to-day finds himself confronted, not by one new duty, but by a whole procession of them, each insisting upon immediate attention and each supported by arguments he is unable to answer. He has scarcely acknowledged his obligation in one direction before he is reminded of three or four others of which he had never heard. It is as if all his debts had fallen due on the same day; he contemplates the accumulation of notes on his desk and wonders whether he is solvent. Each claim, perhaps, is just. It is their simultaneous arrival that is so disconcerting.

The consequence is that the moral life has become an expensive establishment. We used to reckon that the necessities of existence were food, clothing and shelter, and that a man who had provided these for his household had discharged his principal responsibilities. But the cost of living ethically has risen faster than the cost of living at all. A person of moderate means can still keep himself fed and housed. It is when he attempts to keep himself virtuous, according to the enlarged requirements of his contemporaries, that he discovers his income to be inadequate.

Nor is it sufficient to do nothing wrong. The modern critic takes little interest in the things you have done; he is concerned with what you have left undone. Whatever evil exists in the community, he will point out, exists in part because you did not prevent it. You are blamed for your neglect. And since there is no end to the things a man can neglect, there is no end to the blame.

I do not say the critic is mistaken. I only observe that good citizenship, which once was within the reach of any honest person, has become a strenuous profession.
