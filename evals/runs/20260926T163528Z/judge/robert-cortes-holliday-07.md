# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-07

## The passage (by the author)

And where, Oh, where! (not even in London) can one find so many alleys to the square inch? Many of them, lanes of but a few blocks in length, highly respectable, even aristocratic, quarters of the town. Such as Camac Street, tucked away between Thirteenth and Twelfth Streets, one block of it either side of Locust, and the home or haunt of those of artistic persuasion. Here the famous Franklin Inn Club, the charming Poor Richard Club, and divers other clubs of kindred spirit. Unusual this quaint street of art in this: in fixing it up for its present purpose its quaintness and its "artiness" have not been overdone. Far, far finer in effect than New York's over eccentric alley of painters, Washington Mews, its original loveliness has simply been restored. It is as jolly to look upon as London's artist nook, Cheyne Row. Perhaps even jollier.

Now another unusual thing about Philadelphia is that Philadelphians standing within three blocks of the place can't tell you where South Carlisle Street is. Professional Philadelphians, such as policemen, firemen, postmen, street car men, can't do it. In the attempt they contradict each other, and quarrel among themselves. For the benefit of both Philadelphians and visitors to the city I will set down here exactly the location of South Carlisle Street. West of Broad, south of Pine, it runs one block from Pine to Lombard Street. After a jump, where there isn't any of it, north of Market Street there is more of it.

But what the dickens is South Carlisle Street, and why should anybody care where it is? Well, though it isn't in the books on Historic Shrines of America it is a street you "hadn't ought to" miss. It's about twelve feet (or something like that) from wall to wall. The doorways seem to be about three feet wide. There, in South Carlisle Street, Philadelphia's mahogany doors, fan-lights above, white pillars before, marble steps below, her immaculate red brick, her freshly painted wooden shutters, her gleaming brass knockers are in their most exquisite perfection.

## Draft A

Philadelphia keeps some of its best things off the main streets. The broad avenues are there for anyone to see, but the character of the city lives in the small streets that run between them, the ones a visitor can walk past a dozen times without noticing.

Camac Street is the best known of these, and it has earned its reputation. It is an artists' lane, restored with real taste, which is rarer than it sounds; restoration so often means a coat of paint and a brass plaque. Here the work has been done with some respect for what was there. The clubs that have settled along it give the street a life of its own, and it manages to feel lived in rather than preserved.

South Carlisle Street is another matter. Even people who have spent their whole lives in Philadelphia cannot find it, and most of them, asked for directions, will tell you with some confidence that it does not exist. It does. It lies [location needed: where South Carlisle Street is], and once you know where to look it is easy enough to reach, though the knowing is the hard part.

What you find when you get there is a street so narrow that it seems less like a thoroughfare than a gap the builders forgot to close. The houses face one another at a distance you could almost reach across. Because there is so little of it, the eye has nothing to do but look closely, and what it sees is Philadelphia in miniature: the classic doorways, the old brick, the shutters folded back against the walls, the knockers waiting on the doors. These are the things the city is known for, and on a wider street they would be lost among traffic and signs. Here they are the whole view.

It is worth the trouble of finding. A street that even the natives cannot place has a way of feeling like a private discovery, and South Carlisle Street, narrow and quiet and unmistakably Philadelphian, rewards the person who takes the trouble to look it up.

## Draft C

Philadelphia keeps much of its charm tucked away where the visitor, hurrying along the broad avenues with his guidebook, will hardly stumble upon it. The little side streets are what is meant. They are not advertised; they are scarcely even acknowledged; and the Philadelphian himself, questioned about them, looks at you with mild surprise, like a man asked after some distant and uninteresting relative.

Camac Street is the best known of them, and it deserves to be. It has been restored, and restored (which is by no means always the case) with admirable taste. The artists have discovered it, and have settled there, and have founded their clubs there, and the result is a lane which looks as a lane in an old city ought to look and so seldom does. [example needed: the names of the clubs] One walks down it with the pleasant sensation of having wandered, so to say, into a picture.

South Carlisle Street is another matter. It is not so much hidden as mislaid. Philadelphians born and bred, living within easy walking distance of it, could not find it with a map. For the benefit of the curious, then, it lies [location needed: where South Carlisle Street runs]. Having found it, you will perhaps wonder how anybody could miss it; having looked away for a moment, you will perhaps understand.

It is extraordinarily narrow. Two persons of generous proportions, meeting in the middle of it, would have to come to some arrangement. But in this narrow compass the whole character of the old city is gathered up and displayed, as it were, for inspection. Here are the classic Philadelphia doorways, with their modest dignity. Here is the brick, mellowed to the colour that only brick and time together can manage. Here are the shutters, and here, polished or neglected as the case may be, are the knockers. Nothing is grand. Everything is right. And one comes out again into the traffic of the broad street feeling that one has been, for a few minutes, a guest in a house where nobody thought to mention that visitors were expected.

## Draft D

Philadelphia keeps a great many of her treasures in her side streets. The broad avenues are for the visitor; the little lanes are for the person who has time. And the person who has time will do well to begin with Camac Street. Camac Street is the artists' lane. It has been restored, and restored, which is not always the case, with taste. The old houses stand as they stood, only rather cleaner, and within some of them are clubs, where artists and those who like to be near artists gather in the evening. It is quaint, but not too quaint; it has been improved, but not improved away.

And then there is South Carlisle Street. I have made some inquiries about South Carlisle Street, and the chief thing to be learned about it is that nobody can find it. Ask a policeman, and he will direct you, with great confidence, to somewhere else. Ask a lifelong resident of the town, a man who was born and bred within a mile of it, and he will think a moment and tell you that there is no such street. There is. It lies [location needed]. I set this down plainly, for the benefit of the natives.

It is a very narrow street. It is so narrow that one feels it would be a kind of rudeness to walk down the middle of it, and so narrow that a person standing upon one doorstep could, with no great effort, hand a cup of sugar to a person standing upon the doorstep opposite. But in that small space is gathered, as in a little museum, everything that the city does best. There are the doorways, those classic Philadelphia doorways, with their white steps and their fanlights. There is the brick, the old red brick, mellowed by the years to the colour of a good apple. There are the shutters, folded back. And there are the knockers, brass knockers, iron knockers, knockers in the shape of this and of that, each one polished and each one, so to say, waiting. The whole city, one might say, is here in small.
