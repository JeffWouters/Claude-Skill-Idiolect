# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-07

## The passage (by the author)

But in the use of this effective method of reiteration there is a difference between nature and a book. Nature does not care whether she bores us or not: she has us by the buttonhole, and we cannot get away. Not so with a book. When we are bored, we lay it down, and that brings the interview to an end. It is from the fear of our impatience that most writers abstain from the natural method of producing an impression.

And they are quite right. It is only now and then that an audience will grant an extension of time to a speaker in order that he may make his point more clear. They would rather miss the point. And it is still more rare for the reader to grant a similar extension in order that the author may tell again what he has told before. It is much easier to shut up a book than to shut up a speaker.

The criticism of Dickens that his characters repeat themselves quite misses the mark. As well object to an actor that he frequently responds to an encore. If indicted for the offense, he could at least insist that the audience be indicted with him as accessory before the fact.

Dickens tells us that when he read at Harrogate, "There was a remarkably good fellow of thirty or so who found something so very ludicrous in Toots that he could not compose himself at all, but laughed until he sat wiping his eyes with his handkerchief, and whenever he felt Toots coming again he began to laugh and wipe his eyes afresh."

"Whenever he felt Toots coming again"—there you have the whole philosophy of the matter. The young fellow found Toots amusing when he first laid eyes on him. He wanted to see him again, and it must always be the same Toots.

## Draft A

Nature repeats herself without apology. The sun rises every morning in very much the manner of the morning before, and the seasons return in their accustomed order, and nobody thinks of complaining that the performance lacks originality. The reason is that we cannot get away, for there is no other sun to go to, and a man who is weary of the spring must wait for it all the same. The writer enjoys no such advantage. His audience is at liberty, and a reader who finds that he has heard a thing before has only to close the book. It is this independence of the reader, rather than any law of art, that makes the writer so anxious never to say the same thing twice.

Yet I think the anxiety is often misplaced. The critics are fond of reproaching a novelist because his characters repeat themselves; the comic figure, they complain, is forever making the same joke and falling into the same absurdity, and they take this for a symptom of poverty in the author. I am not sure that the public agrees with them. What the public requires of an old friend is not that he should surprise it, but that he should come back exactly as he went away.

## Draft B

Nature is not afraid of repeating herself. She brings round the same seasons in the same order, and the sun rises every morning with no attempt at novelty. Nobody accuses her of poverty of invention. The reason is simple enough: we cannot get away from her. The writer enjoys no such security. His audience is free to leave at any moment, and it takes only a very slight motion of the hand to close a book. So he lives in dread of saying the same thing twice, and his critics encourage the dread.

There is a kind of criticism which finds fault with a novelist because his characters repeat themselves. The comic person, it is observed, has one way of speaking, and he speaks in that way whenever he appears; he learns nothing, he changes nothing, and he goes out of the story as he came in. This is set down as a defect of art. I think the critic mistakes what the audience wants. We do not wish our old acquaintances to improve. When a familiar figure comes back upon the scene, the pleasure lies precisely in finding him unaltered, and in hearing him say the thing which we knew he was going to say before he opened his mouth.

## Draft C

Nature repeats herself without the least embarrassment. The sun rises every morning in the same quarter, the robin sings the same song he sang last year, and the tide comes in and goes out with a monotony that would be intolerable in anything less majestic. Nobody complains. The reason is not that we admire repetition, but that we cannot get away from it. Nature has us for the duration. We may grumble at the weather, but we cannot close the sky and put it back on the shelf.

The writer is in no such fortunate position. His audience is held by the slenderest of threads. The reader can at any moment shut the book and go about his business, and the writer knows it. So he lives in a perpetual terror of saying the same thing twice. He hunts for synonyms as a man hunts for a lost collar-button, and he would rather be obscure than be caught in a repetition.

It is this terror which has made the critics severe upon [novelist], whose characters, they say, repeat themselves. A certain person has only to open his mouth and we know what he will say, for he has said it before. The criticism has a professional sound, but I think it is misplaced. ## Draft D

Nature repeats herself without the least embarrassment. The sun comes up in the same quarter every morning, the tide goes out and comes in again, spring arrives each year with very much the same flowers it brought the year before, and no one writes to complain. Actors do it too. A comedian may say the same line in the same way every night for a season, and the audience that has come to hear it laughs as if it were new, and would be annoyed if it were changed.

The writer is not allowed this freedom, and I think I see why. We cannot get away from nature. We are stuck with the sunrise whether we care for it or not, and so we have made our peace with its sameness. The reader of a book is under no such compulsion. If he finds that he has read a thing before, he can shut the covers and go and do something else, and the writer, knowing this, goes in dread of saying anything twice.

It seems to me that this dread is carried too far, particularly by the critics. A charge often brought against a novelist is that his characters repeat themselves, that his comic people say the same sort of thing every time they come into the room. I think this misreads what readers want. Nobody wishes a comic figure to be improved. We want him to come in, say his piece and be exactly the man he was before, and the pleasure lies in the recognition.

I once saw this proved at a public reading. [example needed: the reader and the book] When a certain familiar comic figure came back into the story and said once again the thing he always said, one listener in the audience could not contain himself, and his delight was louder and warmer than at anything new in the whole evening. He had not come to be surprised. He had come to meet an old friend, and the old friend had not let him down.
