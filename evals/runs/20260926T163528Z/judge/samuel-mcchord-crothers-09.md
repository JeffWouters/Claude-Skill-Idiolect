# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-09

## The passage (by the author)

The author says: "It is believed that this story contains no indecorous stimulants, nor is it filled with inexplicated incidents imperceptible to the understanding. When anxieties have been excited by involved and doubtful events, they are afterwards elucidated by their consequences. In this the writer believes that he has generally copied Nature."

I have a feeling that those inexplicated incidents in your novel might have been elucidated by their consequences if you had chosen a person whose actions were of the kind to have some important consequences. In tying up to an inconsequential person you lost that chance.

I don't mean to discourage you, because I believe you have it in you to make a novel that would be as interesting as half the biographies that are written. But you must learn a trick from the successful biographers, and not invest in second-rate realities. The best is none too good. You have to exercise judgment in your initial investment.

Now, if I were going to build a realistic novel, and had as much skill in detail as you have, and as much intellectual capital to invest, I would go right down to the business centre, so to speak, and invest in a really valuable piece of reality; and then I would develop it. The first investment might seem pretty steep, but it would pay in the end. If you could get a big man, enthusiastic over a big cause, in conflict with big forces, and bring in a lot of worth-while people to back him up, and then bring the whole thing to some big conclusion, you would have a novel that would be as real as the biographies I have been reading, and as interesting. I think it would be worth trying.

Respectfully yours,

R.S. LANDMANN.

P.S. If you don't feel that you can afford to make such a heavy investment as I have suggested, why don't you put your material into a short story?

## Draft A

I have read your novel with the attention it deserves and with more patience than you suppose. You ask me what is wrong with it. The events are loose, and they remain trivial to the end, not because nothing happens in them but because nothing that happens matters very much to anybody, least of all to the reader who is asked to follow them through chapter after chapter. Your hero is an amiable young man who might have lived next door to any of us for a lifetime without our ever having found it necessary to learn his name.

I am not one of those who would banish the ordinary from realistic fiction. But the ordinary must happen to somebody who counts. The good biographies understand this without being told. Nobody reads the life of a great man for the sake of his breakfasts, and yet his breakfasts become interesting because they are his and because we know what business awaited him when he rose from the table. Small things take their importance from the large thing they lead up to.

My advice, then, is that you choose a major figure and set him to fighting for a great cause. I would have you treat that choice as a prudent man treats a worthwhile investment. You are about to put a considerable portion of your life into this hero, and you have every right to expect some return on the capital. It is poor economy to lavish your best observation on a character who can pay no dividend. Choose a man whose defeats would be losses to the world and whose victories would be worth the reader's while to share; then the trivial details will earn their keep along with the rest.

If this cannot be done, and I grant that the material may refuse it, there remains an honourable way out, which is to turn the whole thing into a short story. A small hero is quite at home in a small space. What wearies us through a long book will often charm us in a few pages. Not every acquaintance can be invited to stay the week.

## Draft B

My dear friend, I have read your novel through, and I would not have you think that I read it grudgingly. There were pages I enjoyed. But when I laid it down I found I could not remember what had happened, and on reflection I am not sure that anything had. Your events were loose, and loose events are not in themselves a fault. Life is made of them. The trouble was that they happened to a man who did not greatly matter, and so they did not greatly matter either. A trivial hero has a way of making everything about him trivial. He drops his hat, and it is only a hat.

Now consider how the good biographer goes about his work. He has no more power than you to invent a plot. He must take the days as they came, with all their errands and dinners and small misunderstandings. Yet we read him with a kind of breathless interest, because the man whose days they were was doing something large. The dinner is not merely a dinner; it is the evening before a great decision. The errand is part of a campaign. The importance of the man runs backward into every detail and lends it a borrowed dignity.

I would have you take a leaf out of the biographer's book. Choose for your hero a person of consequence, one who is fighting for a great cause, and let the little happenings of realistic fiction gather about him. I do not say this is easy. It is rather like choosing an investment. You must put a good deal of capital into your man before he will pay you anything, and you must be reasonably sure he is sound. But if you choose well, the returns come in on every page, and the reader is glad to follow him even to the barber's.

And if, after looking about you, you find no such figure in your material, I would not have you despair. There is an honourable alternative. Cut the thing down to a short story. What will not bear the weight of a life will very often carry an afternoon.

## Draft C

Dear [name needed],

I have read your novel through twice now, and I owe you a plain answer rather than a polite one. The trouble is not your sentences, which are often fine, nor your eye for the ordinary business of life, which is sharp. The trouble is your hero. He does not matter enough, and because he does not matter, nothing that happens to him matters either.

You have given us a string of loose events: a quarrel here, a journey there, a disappointment, a small recovery. In a realistic novel that is exactly what life looks like, and you are right to refuse the tidy plot. But loose events only hold together when they happen to someone whose fate we care about. Think of how a good biography works. Its incidents are often trivial in themselves, a dinner, a letter, a bad week, yet we read every page because the person at the centre is important. The weight of the life lends weight to each detail. Your book has the details without the weight, and so they stay trivial.

My advice, then, is to begin again with a larger figure. Choose a protagonist who counts for something in the world, and set him fighting for a cause worth the fight. I would urge you to treat that choice the way a careful person treats an investment. You are about to spend a year or more of your working life on this book. Put that time into a subject that will repay it, one whose stakes will carry the reader through the quiet chapters as well as the loud ones. A great cause gives even the smallest scene a reason to be there.

If you cannot find such a figure, or if this particular material will not bend to one, then do not stretch it. Cut it down to a short story. What reads thin across three hundred pages can be sharp and complete across twenty, and there is no shame in that form. There is only shame in a long book about a man nobody needed to read about.

With every good wish for the next draft,

## Draft D

My dear friend,

I have read your novel with the attention it deserves, and I must tell you frankly what I think is the matter with it. The events are loose enough, but that is not the trouble. The trouble is that nothing which happens to your hero seems to matter, because your hero himself matters so little that the reader cannot be persuaded to take his fortunes more seriously than he would take the fortunes of a stranger overheard in a railway carriage. I followed him with a mild good will that never rose to anxiety. A small man may have large misfortunes; we do not believe in them.

Consider how the good biographies manage this. The biographer of a great man has no need to invent significance, for his hero is fighting for something that was worth fighting for, and every trivial incident takes on the color of the cause. We read with interest what he had for breakfast on the morning of the battle. Without the battle we should not read it at all.

What I would urge upon you is that you choose your protagonist as a prudent man chooses an investment. You are about to put a considerable part of your life into him, and you had better see that he is the kind of security which will pay a return. Take a major figure. Let him be one who is fighting for a great cause and who has something to lose besides his own comfort, so that your realism will have something substantial to be realistic about. The small details of his life will then not be small, since they will be the details of a life that counted.

If you cannot find such a man, or cannot quite bring yourself to believe in him, there remains an honorable way out. Turn your material into a short story. A short story may be about a little man, since it does not ask the reader to stay with him long enough to discover how little he is. It is a good deal kinder to both of you.

Believe me, with all good wishes for the next book,

Yours faithfully,
