# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-04

## The passage (by the author)

Part of the code of honor begins only when the Christian precept has been broken. Is it so bad a thing, in a fallible world, to be told what to do after you have once done something wrong? The Catechism, as a practical guide, is wofully incomplete without the code of the gentleman as an appendix. If you had sinned, the Puritan told you to repent; and he was quite right. But there is work left for the sinner after the repenting has been done. Both Honor and the Catechism will do their best to keep you out of a mess. The difference comes later: for after you have got into a mess, the Catechism leaves you to God, while Honor shows you how, if you have done ill to fellow beings, to repair that ill and not extend it.

Honor is a matter of practical politics—frightfully unpractical politics, in another sense, they often are. A cynical young woman once said to me that she found cads more interesting than gentlemen, because you could always tell what a gentleman would do in a given situation, whereas you could never tell, in any situation, what a cad would do. Cads may or may not be the proper sport of cynical young women; but to the average busy creature the gentleman is wholly delightful in that he is wholly predicable. The Christian is not predicable, for the simple reason that he has been given a counsel of perfection. You know that any given Christian will, by the day of his majority, have done some, at least, of the things which the Catechism has expressly warned him not to do. "The way that can be walked upon is not the perfect way," said Laotse long ago. The Church does not believe that you have always done everything that your sponsors in baptism so cheerfully said you would do. The confessional is itself the greatest confession that the Church has ever made. One of the most convenient things about Honor is that its explicit code is limited; and you can say of some men when they die that they have never for a moment ceased to be gentlemen. Honor is of the world, worldly—and some people have distorted that magnificent fact into an accusation. That is what Mr. Kipling has done in "Tomlinson."

## Draft A

It is often said, by the more earnest sort of people, that the gentleman's code of honour is a poor, worldly substitute for religion. I do not think it is a substitute at all. The two do different work, and a man who has only one of them is, in some respect, ill equipped. Religion tells him what to do when he has sinned: he is to repent, to confess, to amend his heart. Honour tells him what to do when he has wronged his neighbour; he is to put the thing right. The one is concerned with the state of his soul, the other with the damage he has done to some one outside it; and the damage, it must be admitted, is not repaired by any amount of contrition, however heartfelt, however long drawn out. A man may be sincerely sorry for a debt and still leave it unpaid.

There is another difference, and it goes deeper. The Christian ideal asks for nothing less than perfection, and no one attains it; very few, I venture to say, seriously expect to, though they would be shocked to hear it put so plainly. The consequence is odd, but it is plain enough to any one who has watched believers closely. Since every man falls short of the standard, and none can say beforehand where he will fall short, the believer becomes curiously unpredictable—capable of great sacrifice on Monday and of a mean little evasion on Tuesday. You never quite know where you are with him. Honour asks a great deal less. Its rules are few, they are explicit, and they are well within the reach of an ordinary man: [example needed: the rules of the code]. Because they can be kept, they are kept; and because they are kept, the gentleman can be relied upon. It is a code a man may follow from youth to old age without once being told that he has failed by definition.

Some one will say that all this is very worldly. Granted: it is. But I cannot see that worldliness is a fault in a rule which was never meant for heaven, and which has to be worked, day in and day out, among ordinary people in the world. Honour does not pretend to save anybody; it pretends only to make him fit to live with. That is a modest aim. It is also, I fancy, a rarer achievement than we suppose.

## Draft B

Religion and honour are often set up against one another, as if a man must choose between the altar and the club. I cannot see why. They seem to me to be doing different work, and doing it, on the whole, very comfortably side by side.

Consider what each has to say when a man has done wrong. Religion tells him to repent: to be sorry, to confess, to amend his heart. That is excellent advice, and I should not dream of quarrelling with it. But it is chiefly concerned with the state of his own soul. Honour asks a blunter question. What have you done to the other fellow, and what are you going to do about it? It is not content that the sinner should feel badly; it wants the damage made good. The apology must be offered, the debt paid. Religion looks after the man; honour looks after the man he has injured. One would be sorry to do without either.

There is a further difference, and it is here, I think, that honour earns its keep. The Christian ideal asks for perfection. It asks us to be pure in thought as well as in deed—and nobody, so far as I know, has ever managed it. The consequence is a curious one. Because the believer is always reaching for more than he can grasp, one never quite knows where he will stop. He may rise to heroism; he may, the next day, fall a long way short of plain decency, and comfort himself that all have sinned. He is, in short, unpredictable. (I do not say this unkindly; it is the price of a very high aim.)

The gentleman is another matter. His code is limited, and it is written down—or as good as written down. He pays his debts; he keeps his word; [example needed]. The list is not long. But precisely because it is not long, he can keep it, and keep it all his life. You may rely on him. You know what he will not do.

It is sometimes objected that honour is a worldly thing—that it concerns itself with reputation and with what the neighbours will say. So it does. I cannot think that a fault. We live in the world, after all, and the world must be got on with; a rule that works among men is not the worse for being a rule of this earth rather than of heaven.

## Draft C

It is common to treat a gentleman's code of honor as a secular rival to religion, or at best a pale worldly copy of it. I think that is a misunderstanding. The two do different work, and a man is better equipped with both than with either alone.

Take the matter of wrongdoing. Religion has a clear answer to what a man should do after he has done wrong: he should repent. He should acknowledge his sin, feel sorrow for it and seek forgiveness. That is the right answer to one question, the question of the state of his own soul. But it leaves another question largely open, and that is what he owes the person he has harmed. Honor speaks directly to this. It tells a man that when he has injured another he must set it right: apologise, make restitution, repair what he broke. Repentance looks inward and upward; honor looks outward, at the damage done to others and at the debt that remains.

There is a second difference, less often noticed. Christian ideals demand perfection. They ask a man to be without fault in every part of his life. No one attains this, and everyone who takes it seriously knows it. The odd consequence is that believers are unpredictable. Since each falls short of an impossible standard, each falls short in his own way and at his own points, and you cannot easily tell in advance where a given man will hold firm and where he will give way.

Honor works differently. Its rules are limited and explicit. A gentleman knows exactly what is required of him [example needed]. The list is short and the lines are clear. Because the demands are within reach, a man can actually keep them, and keep them for his whole life. That makes him reliable. You know what a gentleman will and will not do, and you can build trust, friendships and business on that knowledge.

Critics sometimes say that honor is too worldly: that it concerns reputation, conduct among men, the ordinary dealings of society, rather than the higher things. But that worldliness is a strength, not a fault. It is exactly what allows honor to complement religion rather than compete with it. Religion keeps the ideal in view; honor gives a man a floor he will not fall below. Between the two, he has both something to aspire to and something he can be counted on to do.

## Draft D

It is often said, rather loftily, that the code of a gentleman is a poor worldly substitute for religion. I do not think it is a substitute at all; I think it does a different work, and does it better than religion has ever pretended to. Religion tells a man what to do after he has sinned. He is to repent, to be sorry before God, to amend his heart. That is well enough, so far as his own soul is concerned. But it says very little about the man he has injured, who is still injured, however contrite the sinner may feel. Honor has something to say about him. It tells the gentleman, quite plainly, that he must go to the man he has wronged and make good the harm—and that his private remorse is his own affair.

There is another difference, and it goes deeper. The Christian ideal asks for perfection; it asks of us a goodness without flaw or limit, and nobody attains it, or nobody I have met. The consequence is curious. Since no one can live up to the whole of the ideal, every believer must choose, without saying so, which part of it he will neglect; and one can never tell beforehand which part he has chosen. He may be heroic on Monday and shabby on Tuesday, and both from the purest motives. I do not say this to mock him. I say only that he is unpredictable, and that one cannot build much upon him.

The gentleman is another matter. His rules are few, and they are written out for any one to read: [example needed: the explicit rules of the code]. These are modest demands, and a man may keep them from his youth to his grave without once pretending to be a saint. That is precisely their virtue. You know what a gentleman will do, because he knows it himself; and a code which can actually be followed for a lifetime is worth a good deal more, in the conduct of affairs, than one which is only admired.

We are told that honor is too worldly. Well, so it is; it was made for the world, and the world is where we have to live.
