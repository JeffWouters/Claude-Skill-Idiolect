# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-10

## The passage (by the author)

But on the shore side of the San Francisco water front, my fancy was much taken by the salt sea savor of the signs of the houses of entertainment—signs reminiscent of the jovial days of briny romance, echoed in the chantey in "Treasure Island," which has as its refrain:

Yo, ho, ho, and a bottle of rum.

I passed, among others, the Marine Café, the Navy Café, the Admiral Café, the Harbor Bar, and the Ferry Café.

I did not turn up Market Street, but went on around the nose of the peninsula, which is the foundation of San Francisco. I passed a three-masted ship, the Lizzie Vance, lying by her wharf, with men aloft in her rigging. Then I clambered up endless relays of rickety wooden stairs mounting Telegraph Hill. On either side of the ladder-like steps, ramshackle cabins bedecked with lines of fluttering "wash." Like the celebrated editor of Puck, H. C. Bunner, I might say that in my travels I've missed many a cathedral but I never missed a slum.

I went along through the Latin Quarter, slid down the steep slope of Kearny Street, and found myself wandering into that quaint little park, Portsmouth Square, where R. L. S. in his most stressful days lounged in the sun and listened to the tales of the vagabonds of the Seven Seas. Somewhat bigger than tiny Gramercy Park, hardly as large as little Madison Square, this park. In the center of the bit of rolling lawn, before a towering screen of rustling trees, the graceful little stone ship, buoyant on its curling stone wave, rides atop its tall stone pedestal graved "To Remember Robert Louis Stevenson," and on the face of which is cut that most fragrant of creeds, which (as everyone knows) begins: "To be honest, to be kind, to earn a little and to spend a little less, to make upon the whole a family happier for his presence" ...

## Draft A

The best way into the old city is to start at the water and walk uphill.

Along the docks the saloons and cafés wear their trade on their signboards. Nearly every one of them carries a name borrowed from the sea, [example needed], as if the owners wanted no sailor coming ashore to feel he had left his ship entirely behind. You pass them one after another, doors open, the smell of the harbour drifting in and out, and you begin to understand what sort of city this was before it was anything else.

A little further on, a sailing ship lies moored at the wharf. It is worth stopping to look at her, rigging and all, because she belongs to the same story as the names over the saloon doors. Then you turn away from the water and start to climb.

Telegraph Hill does not make it easy. The streets give out and become stairways, steep ones, wooden and uneven, that zigzag up the slope between shabby little cabins perched wherever there was room to put one. The cabins lean and sag, and their gardens are whatever manages to grow on a hillside, but the people who live in them have the best views in town, and you will be glad of every pause for breath because each one gives you more of the bay.

Coming down the other side you drift into the Latin Quarter, where the streets narrow and the talk in the doorways is not all in English. [example needed] It is a different city again from the waterfront, and only a few minutes' walk away.

The walk ends, fittingly, in Portsmouth Square. Here stands a monument to Robert Louis Stevenson, and it is shaped like a ship, a small vessel riding on its pedestal as though it had sailed up from the docks you started from. Carved on it is his creed: [quotation needed]. Read it slowly. After a morning among sailors' saloons, a moored ship and a hillside of makeshift homes, it seems the right thing to find at the end of the road.

## Draft B

Down by the docks the saloons and cafés wear the names of the sea. You read them over the doorways as you walk: [example needed: a saloon's seafaring name], and others of that complexion. It is a pleasant thing, I think, that a man who has never been farther out than the end of the pier may take his coffee under the sign of a ship. The water is at your elbow all the way. Presently you come upon a sailing ship lying at her moorings, very quiet, her rigging drawn against the sky like a diagram in an old textbook. You stand and look at her for a while. Then you turn your back upon the waterfront and begin to climb.

Telegraph Hill is not climbed so much as scaled. The stairways go up steeply—some of them, it seems to me, almost perpendicularly—between shabby little cabins that cling to the slope as though they had been thrown there and had stuck. Nobody appears to have any intention of improving anything; and one would not wish it otherwise. Shabbiness of this sort has a character which a new coat of paint would only spoil. When you have recovered your breath at the top, you descend again, somewhat more gratefully, into the Latin Quarter. Here the language of the shop windows changes, and, I fancy, the temperament of the street changes with it.

And so, by way of narrow streets, to Portsmouth Square. It keeps, in a manner perfectly appropriate, its memorial to Robert Louis Stevenson. The monument is in the shape of a ship, riding upon its pedestal as though it had merely put in here for a moment on its way to somewhere else. Upon it is inscribed his creed. I read it through slowly, standing there in the square, with the traffic of the old city going by. It seemed to me, on the whole, exactly the right place for it.

## Draft C

Down by the docks the saloons and cafés have a habit of christening themselves after the sea. One reads their signboards in passing ([example needed: two or three of the seafaring names]), and it is as though the entire waterfront were endeavouring, so to say, to put out from shore. The names are agreeable enough, and they accomplish their business on the imagination.

A little farther along lay a sailing ship, moored. I stopped to consider her. There is something about a vessel of that description, fastened quietly to a wharf, which makes a man feel he has stumbled upon a relic—a creature out of literature rather than out of a shipyard. I contemplated her for some while, and then turned my back on the water and commenced to climb.

Telegraph Hill does not invite you up; it challenges you. The streets surrender, and in their place come stairways, steep and precipitous, ascending among shabby little cabins that cling to the slope as best they can. You climb, and pause, and climb again, and arrive considerably out of breath. It is not a fashionable neighbourhood. It is, I suspect, a good deal more interesting than a fashionable one.

From the summit I descended into the Latin Quarter, and so, by degrees and without any particular hurry, into Portsmouth Square. Here stands the monument to Robert Louis Stevenson. It is constructed in the shape of a ship, and it seemed to me entirely appropriate that it should be. Upon it is inscribed his creed, [quotation needed: the inscription]. I stood before it a while, reading the words over and thinking how well the man who wrote them would have understood the saloons with their borrowed seafaring names and the sailing ship waiting at her moorings below the hill. And there it stood, pointed outward, like the names of the saloons below, toward the sea.

## Draft D

Down along the water front in San Francisco the saloons and cafés have a way of naming themselves after the sea. You pass one that is called for an anchor, another for a ship, another for some port or other at the far end of the world. I have not seen this commented upon before, but it strikes me that a man could very nearly take a voyage merely by reading the signboards. Whether the men inside have ever been to sea is another matter. One suspects that some of them have, and that some of them have only drunk to it.

Just beyond, a sailing ship lay moored at her pier, masts and rigging and all, looking a little like an old gentleman who has come to a party in the clothes of another day. We stood and looked at her for a while. Then we turned our backs on the water and went up.

Telegraph Hill does not invite you; it dares you. The way up is by stairways, steep ones, wooden flights and stone flights, climbing among cabins that are, to put it kindly, shabby—leaning a bit, patched a bit, gardened here and there with a geranium in a can. You go up, and you go up, and you stop to breathe, and you go up again. Then down the other side and through the Latin Quarter, where the talk in the doorways is not in English and the smell of cooking is not American either.

And so to Portsmouth Square. Here, in a little park of the old city, stands a monument to Robert Louis Stevenson. It is in the shape of a ship, a small galleon under full sail, which is just what it ought to be. On it is inscribed his creed. I read it through, standing there with my hat in my hand, and I have thought about it since. How much life it takes to make a little creed like that! And how little room, after all, it needs in the square.
