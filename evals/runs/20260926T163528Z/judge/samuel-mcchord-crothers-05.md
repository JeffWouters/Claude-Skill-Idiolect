# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-05

## The passage (by the author)

I take comfort in the philosophy which I glean from the top of a London motor-bus. From my point of vantage I look down upon pedestrian humanity as a Superman might look down upon it. It seems to consist of a vast multitude of ignorant folk who are predestined to immediate annihilation. As the ungainly machine on which I am seated rushes down the street, it seems admirably adapted for its mission of destruction. The barricade in front of me, devoted to the praise of BOVRIL, is just high enough to prevent my seeing what actually happens, but it gives a bloodcurdling view of catastrophes that are imminent. I have an impression of a procession of innocent victims rushing heedlessly upon destruction. Three yards in front of the onrushing wheels is an old gentleman crossing the street. He suddenly stops. There is, humanly speaking, no hope for him. Two nursemaids appear in the field of danger. A butcher's boy on a bicycle steers directly for the bus. He may be given up for lost. I am not able to see what becomes of them, but I am prepared for the worst. Still the expected crunch does not come, and the bus goes on.

Between Notting Hill Gate and Charing Cross I have seen eighteen persons disappear in this mysterious fashion. I could swear that when I last saw them it seemed too late for them to escape their doom.

But on sober reflection I come to the conclusion that I should have taken a more hopeful view if I had not been so high up; if, for example, I had been sitting with the driver where I could have seen what happened at the last moment.

There was much comfort in the old couplet:—"Betwixt the saddle and the ground, He mercy sought and mercy found."

## Draft A

There is no better observatory for the study of Providence than the top of a London omnibus. From that elevation the street below presents the appearance of a tragedy perpetually on the point of happening. The pedestrians are diminutive creatures, and they move with a confidence entirely unwarranted by their circumstances. A woman steps from the curb with her attention fixed upon a shop window; a cab bears down upon her from one direction and a dray from the other, and I brace myself for the catastrophe. It does not come. When I look again she is on the opposite pavement, and cab and dray have continued upon their errands, unconscious that they have narrowly missed becoming instruments of destiny.

I have watched this spectacle for many miles. Every one of them, as seen from above, is doomed. Not one of them is ever struck.

The explanation lies in the point of view. If I were to descend from my eminence and take my place beside the driver, I should see what is concealed from the upper deck: the little sidestep, the quickened pace, the sudden halt of the horse, and all those last-second escapes which at my altitude are swallowed up in the general confusion. What appears a miracle from above is only good footwork at the level of the street.

It is a comfortable reflection, and I am inclined to carry it beyond the traffic of London. We look down upon a great many lives, our own among them, and we see nothing ahead but disaster, because from where we sit we cannot observe the narrow margin that remains, nor the assistance that arrives when there seems to be no time left for any assistance whatever. There is an old verse about a rider who fell from his horse and yet found mercy on the way to the ground. Rescue need not be early to be in time. The last instant is still an instant, and a great deal may be accomplished in it.

## Draft B

There is no better seat for a moralist than the top of a London omnibus. From that elevation the street below takes on the appearance of a battlefield in which the combatants have not been informed that they are in danger, and cabs, carts and ponderous vans bear down upon a multitude of little figures who go darting across the way with no apparent consideration of what is coming. Every moment I expect a catastrophe. I hold my breath. Yet, so far as my observation extends, none of them is ever struck.

The explanation, I suppose, lies in the point of view. If I were to take a humbler position beside the driver, I should see what the loftier passenger misses: the quick step aside, the sudden halt, the hand upon the bridle, and the innumerable small escapes which are accomplished in the last second and never recorded by anybody. From above, all is doom; from below, it is only a crowded thoroughfare in which people are managing rather well. The tragedy is in the altitude.

It has occurred to me that a considerable part of our despair about the world comes from riding too high. We look down upon the course of human affairs and see nothing but collisions about to happen, because we are too far removed to perceive the swerve that comes in time. But the swerve is there. The old verse about the rider who fell from his horse puts the matter in a couplet, [quotation needed: the old verse about a falling rider], and it is a comfortable doctrine. There was not much time left for the rescue, but there was time enough.

I do not pretend that nobody is ever run over. I only say that the view from the top of the bus is not the whole of the evidence. Hope may be a matter of descending a little nearer to the street.

## Draft C

There is no better place for the study of Providence than the top of a London omnibus. To one who has a taste for moral speculation it offers a spectacle not to be had elsewhere for the price of a ticket. You sit above the stream of traffic as the gods sat above the plains of Troy, and you look down upon the little people who are trying to get across the street.

From that height their case seems hopeless. A man steps from the curb with an umbrella under his arm and a look of mild abstraction on his face. A cab bears down upon him from one side and a van from the other. The horses of our own vehicle are already upon him. You close your eyes, for you have no wish to be a witness at the inquest. When you open them the man is on the opposite pavement, still abstracted, and quite unaware that he has been the subject of your sympathy. The same thing happens a hundred times in an hour. Everybody is doomed, and nobody is struck.

I have come to the conclusion that the fault lies in the point of view. If I were to take my seat beside the driver, lower down and nearer the event, I should doubtless see what the upper deck conceals. I should see the quick step, the sudden swerve, the inch of daylight between the wheel and the coat-tails. What seemed from above to be a tragedy would appear from below as a series of narrow but entirely satisfactory escapes.

This is a comfortable doctrine, and I commend it to those who look down upon human affairs from too great an elevation. The rescue is not always visible from the upper deck. It may come at the last instant, as the old verse about the falling rider would have us believe: [quotation needed]. There is room for a good deal of mercy in a very small space.

## Draft D

There is no better seat in London for a study of human folly than the front of the upper deck of a bus. From that height the street below becomes a kind of stage, and the people on it seem to have been cast, every one of them, in a tragedy. A woman steps off the kerb with her eyes on a shop window. A man with a parcel strikes out across the road as though no wheel had ever turned in it. A boy darts between two carts. From above, each of them is plainly doomed. The bus bears down, the cab swings round, the van comes on without mercy, and one braces for the cry.

The cry never comes. I have ridden a great many miles on the top of a bus, and I have never yet seen anyone struck. The woman reaches the far pavement. The man and his parcel arrive intact. The boy is gone before one has finished being frightened for him.

I suspect the trouble lies in the vantage point. If I sat below, beside the driver, I should see what I cannot see from above: the small margin that remains, the quick side-step, the half-second in which the danger is measured and slipped. The escape happens at the level of the street, and at the last moment, and so from the top deck it is invisible. What looks like ruin from a height is, close at hand, merely a near thing.

It occurs to me that we view a good deal of life from the top of the bus. We look down on our own affairs, and on the world's, and see nothing but collision ahead. We do not see the last-second swerve, because it has not happened yet, and because it will happen where we are not looking. There is an old verse about a rider who fell from his horse and found mercy in the space between the stirrup and the ground. That is a narrow space. It seems, on the evidence of the London streets, to be wide enough.
