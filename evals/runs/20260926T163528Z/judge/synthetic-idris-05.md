## The passage (by the author)

There is also the matter of what the backlog does for those of us who maintain it. A long list is a curious kind of reassurance, since it suggests that nothing has been forgotten, that every concern has a home and that the organisation, whatever its difficulties, is at least keeping track. If you have ever read to the bottom of an old backlog, you may have felt something closer to the atmosphere of a churchyard than a plan: the periodic "grooming" sessions resemble the tending of graves, in which names are reread, the grass is trimmed, a few stones are straightened and everyone departs with the sense of a duty discharged. I do not wish to dismiss the habit of writing things down, which is genuinely better than relying on memory, and the standard advice to prune ruthlessly is correct as far as it goes. The difficulty is that pruning feels like breaking a promise, and so it rarely happens, since nobody wants to be the one who closes the ticket that someone once cared about.

What might a more honest backlog look like? It seems to me that it would begin by distinguishing the work we intend to do from the work we merely wish to acknowledge, and by being willing to say so aloud to the person who asked. A team might, perhaps, keep a short list of genuine commitments alongside a separate record of things heard but not promised—the pleasant idea, the half-formed request, the improvement nobody has yet found time for—and might close old items with a sentence of explanation rather than letting them sink without comment. None of this requires new tools or an elaborate process; it requires only the willingness to tell a colleague, kindly and early, that their request is unlikely to be met. That is harder than creating a ticket, and considerably less comfortable, since it exchanges a vague and pleasant deferral for a clear and faintly awkward refusal. Yet a clear refusal can be planned around, whereas a deferred promise can only be waited upon.

## Draft A

Most teams I have seen carry a backlog far larger than they could ever finish. Hundreds of items sit there, some untouched for so long that nobody remembers who asked for them or why. Everyone knows the list is fiction, yet it keeps growing, and the periodic promise to clean it up quietly fades.

The reason is less about process than about feeling. A long list comforts the people who keep it. It says that nothing has been forgotten, that every request was heard, that the team is busy and in demand. Deleting an item threatens that comfort. Closing a ticket feels like reneging on a promise, even when the promise was never really made. So the item stays, and the list becomes a place where good intentions go to wait indefinitely.

The cost of that comfort is paid by others. Requesters see their ticket marked open and reasonably assume it is on its way. Planning meetings wade through noise to find the few things that matter. New team members cannot tell which entries are live commitments and which are leftovers from a strategy that changed long ago. The backlog stops being a tool for deciding and becomes an archive of things nobody wanted to say no to.

A more truthful alternative starts with separating two kinds of entries. Real commitments are things the team has agreed to do and expects to deliver. Noted ideas are things worth remembering but not promised. They can live in different places, or at least carry different labels, so that nobody mistakes one for the other.

Next, close stale items, and close them with a reason. "Not pursuing because priorities shifted" or "superseded by a different approach" is far kinder than silence. A reason turns a deletion into a decision that someone can read, question or reopen.

Finally, tell requesters early when something won't happen. Waiting until they ask, months later, only makes the disappointment worse and the trust thinner. An early, honest no lets them find another path while it still helps.

A shorter backlog will feel less reassuring at first. It will also be one that people can believe.

## Draft B

Most teams I have worked with keep a backlog that has grown well beyond anything they could reasonably hope to finish, and it seems to me that the reason is less practical than emotional. A long list offers a certain comfort to those who keep it. Every request that has ever been made remains visible somewhere, and the mere fact of its being recorded allows us to feel that nothing has been forgotten, even when nothing is being done about it either. Cleanup is proposed from time to time, usually at the start of a quarter or after a planning session that has gone badly, and yet it rarely happens. Closing a ticket, it turns out, feels uncomfortably like going back on a promise, and few of us enjoy being the person who reneges.

The difficulty is that the backlog, in this state, is not quite telling the truth. It presents itself as a queue of work that will eventually be done, when in practice much of it is a record of ideas that were noted once and never seriously considered again. Perhaps a more truthful alternative would begin by separating these two things: the commitments the team genuinely intends to keep, and the ideas it has merely written down so that they are not lost. The first list ought to be short enough that everyone on the team could describe it from memory; the second can be as long as it likes, provided nobody mistakes it for a plan. Stale items might then be closed deliberately rather than left to drift, each with a brief reason attached, so that anyone who comes looking later can see that the decision was made and not simply avoided.

The harder part, I suspect, is telling the people who asked for something that it will not happen, and telling them early rather than letting the silence do the work. This feels unkind in the moment. It is arguably far kinder than the alternative, in which a request sits unanswered for months and the requester gradually learns, without anyone saying so, that it was never going to be picked up.

What is a backlog actually for? Most teams would say that it records the work they intend to do, and yet almost any backlog that has lived for long enough suggests something rather different, a list that has grown well beyond anyone's capacity to deliver it and is kept not because it guides the work but because its existence is reassuring. It may be that the long list serves its keepers more than its readers, offering the comfort of knowing that nothing has been forgotten even when much of it will never be touched. Cleanup, when someone proposes it, tends to be postponed and then quietly dropped. I suspect this is because closing a ticket feels less like tidying than like reneging on a promise, as though removing an item were an admission that someone's request had been abandoned without their knowledge.

It seems to me that a more truthful alternative begins with separating two kinds of entry that most backlogs mix together: the commitments a team has genuinely made, and the ideas it has merely noted. The first deserve planning and attention; the second might live in a different list, one that nobody mistakes for a promise. Stale items can then be closed deliberately, each with a short reason recorded alongside it, so that the decision is visible rather than silent and anyone who returns to the item later can understand why it was set aside. Perhaps the most important practice of all is telling requesters early when something will not happen, rather than allowing their request to sit unanswered in the hope that the question will somehow resolve itself. This is uncomfortable, since it denies everyone involved the gentle fiction that everything is still possible. It is also, arguably, a form of respect, because it allows people to make other plans while there is still time to make them.

Perhaps a shorter backlog is not a sign of lower ambition but of greater honesty about what a team can actually carry.

## Draft D

Anyone who has spent time on a software team will know the backlog that never shrinks: the list of several hundred items, some of them years old, which everyone agrees is too long and which nobody ever quite gets round to pruning. We usually explain this as a failure of discipline, as though the list had simply grown while our attention was elsewhere. I am not sure that explanation is adequate. A long list is, among other things, a source of comfort to the people who keep it. It records that every request was heard, that no idea was lost, that nothing has been definitively refused; and so long as an item remains open, the question of whether it will ever be done can be quietly deferred. Cleanup rarely happens, I think, not because it is laborious but because closing a ticket feels uncomfortably like reneging on a promise, even when the promise was never really made.

What would a more truthful arrangement look like? The first step, perhaps, is to distinguish between two quite different kinds of entry that the backlog tends to blur together: the commitments the team genuinely intends to honour, and the ideas it has merely noted in case they prove useful. Both are worth keeping, but they are not the same thing, and treating them as though they were allows the second to borrow the weight of the first. The second step is to close stale items deliberately, and with a reason attached—not in a silent purge, which only teaches people that their requests disappear, but in a way that says plainly why the work will not be done. The third, and arguably the hardest, is to tell requesters early when something is not going to happen, rather than letting the item linger until its silence becomes its own answer.

None of this is especially sophisticated. It asks only that we give up the reassurance of the long list in exchange for a shorter one that means what it says. That exchange is less comfortable than it sounds, since it obliges us to disappoint people sooner and more directly than we are used to. But the disappointment was always coming; the only question is whether it arrives honestly or by neglect.
