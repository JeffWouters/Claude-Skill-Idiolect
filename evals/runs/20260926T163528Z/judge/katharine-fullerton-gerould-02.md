# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-02

## The passage (by the author)

There is an old refrain which runs, "Ask me no questions, I'll tell you no lies." I am inclined to think that it is full of social philosophy. Most of us, probably, have put up our hardest fights for veracity on occasions when questions have been asked us that never should have been asked. "Refuse to answer," says the ghost of that extinct Puritan whom we have evoked. An absurd counsel: for, as we all know, to most of these questions no answer is the most explicit answer of all. If the Devil has given you wit enough, you may contrive to keep the letter of the commandment. But usually that does not happen. I dare say many moralists will not agree with me; but I hold that a question put by some one who has no right, from any point of view, to the information demanded, deserves no truth. If a casual gossip should ask me whether my unmarried great-aunt lived beyond her means, I should feel justified in saying that she did not, although it might be the private family scandal that she did. There are inquiries which are a sort of moral burglary. The indiscreet questioner—and by indiscreet questions I mean questions which it is not conceivably a man's duty either to the community or to any individual to answer—is a marauder, and there is every excuse for treating him as such. I am sure that every reader remembers, in his own experience, such questions, and counts among his acquaintance at least one such questioner. Let him say whether, in these conditions, he has felt it his moral duty to hand over information, any more than he would consider it his moral duty to hand over his plate to a thief. I am not speaking of cases where the temptation to lie is merely the temptation to save one's face: it is not permissible to lie merely to save one's face. But it is sometimes permissible to lie to save another person's face—as it was pardonable, surely, in Desdemona to declare that Othello had not murdered her.

## Draft A

There are questions which no one has any business to ask, and yet they are asked every day, across tea-tables and over garden walls, with the most innocent air in the world. What is one to do with them? The moralist, I know, has his answer ready: one must either tell the truth or hold one's tongue. It sounds very well. Unfortunately it does not work.

For silence, in these matters, is seldom silent. Let one decline to answer, however civilly, and the questioner has his answer before one has finished declining. The refusal is itself a confession—and generally a more damaging confession than the plain fact would have been. [example needed] One might better have spoken, for then at least one would have chosen the words.

Now I do not see that a person who has no rightful claim to a piece of private knowledge is owed the truth about it. Why should he be? Prying is a kind of theft. The inquisitive acquaintance is picking a lock, and it has never yet been held that one must hand the burglar the spoons because he asked for them politely. If I put him off with a fiction, I have deceived him, certainly; but I have deceived him out of something that was never his to have.

But here I must draw a line, and draw it firmly. There is all the difference in the world between the lie that protects oneself and the lie that protects another. If I lie merely to spare my own dignity—to seem a little finer, or a little wiser, or a little better placed than I am—I am doing a shabby thing, and no amount of casuistry will make it otherwise. Vanity is not a secret one has any right to guard with falsehood. It is quite another matter when the secret belongs to someone else: a trouble confided in trust, which the questioner would carry off to the next house before dinner. There, I think, the lie may be excused—not praised, perhaps, but excused. One is not then saving one's own face. One is simply keeping shut a door that somebody else has asked one to keep shut; and I cannot believe that the recording angel, whatever else he may set down against us, will set down that.

## Draft B

We are taught that lying is wrong, and as a rule it is. But there is one situation in which the rule deserves a closer look: the intrusive question, asked by someone who has no business asking it.

The usual advice is simply to decline to answer. Say nothing, change the subject, tell the questioner it is none of their concern. The trouble is that this rarely works. A refusal is itself an answer. If someone asks about a private matter and you pointedly refuse to say [example needed], you have told them what they wanted to know as surely as if you had spoken. Silence in the face of a direct question is read, and usually read correctly. So the choice is often not between telling the truth and keeping a secret; it is between telling the truth and telling a lie.

That changes the moral picture. Our duty to tell the truth is owed to people who have some claim on it. A person with no rightful claim to private information is owed no truth about it. The one who pries is not an innocent inquirer who happens to be deceived; he is trying to take something that does not belong to him. Prying of this kind resembles theft more than conversation, and we do not usually think a thief is wronged when we refuse to hand over the goods, even by a misleading word.

Still, the justification has limits, and they matter. It does not cover every lie told under pressure. Lying to protect one's own dignity, to avoid embarrassment or to spare oneself an awkward admission, is wrong. The discomfort is ours to bear, and the lie serves only our vanity. The case is different when what is at stake belongs to someone else. When a lie is the only way to shield another person's private affairs from someone with no right to them, it can be excused. The liar in that case gains nothing for himself. He is guarding a trust, and the fault for the falsehood lies at least as much with the one who forced the choice as with the one who made it.

## Draft C

We are told, from the nursery onward, that we must never tell a lie. It is an excellent rule for the nursery; but it was made, I think, by people who had never been asked an impertinent question at a dinner-table. For the trouble with the impertinent question is that there is no honest way out of it. You may refuse to answer, of course; you may declare, with all the dignity you can muster and a good deal more than you actually feel, that the matter is nobody's concern but your own. And the questioner, who is not a fool (the prying sort seldom are!), will draw his own conclusion from your silence, and it will generally be the right one. A refusal is very often only a slower way of telling the truth.

Now I cannot see that any one who has no rightful claim to a piece of knowledge is owed the truth about it. The man who pries into what does not concern him is not asking a favour; he is trying to take something that is not his. It is a kind of theft, and a mean kind, since what he steals cannot be given back. We do not think it wicked to lock our doors against the burglar, or to mislead him, if we can, about where the silver is kept; and I confess I do not understand why we should consider it wicked to mislead the gossip, whose intrusion is of precisely the same character. Very well, then. He has put himself outside the bargain on which truthfulness rests; and he can hardly complain if the bargain does not protect him.

But there is a distinction to be drawn here, and it is not a small one. To lie in order to save one's own face is another matter altogether. If the thing he is hunting does one no credit, one must endure its discovery; vanity is not a good enough reason for deceit, and I should not like to defend the person who thought it was. It is quite different when the secret belongs to some one else—to a friend who trusted us, or to some one who cannot speak for himself. Then, I think, the lie may be forgiven. It is not told for our own sake, and that makes all the difference.

## Draft D

We are all taught, in the nursery, that a lie is a lie, and that there is an end of it. But the nursery did not reckon with the person who asks what is none of his business. Every one has met him. He wishes to know [example needed: the sort of private question he puts]; and he asks it with an air of perfect innocence, as though the answer were owed him like change at a shop. What is one to do? The moralist says: refuse to answer. It sounds very well. Yet in practice a refusal is nearly always an answer, and often a louder one than the truth would have been; the silence, the change of subject, the heightened colour, tell him precisely what he came to find out.

Let us look into the matter a little more squarely. Truth is owed, surely, to those who have some rightful claim upon it—to a judge, to a partner, to a friend who must act upon what we say. It is not owed to every one who happens to put a question. The man who pries into a private matter has no more title to the answer than a thief has to your purse; he is, in plain words, trying to take what does not belong to him. I do not see that we are bound to hand it over merely because he has asked politely. One does not, after all, feel obliged to tell a burglar where the silver is kept.

Granted; but there is a limit, and I think it lies here. If I lie only to save my own face, to spare my own pride or cover my own foolishness, I have done a shabby thing, and no amount of casuistry will make it otherwise. My dignity is my own affair; I must bear the cost of it. It is quite another matter when the secret is some one else's. To lie in order to shield another person—one who trusted us, and who cannot defend himself—may not be admirable. But it can be excused. That is the whole of the distinction.
