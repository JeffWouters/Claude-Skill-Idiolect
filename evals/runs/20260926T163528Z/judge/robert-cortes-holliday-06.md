# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-06

## The passage (by the author)

Over on the Avenue busses are busily pushing plows hitched on before. There one has got stalled in a drift. It whirs and buzzes and backs and starts and whirs and buzzes over and over again. No use, it seems. Still, draped along the curb, the spectators stand, unmindful of the gale, as absorbed as if at a Yale-Princeton game. Buzzzzzzzzz—Whirrrrrrrrrrr—and away. She's off! A feeble cheer goes up. And everybody starts onward again in better humor with himself for having seen so entertaining a show.

It snowed the night through.

In the morning banks of snow breast-high through the side streets. Through a narrow aisle down the middle of the roadway trucks cars and wagons slowly go in single file. Moving thus all in a single line they have something the effect of a circus parade—elephants and lion cages and so on.

And lions remind me. It is always well to look at public statues and outdoor pieces of sculpture the morning after a heavy snow. You are likely to find them very comical apparitions. The celebrated literary lions before the New York Public Library, for instance, wore throughout the day after the first big snow of this winter ridiculous tall caps pulled down very rakishly over their eyes.

Streaming from the direction of the railroad station were coming the swarms of our commuter friends, the legs of many of them hoisting along those prodigious "arctics" which are all the vogue nowadays. Isn't it curious? There was a time when if you were obliged to wear glasses you got them as nearly invisible as possible. If you were a man you felt there was something shameful about having "weak" eyes. If a woman, you "just knew" that glasses made you look "horrid." And when you wore overshoes you got them as inconspicuous as possible. Now you affect shell spectacles that can be seen a block away, and having huge lenses. Now there is nothing smarter, apparently, than for a young woman with a trim foot to come into town swaddled in floppers which fit her slim ankles like a bucket.

## Draft A

Now, the buses of the great city have been fitted with plows. This is efficiency. A bus with a plow upon its nose, so the idea runs, is not a bus at all, but a kind of conquering engine, before which the snow must fall back in rout. With a roar of the motor and a spin of the wheels the bus advances. It advances perhaps a yard. With still further roaring and a still more furious spinning of the wheels it advances again. No, it is stuck fast in the drift. And upon the sidewalks, bundled to the ears, stand the bystanders, who have nowhere in particular to go, and who cheer. They cheer the bus when it moves an inch, and they cheer the drift when it does not. It is the finest free entertainment of the winter.

Next morning the town is another place. Lanes have been cut through the white, and the traffic creeps along them single file, taxicab behind truck, truck behind limousine, in a long and humble procession, like a line of penitents. Nobody is in a hurry, because nobody can be. The statues, too, have been touched by the storm. Each wears a cap of snow, set on at a jaunty angle, and the lions before the Library, those grave and dignified beasts, look for once as though they might be persuaded to enjoy themselves.

And then there are the overshoes. The commuter comes up out of the ground in overshoes of an astonishing bulk, clumping along like a diver on the floor of the sea. Fashion in these matters has turned clean round. There was a time, and not so very long ago, when a person who wore glasses took them off in company, and a person who wore galoshes would sooner have got his feet wet. Such things were hidden, as one hides a patch. All that has changed. Glasses are now large, and dark-rimmed, and worn as a sort of decoration. Galoshes flap open and are displayed. The overshoe, as its wearer's easy stride plainly tells you, is nothing to apologise for. It is the thing.

## Draft B

The snow came down on New York hard enough to stop the city in its own streets. The buses had been fitted with plows for exactly this kind of storm, and for a while they pushed on bravely. Then, one after another, they ran into drifts they could not shift and simply stuck there, engines running, wheels turning to no purpose. Nobody seemed especially upset. A crowd gathered around each stranded bus as if it were a show put on for their benefit, and when a driver managed to rock his machine an inch forward the bystanders cheered him the way they might cheer a runner on the last lap. When he slid back again, they cheered that too.

By the next morning the city had dug itself narrow lanes, and traffic went through them single file, crawling. Cars that would ordinarily have fought for every foot of the avenue now waited politely behind one another, since there was nowhere else to go. The statues had come through the night with snow caps on their heads. The library lions sat under theirs with a patient, slightly foolish dignity, like large animals who had been dressed up by children and decided not to object. [example needed: another statue]

The commuters were dressed for the occasion, and the occasion called for overshoes. Everyone was clumping along in them, bulky and black and unlovely, and nobody seemed in the least embarrassed. That is the real change. There was a time when anyone with a claim to style hid such things. Glasses were taken off before a photograph and galoshes were left at home even when the gutters ran, on the principle that it was better to be half blind and wet than seen to be sensible. Somewhere along the way the principle turned around. Glasses became something to choose with care and wear on purpose, and the overshoe has followed them out into the open. People now look at one another's galoshes on a snowy morning the way they once looked at hats, and it is the person without them who looks as though he did not quite think it through.

## Draft C

The snow came down upon New York with a thoroughness that the city does not as a rule permit to anything but the rush hour. By evening the avenues had gone, and in their place lay long, soft, rolling drifts in which the old landmarks stood about like lost strangers. The bus companies, to do them justice, had seen what was coming. Their buses had been fitted out with plows, and they sallied forth to do battle. It was a gallant sight, and a brief one. One after another the plow-fitted buses put their noses into the drifts and stopped, and there they stayed, snorting and helpless. Upon the sidewalks the bystanders gathered, as bystanders in this city will gather for any trouble which is not their own, and cheered. Whom they were cheering I cannot say; the snow, it may be.

The next morning the town had gone back to work, after a fashion. Lanes had been cleared down the middle of the main streets, and through these the traffic crawled in single file, taxicab behind truck behind limousine, with the slow dignity of a funeral. The statues had all put on hats in the night. The lions in front of the library wore tall white caps, cocked at a rakish angle, and looked rather pleased with themselves.

But the most instructive sight of all was the commuter. He came up out of the subway shod in huge overshoes, bulky and buckled, and he walked in them without the least trace of shame. Here is a real revolution in manners. There was a time, and it is not so very long ago, when a man would have gone about half blind rather than be seen in spectacles, and would have endured wet feet to the edge of pneumonia rather than own to galoshes. Such things were hidden, apologised for, whisked off in the hall. Now they're shown. The glasses are worn large and plain upon the nose; the galoshes go clumping down the avenue with a certain swagger. The very things that fashion once told us to hide, fashion now tells us to exhibit. [example needed: a particular commuter or pair of overshoes observed]

## Draft D

The snow came down upon New York as though it had been saving up for the occasion, and by nightfall the city had the look of a place which has been handsomely and completely surprised. The buses, fitted out with plows for exactly this emergency, went forth valiantly to meet it. They did not get far. One after another they stuck in the drifts, snorting, grinding, backing a little, lunging a little, and settling deeper. And around each of them a crowd collected, as a crowd will in this town around anything whatever that is in difficulty. The bystanders did not help; they watched. When a bus made a gallant heave and gained a foot they cheered; when it lost the foot again they cheered also, with perfect impartiality. It was, so to speak, a sporting event, and no admission was charged.

The next morning the town was a different spectacle. Lanes had been cleared through the principal streets, and through these the traffic crawled along in single file, with the patient, dejected air of a procession which has forgotten where it was going. The statues, meanwhile, had all acquired headgear. The lions before the library sat there with snow caps drawn over their brows, looking rather less majestic and considerably more comfortable than usual. [example needed: another statue] Nobody had ordered these caps, and nobody seemed inclined to remove them.

On foot the commuters came plodding through it in bulky overshoes, and this reminded me of a curious revolution in fashion. There was a time, not so very long ago, when a person of any refinement concealed his glasses whenever he could, and would sooner have gone about with wet feet than be seen in galoshes. Both were regarded as confessions of infirmity, like an ear trumpet. That time, it seems, has gone. Glasses are now worn conspicuously, with large and fashionable rims, as an ornament; galoshes are flapped about unbuckled, with an air, as it were, of having chosen them. What was once hidden is now displayed. The snowstorm merely gave the new fashion a particularly fine morning on which to show itself off.
