# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-08

## The passage (by the author)

There is a fashionable attitude of mind among many who pride themselves on their acute intellectualism. It manifests itself in a supercilious compassion for the efforts and ambitions of the man of action. He, poor fellow, is well-meaning, but unilluminated. He is eager and energetic because he imagines that he is accomplishing something. If he were a serious thinker he would see that all effort is futile. We are here in an unintelligible world, a world of mighty forces, moving we know not whither. We are subject to passions and impulses which we cannot resist. We are never so helpless as when we are in the midst of human affairs. We have great words which we utter proudly. We talk of Civilization, Christianity, Democracy, and the like. What miserable failures they all are. Civilization has failed to produce contentment. It has failed to secure perfect justice between man and man, or to satisfy the hungry with bread. Christianity after all these centuries of preaching leaves mankind as we see it to-day—an armed camp, nation fighting nation, class warring against class. The democratic movement about which we hear so much is equally unsuccessful. After its brilliant promises it leaves us helpless against the passion and stupidity of the mob. Popular education adds to the tribulations of society. It rapidly increases the number of the discontented. The half-educated are led astray by quacks and demagogues who flourish mightily. The higher technical education increases that intellectual proletariat which Bismarck saw to be a peril. Science, which once was hailed as a deliverer, is now perceived to bring only the disillusioning knowledge of our limitations. The bankruptcy of Science follows closely upon the bankruptcy of Faith. Mechanical inventions, instead of decreasing the friction of life, enormously increase it. We are destined to be dragged along by our own machines which are to go faster and faster. Philanthropy increases the number of the unfit. The advances of medicine are only apparent, while statistics show that tuberculosis, a disease of early life, decreases, cancer and diseases of later life increase.

## Draft A

There is a class of persons who have lately discovered that they are deep thinkers, and who bear the discovery with a melancholy dignity. They do not do anything in particular; to do anything would be to confess that they had not thought it through. They look out upon a world which they declare to be chaotic and incomprehensible, a world in which no cause leads to its proper effect and no purpose is ever fulfilled, and from this survey they draw the conclusion that all effort is pointless. Having drawn it, they are at leisure to pity the rest of us.

For it is the energetic man whom they chiefly commiserate. He builds, he organizes, he subscribes to good causes, he goes to bed tired and gets up hopeful. To the deep thinker this is a pathetic spectacle. The poor fellow does not know that the universe has no use for his exertions, and that he would be wiser to sit still and contemplate the futility of it all, because he has not read the right books, or, having read them, he has not taken them to heart.

It is instructive. Hear him enumerate the failures of mankind. Civilization, he tells us, has failed; it has only multiplied our wants. Religion has failed, and democracy has failed rather more conspicuously. Education has produced a population that can read without being able to think. Science has answered every question except the one that matters, and invention has given us the means of going faster to no destination. Charity, he observes, perpetuates the evils it relieves. Medicine keeps alive those whom nature had sensibly intended to remove. The catalogue is long, and he recites it with a certain satisfaction, as a man might recite the list of his own achievements.

I do not deny that there is something in each of these complaints. What I notice is that the deep thinker is never himself among the failures. He stands a little apart, a spectator at the collapse of everything, and finds in his own clear sight of the ruin a sufficient compensation for having contributed nothing to prevent it, which is a comfortable position and has besides the merit of being perfectly safe. Nobody can accuse a man of failing who has taken care never to attempt anything.

## Draft B

There is a kind of person who looks upon the busy man with an indulgent pity. He does not despise him. He regards him as one regards a child who is building a sand castle below the high-water mark, happy in his work and ignorant of what the tide has in store for it, and he feels that it would be unkind to mention the tide.

These gentle mourners are commonly to be found among those who describe themselves as deep thinkers. They have looked into the nature of things and found it wanting. The world, as they conceive it, is a chaos which no one understands and which nobody ever will, and in such a world every effort is equally pointless and the only dignified attitude is that of the spectator who has seen through the performance. They do not act. Action is for those who have not yet thought the matter out.

It is instructive to hear them take an inventory of human failure. Civilization has failed, for men are no better than they were. Religion has failed; its temples stand, but its spirit has departed. Democracy has failed, and education has failed, and science has failed to answer the only questions which really matter, while invention has only multiplied our wants, charity has multiplied the poor, and medicine keeps alive a great many persons who could more reasonably have been allowed to die. Nothing is left over. The deep thinker folds his hands with the air of a man who has finished an unpleasant but necessary piece of accounting.

Meanwhile the energetic doer goes on doing. He mends a road, or teaches a school, or sets a bone, without having first settled whether the universe is intelligible. It may be that he is shallow. But it is a curious fact that the deep thinkers are obliged to travel upon his roads, and to send for him in some haste when their own bones are broken.

## Draft C

There is a certain type of intellectual who looks upon the energetic person with a pity that is not unmixed with contempt. He sees him going about his business, building houses, organizing committees, and making improvements, and he shakes his head. The poor fellow does not understand. He thinks that what he does makes some difference. He has not yet gone deep enough to discover that nothing makes any difference.

For the deep thinker has made that discovery, and it is the source of his distinction. He has looked into the universe and found it to be a chaos. It has no plan that he can make out, and he is not so presumptuous as to suppose that there is a plan which he cannot make out. The world is incomprehensible, and therefore all effort in it is futile. Those who continue to make efforts show only that they have not thought the matter through.

It is a pleasant thing to be in the company of one of these gentlemen, provided one does not stay too long. He has a catalogue of failures, and he will read it to you with the relish of a connoisseur. Civilization, he tells you, has failed; it has only made men more miserable in more complicated ways. Religion has failed, and democracy has failed. Education has failed, for it has taught people to read without teaching them anything worth reading. Science has failed to explain anything that matters, and invention has only multiplied the things we can do without. Charity has failed, for it has pauperized those it meant to help. Medicine has failed, for it keeps alive those who would be better off dead. He goes through the list without haste, and at the end of it he has the look of a man who has done a good morning's work.

What puzzles me is the air of superiority with which all this is delivered. It is not the melancholy of a man who has lost something he cared for. It is the satisfaction of a man who has found himself above the need of caring. The doer, blundering along with his little schemes, is an object of compassion. He is still in the kindergarten of experience, while the pessimist has graduated.

## Draft D

There is a kind of person, very common just now, who has thought deeply, and who wishes you to know it. You will find him at dinner parties and in the better sort of magazine, and you will recognise him at once by the gentle sorrow with which he regards anyone who is busy. He has looked into the heart of things, he tells you, and found nothing there. The world is a chaos. Its purposes, if it has any, are beyond our understanding. Everything we do is done in the dark and comes to nothing, and the only honest attitude for a thinking man is a sort of weary, well-dressed despair.

From this height he looks down on the people who are still trying. The man who builds a bridge, the woman who organises a school, the doctor hurrying to a sickbed: he pities them all. They have not yet understood, poor souls, that it makes no difference. They are like children building sandcastles against the tide, and he watches them with the tender melancholy of one who has seen the tide.

Ask him to explain and he will give you a catalogue. Civilisation, he says, has failed; it has only made us more comfortable in our misery. Religion has failed, and so has democracy, which has merely handed power from the few who abused it to the many who do not know what to do with it. Education has failed, since it teaches people to read without teaching them anything worth reading. Science has failed to tell us why we are here. Invention has given us machines and taken away our peace. Charity has failed, because the poor are always with us. Medicine has failed, because in the end we all die.

It is an impressive list, and he delivers it very well. What strikes me about it, though, is that he is quite comfortable while he recites it, in a warm room, well fed, in a lighted house, and that almost every one of these comforts was provided by the busy people he pities. The deep thinker has decided that effort is useless. It is fortunate for him that nobody else has.
