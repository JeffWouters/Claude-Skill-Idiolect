# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-09

## The passage (by the author)

But, lo! as I say, this man started this new book. It began as a series of articles for which he was to be paid—that was why it was begun at all. Now see! With him it was as Professor George Edward Woodberry says of Poe in his admirable "Life"—for a time his genius had "slept." With the start of the new book he awoke. It began to run right out of the ends of his fingers. Took (that book) hold of him completely. He couldn't leave it. Go to bed, have to get up and go at it again. Try to go out for a round of exercise. After a block or so from his quarters, walk slower and slower. Miserable. Tortured. Turn back. Immediately happy again. Soon be back at work. Anybody who entangled him with an invitation anywhere enraged him beyond measure.

New book finished. Everything fine. Got another commission. Easy enough job. Set to at it. Empty vessel again! In despair. He'd make all sorts of excuses to himself to leave his place early in the morning to postpone beginning work. He'd go anywhere, with anybody, to keep as long as possible from facing that task again. Couldn't give any sensible explanation of his prolonged delay to the publishers. Kept putting them off again and again, with one cripple-legged excuse after another, in the hope that he'd come round. Matter became a disgrace.

Still queerer cases than that I know. Fellow who shared an apartment with me one time. When according to the accepted law of nature his mind should have been in a very bad way, then always was he at his best. After leading a regular, wholesome life for a period his mind would become dull, stale and unprofitable. When, following a very different sort of period, he should in all reason have awakened with a splitting head, a swollen eye and a shaking hand, he would get up at about dawn one morning in rattling fine spirits, his mind as clear as a bell, and with an impassioned desire to work. Could, then, write like a streak. But doesn't William James touch upon such a matter as this somewhere?

## Draft A

Speaking of work reminds me of how curiously it comes and goes. I have observed this thing in writers for some time, and I have not yet seen anybody who could tell me the rule of it. There does not appear to be any rule. The stuff arrives, or it does not arrive, and the writer (as the case may be) is either a happy man or a man writing letters of apology.

Consider the case of [writer]. He was engaged to do a series of articles, a modest paid series, a few thousand words here and there, the kind of thing a man does in the morning and forgets by lunch. But the thing would not stay modest. It grew. It put out branches. It became a book, and the book took hold of him and would not let him go. Early and late, week in and week out, he was at it, not because anyone had asked him to be but because he could not stop. Some time afterwards he was given another commission, an easy one, the sort of job you could do with your left hand. And it stuck. It stuck for months. His publishers wrote to him, and he wrote back. He had been unwell; the weather had been against him; he had been called out of town; he was, he assured them, very nearly done. He was not very nearly done. He had not, I believe, begun.

Then there was a young man with whom I once shared rooms. When he lived sensibly—early to bed, plain food, a brisk walk in the morning, no nonsense—he could not write a line worth keeping. His virtue was sterile. But let him come home at some unholy hour, after a night of excess which I will not describe, and he would sit down with a wet towel about his head and turn out the best work of his life. This is not advice. I merely report it.

Now, has any psychologist explained these things? I should like very much to know. I have looked, in a general way, and have found [example needed]. Perhaps the explanation is in some learned volume I have not read. Perhaps it is not anywhere at all. Just so. The muse, it seems, keeps her own hours, and she does not consult the publishers.

## Draft B

Nobody who writes for a living can tell you, with any honesty, when the work will come and when it will refuse to. I have stopped pretending that I can.

Some years ago I agreed to write a series of articles for pay. It was meant to be a modest arrangement, a few pieces and a cheque at the end of each. Instead the thing grew under my hands. One article asked for another, and the series turned itself into a book, and the book took hold of me so completely that for a long stretch I thought of nothing else. I worked early and late without being asked, and without much noticing that I was working at all.

Later a commission came along that should have been the easiest job of my life. The subject suited me, the terms were generous, and nothing stood in the way but the writing. It stalled. Weeks became months, and I sent my publishers a steady supply of excuses, each more ingenious than the last and none of them the truth, which was simply that the words would not come. I could not have told them why, because I did not know.

I once shared rooms with a man whose case was stranger still. Whenever he resolved to live sensibly, going to bed at a decent hour, eating plainly, walking in the fresh air and keeping away from drink, his work dried up entirely. He would sit at his desk with a clear head and produce nothing worth keeping. But after a night of excess, when by every rule he ought to have been fit for nothing, he would sit down and write better than he ever did otherwise. I watched this happen more than once, and I never saw it fail. [example needed]

I do not offer his habits as a method. I mention them because they make nonsense of the neat advice we are all given about regular hours and clean living, and because my own experience makes nonsense of it in a different way.

I should like to know whether any psychologist has ever looked into this properly and found an explanation. If one has, I have not come across it. Until someone does, I suspect most of us will go on as we are, grateful when the work arrives, bewildered when it leaves, and inventing excuses in between.

## Draft C

There is no accounting for the manner in which a writer's productivity comes and goes. It arrives when it has no particular business arriving, and it departs, like a visitor who has suddenly remembered another engagement, at precisely the moment when it is most urgently required. I have in mind a writer who once undertook a series of articles for payment. It was a modest enough undertaking, so many pieces at so much apiece. But the thing expanded. It expanded into a book, and the book took possession of him; it consumed his days and a considerable portion of his nights, and he did not appear to mind in the least. Nobody had commissioned a book. Nobody was expecting one.

Some while afterwards the same man accepted a commission which was, by every reasonable measure, an uncommonly easy one. Here, you would say, was something he could accomplish with his left hand. He did not accomplish it. For months it stalled. His publishers wrote to him, and he wrote back to them, and what he wrote back was excuses. I gather they were rather more ingenious than anything else he was producing at the time. The book which nobody wanted had cost him, apparently, nothing but his entire existence; the easy assignment which somebody did want was more than he could bring himself to touch.

A former flatmate of mine was, if anything, a stranger case. When he had lived for a period as the moralists would have us live, early to bed, plain food, fresh air and everything else of that description, he could not put together two sentences that he cared to preserve. He sat before his paper in excellent condition and produced nothing whatever. It was following nights of excess that he did his finest work. He would approach his table, somewhat the worse for wear, and the words would come as though they had been patiently waiting for him. I do not offer this as a recommendation. I merely report it.

Has any psychologist explained such cases? Perhaps one has. If so, I should very much like to read him, and I should be interested, too, to discover what he makes of the remainder of us.

## Draft D

There is no predicting, so far as I can discover, when a writer will work and when he will not. Consider the case of a certain author. He was engaged, and paid, to produce a series of articles. Nothing particularly ambitious was expected of him. But the articles declined to remain articles. They expanded, installment by installment, into something longer and weightier, and presently they were a book. The book then took possession of him entirely. He breakfasted with it, slept with it, carried it about the streets with him; it consumed him, as it were, body and soul. Nobody had commissioned a book, and I doubt whether he could have stopped it had he tried.

Some time later he accepted a commission of quite another character. It was an easy undertaking, the sort of assignment a man could accomplish, you would suppose, with his left hand of an afternoon. There it stopped. Weeks went by, and then months, and all the while he wrote to his publishers one excuse after another ([example needed: the excuses he gave]), each more elaborate than the last, while the publishers waited, not always patiently, for a manuscript that did not arrive. The work simply would not come, and he could not have explained why. Here was a man who had surrendered himself to a task nobody required of him, and who could not now be persuaded to finish a trifling one he had promised.

Then there is the case of a gentleman who formerly shared lodgings with me. When he lived sensibly and attended conscientiously to his health he could not produce a sentence worth preserving. He would sit at his table, virtuous and vacant, the entire morning through. Let him be out half the night in riotous company, however, and come home at an hour I shan't specify, and the following day the pages fairly poured from him. He was, in a manner of speaking, a man with whom dissipation agreed, at least at the writing desk. I don't recommend his method; I merely report it. What is one to make of such contradictions? Perhaps some psychologist has investigated them and recorded everything in a learned monograph; if so, I should very much like to read it. It might explain a good deal, to the writer as much as to anybody.
