## The passage (by the author)

Now I know as well as other people that it is positively harder to get into our old universities to-day than it was in our fathers' day. But granted the enormously increased facilities for preparation all over the land, it is not relatively anything like so hard. Certainly, once in, it is possible to get through the college course with less work than ever before. In the first place, there is a much wider choice of subjects on which a boy can get his degree: his tastes are consulted as they never used to be. If he does not want to endure the discipline of Greek, he can get an A.B. at every college in the country—except Princeton—without knowing a word of Greek. Even at Princeton, he can take a Litt.B. and let Greek forever alone. He can study sociology, or Spanish, or physical culture, or nearly anything he likes. I have even heard that in one of our state universities there is a department of hat-trimming, which contributes its quota to the courses for a (presumably feminine) academic degree.

It may be objected at this point that the fluctuations of colleges have nothing to do with our standards of culture. I think they have, a great deal. No one will deny that culture can be got elsewhere, or that colleges do not suffice in themselves to give it. But if colleges do not consider themselves custodians of culture, warders and cherishers of the flame, they have no reason for existence. It is a platitude that business men consider college a worthless preparation for business life—save as a young man may have laid up there treasure for himself in the shape of valuable "connections." Even the conception of college as a four years' paradise intervening before the hell of an active struggle for existence, does not touch upon the original reason for universities' being at all. Universities were invented for the sake of bringing their fortunate students into contact with the precious lore of the world, there garnered and kept pure. There was no idea on the part of their founders that every one would or could partake of academic benefits. The social scheme would not originally have allowed that; still less would the conception of the public intellect have admitted the notion. Every one was not supposed to be congenitally qualified for intimacy with the best that has been thought and said in the world. They had no notion, until very recently, of so changing the terms of that intimacy that every one might think he could have it. Learning, culture, were not to be adulterated so that any mental digestive process whatsoever could take them in.

## Draft A

There is a curious paradox in the present state of our colleges. It has never been harder to get into them, and it has never been easier to get out of them with a degree. The entrance examinations grow stiffer every year; the candidate is sifted, weighed and certified before he is permitted to set foot inside the gate. Once there, however, he finds that very little is asked of him. I do not say that every young man ought to be made to read Greek; I say only that a university which no longer expects any one to do so has quietly changed its notion of what it is for. The door is narrower, and the house behind it is emptier.

What, then, is a university for? Not, surely, to prepare young men for business. Business is an honourable calling, and it has its own excellent schools of instruction, in the counting-house and the market. The university exists for something else: to preserve the finest learning the race has accumulated, and to transmit it, unimpaired, to the next generation. It is a guardian before it is a teacher. If it forgets the first office, the second becomes merely a matter of convenience—a pleasant interval of lectures and amusements between school and employment. One may defend that interval, perhaps, on other grounds. One cannot call it culture.

Some one will object that learning ought to be open to every one, and that the old requirements were a kind of privilege. Granted; but there is a difference between opening the door and lowering the ceiling. The founders of our colleges were not, I fancy, indifferent to the spread of knowledge. They simply never imagined that the way to spread it was to dilute it until everybody could swallow it without effort. They meant the university to hold a standard, and to hold it against the fashions of the moment; they did not mean it to follow the fashions, at a respectful distance, and call the result liberality. A diluted learning is not a democratic learning. It is only a weaker one, and those who receive it are cheated of precisely the thing they came for. The point is not that the university should shut out the many. It is that it should give them, when they come, something worth having.

## Draft B

It is a queer thing, and I have never heard it satisfactorily explained, that our colleges should have grown harder to enter and easier to leave. ## Draft C

Our colleges have been changing, and the direction of the change deserves more attention than it gets. On the surface the picture looks healthy. Getting in is harder than it used to be; the examinations at the gate are stiffer and the competition for places keener. One might conclude from this that standards are rising. But entry is only half the story, and it is the lesser half.

Once a student is inside, the degree asks less of him than it once did. The old prescribed course of study has given way to free choice of subjects. A young man may assemble his education from whatever mix of courses suits his taste, and he will emerge with the same diploma as one who took the harder road. Greek, long the backbone of a liberal education, is now optional, and what is optional is, for most students, soon abandoned. The door is narrower, but the house behind it has fewer rooms that anyone is required to enter.

This matters because of what a university is for. It is not a training school for business, and it was never meant to be one. Its purpose is to preserve the finest learning a civilisation has produced and to pass it on intact to the next generation. That is a guardianship. A guardian who lets each visitor decide which parts of the treasure are worth keeping is not guarding very much. The value of a prescribed course lay precisely in the fact that it did not depend on the preferences of young students. It held up a standard that stood outside the student and asked him to rise to it.

When the university instead reshapes itself around what students would like, or what will be useful to them in commerce, it trades that role for another one that many institutions can already fill. Preparation for business can be had in many places. The careful transmission of the best that has been thought and written has, in most societies, only one real home. If the university does not do it, it is hard to see who will.

Nor can the change be defended as a return to first principles. The founders of our colleges did not mean learning to be diluted so that everyone might have a portion. They meant it to be kept whole, and offered to those willing to meet its demands. Making the gate harder while making the course softer gets this exactly backwards. It turns the degree into a mark of admission rather than a mark of learning, and it leaves the university guarding a door while the culture it was built to protect goes quietly out the back.

There is a curious contradiction in the colleges just now. It has never been harder to get into them, and it has never been easier, once inside, to get out again with a degree. What is it for? Not, I think, to fit young men for business. There are a great many excellent places in which to learn business, and most of them are in business itself. A university has another errand, and a harder one: to keep the finest learning of the race alive, and to hand it on, undiminished, to those who can receive it. It is a guardian before it is anything else. If it does not preserve that learning, nobody will; the counting-house has no time for it, and the newspapers have no patience with it. When the colleges turn themselves into schools of practical success, they do not add a new service to the old one. They simply abandon the old one, and there is no one left to take it up.

We are told, of course, that this is democracy; that learning must be made easy so that every one may have a share of it. It is a generous notion, and I should be sorry to seem ungenerous in opposing it. But learning that has been made easy for every one is no longer quite the same learning. It has been watered, and a watered wine is not a cheaper wine; it is a different drink. I cannot believe that the men who founded our colleges intended anything of the kind. They meant them, surely, to be places where the best that had been thought was kept safe and taught with rigour—not places where it was diluted until any one could swallow it without effort. It manifests itself in a supercilious compassion for the efforts and ambitions of the man of action. He, poor fellow, is well-meaning, but unilluminated.
