Every forkful I shake a little. Clumps of grass break up. Air gets in. The heap needs air as much as it needs food. Without it the middle goes wet and sour and grey. It smells like a drain.

I learned that the year I left the heap alone too long. 18 months. The bottom was a black paste. The smell stayed on my hands for two days. I turned it three times that year to put it right.

Halfway down it changes. The compost is dark and loose and crumbles in the hand. There are worms, red and thin, hundreds of them. There is steam on a cold morning. I once pushed a thermometer into the middle. 58 degrees. Hot enough to hurt if you left your hand there.

Here is the count from this October.

Two hours and ten minutes. About 140 forkfuls. Three barrow loads of finished compost from the bottom. One teaspoon, one fork from the kitchen drawer, one sock. No sign of the caddy lid, which went missing in May.

The finished compost is sieved into the barrow. The sieve is a square of wire mesh on a wooden frame. A centimetre gap. What passes through goes on the beds. What does not goes back in the heap. Eggshells mostly, and avocado stones. They never rot.

Then I water the new heap. Two cans, about 20 litres. I put the boards back on the right bay and cover the top with an old carpet. The carpet keeps the heat in and the rain off.

The left bay is empty now. It starts again the same evening, with the peelings from dinner.

But it is heavy work, and slow. A plastic bin with a lid costs 35 euros at the garden centre. You tip in your scraps and wait. Ruud over the fence has one. He does not turn it. He says it gets there in the end. It does, in about two years.
