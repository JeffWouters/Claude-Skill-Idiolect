Last Tuesday it came at about two. I woke at six to the quiet. Snow makes the street go still. No tyres, no bus. Just the tick of the boiler and a blackbird somewhere in the hedge.

The shovel lives in the shed with the handle up. It is a wide aluminium one, 50 centimetres across the blade. I bought it at a car boot sale for eight euros. Before that I used my grandfather's coal shovel. It is narrow and heavy and good for nothing but coal.

Here is the method.

First, boots and two pairs of socks. Then gloves with a grip on the palm. Wool gloves are wet in ten minutes. Your hands go stiff before the path is half done.

I start at the step and not at the gate. That way I am always standing on cleared ground. If you start at the gate you tread the snow flat on the way down. Flat snow turns to ice by evening.

So then the first cut. I push the blade straight down the middle, one width, all the way to the gate. That opens a line. Then I work outwards from it, left side first, then right. Push, lift, turn, throw. Push, lift, turn, throw.

You throw to the side the wind is going. Here that is east, mostly. I learned this the first winter in the house. I threw everything onto the west border. The wind brought half of it back across the path by lunch.

I never lift a full blade. A full blade of wet snow weighs about seven kilos. I weighed it once on the kitchen scales, a bucket at a time. Half a blade is enough. Your back knows the difference in the morning.

The last centimetre is the part that matters. The shovel leaves a thin skin that packs down under feet. So I go over the whole path again with the stiff yard broom. Then grit, a handful every metre or so, from the tin by the back door. The tin says SALT on the lid in my father's hand. It has held grit for years.
