There is also the matter of power, which the format tends to acknowledge only obliquely. However kind the manager may be, and however informal the setting, the person across the table has some say over the report's pay, reputation and future, and both of them know it. The standard advice — that managers should listen more than they speak, that the time belongs to the report, that the agenda should be theirs to set — is correct as far as it goes, and I do not wish to dismiss it; it has made a great many of these meetings considerably more humane than they once were. The difficulty is that no rule about who speaks first can dissolve an asymmetry that exists precisely because of the relationship itself. Arguably the most honest one-to-ones are those in which the imbalance is named rather than concealed, in which the manager admits, in some sense, that they are asking for candour from someone who has good reason to withhold it.

What might it mean to approach the one-to-one differently? It seems to me that the most valuable thing a manager can bring to it is not a better template but a willingness to be surprised, to treat the conversation as a place where their own assumptions about the work, the team and the person in front of them might be quietly corrected. This might mean, perhaps, tolerating silences that feel faintly awkward, or returning to a remark made weeks earlier to show that it was genuinely heard, or admitting uncertainty about a decision rather than defending it. None of this requires new tools or an elaborate process; it requires patience, and a certain readiness to hear things that are uncomfortable to hear. It is also slower, since trust of this kind accumulates only through many small occasions on which something risky was said and nothing bad followed, and there is no way to hurry it.
