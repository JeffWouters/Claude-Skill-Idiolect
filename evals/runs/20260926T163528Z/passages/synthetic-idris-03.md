What is a technical interview really for? The official answer is simple enough: an organisation needs to know whether a stranger can do the work, and the interview is the most efficient instrument it has found for finding out. Yet those of us who have sat on either side of the table know that the hour rarely feels like a measurement, and that both parties leave it with a curious sense of having taken part in something neither of them quite designed. The candidate performs competence for people who are, in some sense, performing judgement; the interviewers, for their part, perform a confidence in their own process that most of them privately doubt. It may be that the interview survives, despite a long and rather weary literature on its shortcomings, precisely because it does something other than predict who will do the work well. I suspect it is less an examination than a ritual of mutual performance, in which two groups of strangers agree to pretend, for an hour, that they already understand one another.

Consider the experience from the candidate's chair. You are asked to solve a problem you will almost certainly not meet in the job, on a whiteboard or a shared screen, while several people you have just met watch you think and take notes you cannot read. The skills the situation rewards — composure under observation, fluency in thinking aloud, the knack of seeming unhurried when you are not — are real skills, perhaps, but they are not obviously the ones the work will ask of you, which is mostly to sit quietly with a difficulty for a long time and then ask a colleague for help. Is it any wonder that so many capable engineers come away feeling that they have been assessed on their nerve rather than their judgement? The interviewers, meanwhile, are having an experience of their own; they have been pulled from their ordinary work, handed a set of questions they did not write and asked to reach a verdict about a person on the strength of a single, artificial afternoon.
