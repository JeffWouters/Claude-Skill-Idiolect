What does it mean to decline a request that is entirely reasonable, made by a colleague whose judgement we trust and whose work we admire? Most of the advice on the subject treats refusal as a problem of assertiveness, as though the difficulty lay chiefly in finding the nerve to say the word and the phrasing with which to soften it. That advice is not wrong, and a considerable number of us could stand to hear it; yet it seems to address the easier case, in which the request is unwelcome and the person making it is someone we would rather not oblige in any event. The harder case is the one in which we would genuinely like to help, in which the request is sensible and the colleague deserves a yes, and in which we decline anyway because the hours are already spoken for. I suspect that what makes this kind of refusal so uncomfortable is not the loss of the task but the small injury it seems to do to a relationship we value.

Consider the experience of being asked, late in the afternoon, whether you might look over a colleague's design before it goes to the wider group. The request is modest, the colleague is someone who has helped you more times than you could easily count, and the review would, in some sense, be a pleasure; yet you already know that the evening is promised to work you have been deferring for several days. There follows a curious interior negotiation — a rehearsal of reasons, a calculation of how much sleep might be spared, a search for some arrangement in which nothing has to be refused at all. Is it any wonder that so many of us simply say yes and then do both things badly? The yes costs nothing at the moment it is given; the cost arrives later, spread thinly across the review, the deferred work and the person who has to live with the consequences of both, who is very often ourselves.
