Most advice about one-on-one meetings tells the manager to step back and let the report set the agenda. It is good advice, as far as it goes, and I would not want to discourage anyone from following it, yet it rests on an assumption that seems to me rather optimistic: that the imbalance between the two people in the room can be dissolved by a change of procedure. It cannot be, because one of them writes the other's review, shapes their assignments and has some say in their future; the other knows this, and no arrangement of the agenda will make them forget it. Letting the report lead helps, but it helps in the way that good manners help at a formal dinner, easing the occasion without changing what kind of occasion it is.

What might work better is simply to name the gap. A manager who says openly that the relationship is unequal, and that this probably makes certain things harder to say, has not solved anything; they have, however, made the difficulty something the two people can look at together rather than something each of them must quietly manage alone. That is a modest shift, but it tends to change the character of the conversations that follow.

The rest depends less on technique than on conduct sustained over many meetings. It means welcoming correction rather than merely tolerating it, and letting it be seen that being wrong in front of a report is not an injury; it means allowing pauses to run a little longer than is comfortable, since the thing someone most needs to say is often the thing they take longest to reach; it means returning, weeks later, to a remark that seemed to pass unnoticed. It also means admitting doubt, which managers are perhaps least inclined to do. None of this produces trust quickly, since trust of this kind accumulates gradually, through evidence rather than assurance, and a manager who expects it sooner may be mistaking politeness for candour.
