Most teams I have worked with keep a backlog that has grown well beyond anything they could reasonably hope to finish, and it seems to me that the reason is less practical than emotional. A long list offers a certain comfort to those who keep it. Every request that has ever been made remains visible somewhere, and the mere fact of its being recorded allows us to feel that nothing has been forgotten, even when nothing is being done about it either. Cleanup is proposed from time to time, usually at the start of a quarter or after a planning session that has gone badly, and yet it rarely happens. Closing a ticket, it turns out, feels uncomfortably like going back on a promise, and few of us enjoy being the person who reneges.

The difficulty is that the backlog, in this state, is not quite telling the truth. It presents itself as a queue of work that will eventually be done, when in practice much of it is a record of ideas that were noted once and never seriously considered again. Perhaps a more truthful alternative would begin by separating these two things: the commitments the team genuinely intends to keep, and the ideas it has merely written down so that they are not lost. The first list ought to be short enough that everyone on the team could describe it from memory; the second can be as long as it likes, provided nobody mistakes it for a plan. Stale items might then be closed deliberately rather than left to drift, each with a brief reason attached, so that anyone who comes looking later can see that the decision was made and not simply avoided.

The harder part, I suspect, is telling the people who asked for something that it will not happen, and telling them early rather than letting the silence do the work. This feels unkind in the moment. It is arguably far kinder than the alternative, in which a request sits unanswered for months and the requester gradually learns, without anyone saying so, that it was never going to be picked up.
