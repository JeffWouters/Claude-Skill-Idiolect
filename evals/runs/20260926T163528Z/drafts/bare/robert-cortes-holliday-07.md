Philadelphia keeps a good deal of her charm tucked away where the visitor, hurrying along the broad thoroughfares, is not likely to stumble upon it.  She resembles an elderly gentlewoman who wears her plainest gown to market and reserves her lace for the parlour.  The lace is in the little side streets.  You must go searching for them, and even then you will not discover them all.

Camac Street is the one that everybody hears about sooner or later.  It is an artists' lane, and it has been restored, which is a hazardous operation to perform upon a street; but it has been accomplished with taste, and the result is pleasant without being precious. South Carlisle Street is another matter.  I have inquired of Philadelphians about it, and they have regarded me with courteous bewilderment, for they had lived in the city all their lives and never heard of it.  I will therefore set down plainly where it is.  [location needed: the streets it runs between]  Having found it, you may well wonder whether you have found a street at all or merely an interval between houses which somebody neglected to fill up.  It is very narrow, so narrow that a gentleman of generous proportions might hesitate before entering.  But it is worth the squeeze.  For here, within a space of a few paces, is Philadelphia in miniature, with the classic doorways, the old brick, the shutters folded back against the walls, and upon the doors the knockers.  It is all present, the whole architectural tradition of the town, arranged in a row as though for inspection.  Nobody seems to come to inspect it, and that, I suspect, is part of its attraction.
