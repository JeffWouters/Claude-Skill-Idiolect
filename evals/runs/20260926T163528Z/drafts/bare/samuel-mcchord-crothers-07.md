Nature repeats herself without apology. The sun rises every morning in very much the manner of the morning before, and the seasons return in their accustomed order, and nobody thinks of complaining that the performance lacks originality. The reason is that we cannot get away, for there is no other sun to go to, and a man who is weary of the spring must wait for it all the same. The writer enjoys no such advantage. His audience is at liberty, and a reader who finds that he has heard a thing before has only to close the book. It is this independence of the reader, rather than any law of art, that makes the writer so anxious never to say the same thing twice.

Yet I think the anxiety is often misplaced. The critics are fond of reproaching a novelist because his characters repeat themselves; the comic figure, they complain, is forever making the same joke and falling into the same absurdity, and they take this for a symptom of poverty in the author. I am not sure that the public agrees with them. What the public requires of an old friend is not that he should surprise it, but that he should come back exactly as he went away.

This was demonstrated at a public reading by one delighted listener, who greeted the return of a familiar comic figure with a pleasure that no new invention could have given him. He did not want novelty, but his friend.

The performer on the stage perhaps understands this better than the critic in his study. He knows that the audience has come for the familiar thing, and that it would be disappointed if he offered something original in its place.
