The technical interview presents itself as a test of ability, and both parties generally speak of it in those terms. The candidate is there to demonstrate what they can do; the interviewer is there to find out. It seems to me, however, that this description flatters the occasion. What actually takes place is something closer to a piece of mutual role-play, in which each side performs a version of itself that it believes the other expects to see, and both agree, more or less tacitly, not to mention how artificial the whole arrangement is. That the practice persists is not, I suspect, because anyone is convinced it measures what it claims to measure, but because it serves other purposes: it gives the process a recognisable shape, it allows decisions to be defended afterwards, and it spares everyone the harder question of what a better alternative would look like.

If we consider what the format actually rewards, it becomes clear that an applicant who remains calm under scrutiny, who can think aloud without faltering while a stranger watches and takes notes, will generally do well, and there is nothing wrong with that quality in itself. It is, however, rather different from what most of the work actually requires. The job, in most cases, is patient and collaborative: it involves returning to a problem over several days, asking colleagues for help, reading what others have written, and changing their minds without anyone keeping score. Very little of this can be observed in an hour, and some of it might even count against a candidate who displayed it too openly in the interview room.

The position of the interviewer is hardly easier, since they are asked to form a judgement about the likely conduct of another person over months or years from a single session that has been deliberately contrived, under conditions that neither of them would choose in ordinary working life. Perhaps the most honest thing to say is that interviewers do the best they can with evidence that is thin by design, and that the confidence with which their conclusions are later reported is not always matched by the evidence on which they rest.
