There is no accounting for the manner in which a writer's productivity comes and goes.  It arrives when it has no particular business arriving, and it departs, like a visitor who has suddenly remembered another engagement, at precisely the moment when it is most urgently required.  I have in mind a writer who once undertook a series of articles for payment.  It was a modest enough undertaking, so many pieces at so much apiece.  But the thing expanded.  It expanded into a book, and the book took possession of him; it consumed his days and a considerable portion of his nights, and he did not appear to mind in the least.  Nobody had commissioned a book.  Nobody was expecting one.

Some while afterwards the same man accepted a commission which was, by every reasonable measure, an uncommonly easy one.  Here, you would say, was something he could accomplish with his left hand.  He did not accomplish it.  For months it stalled.  His publishers wrote to him, and he wrote back to them, and what he wrote back was excuses.  I gather they were rather more ingenious than anything else he was producing at the time.  The book which nobody wanted had cost him, apparently, nothing but his entire existence; the easy assignment which somebody did want was more than he could bring himself to touch.

A former flatmate of mine was, if anything, a stranger case.  When he had lived for a period as the moralists would have us live, early to bed, plain food, fresh air and everything else of that description, he could not put together two sentences that he cared to preserve.  He sat before his paper in excellent condition and produced nothing whatever.  It was following nights of excess that he did his finest work.  He would approach his table, somewhat the worse for wear, and the words would come as though they had been patiently waiting for him.  I do not offer this as a recommendation.  I merely report it.

Has any psychologist explained such cases?  Perhaps one has.  If so, I should very much like to read him, and I should be interested, too, to discover what he makes of the remainder of us.
