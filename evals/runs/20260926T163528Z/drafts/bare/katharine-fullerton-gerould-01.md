It is the fashion to-day to speak of the older ages as if they had merely been wrong. They were wrong, of course, about a great many things; their notions of nature were, many of them, quite absurd, and [example needed: a mistaken belief of an earlier age] is only one of a long list. But I do not know that being wrong about the stars ever kept a people from building well. The men who were so mistaken about the heavens made an art we cannot equal, framed laws we still live under, and won, at great cost, a liberty we have grown careless of. Their science has gone the way of all science; their art and their law remain. One may smile at their physics, if one likes. It is harder to smile at their works.

What, then, is culture? I take it to be nothing more mysterious than this: the habit of keeping in view what men have already learned, so that we need not learn it all over again. Progress is not a matter of throwing things away. The man who knows nothing of what went before him is not free of the past; he is simply condemned to repeat it, and generally to repeat it badly. He discovers, with a great flourish, some truth that was old when [example needed: an ancient thinker] was young, and he calls it new. We have all met him (he is very common just now!) and we have all been a little tired by him. Every generation that forgets its inheritance must spend its strength in doing over the work its fathers had done--and it has so much less strength to spare for the work that is really its own.

Nor is it enough to say that the moderns have surpassed the ancients. Perhaps they have, in some things; but one cannot surpass what one has never measured. To go beyond a thinker, you must first know how far he went. The young man who tells me he has outgrown [example needed: an earlier thinker] has usually never read him; and that is not outgrowing, it is only missing. We shall go further than our forebears only by standing where they stood.
