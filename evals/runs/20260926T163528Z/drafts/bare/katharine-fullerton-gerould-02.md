We are told, from the nursery onward, that we must never tell a lie. It is an excellent rule for the nursery; but it was made, I think, by people who had never been asked an impertinent question at a dinner-table. For the trouble with the impertinent question is that there is no honest way out of it. You may refuse to answer, of course; you may declare, with all the dignity you can muster and a good deal more than you actually feel, that the matter is nobody's concern but your own. And the questioner, who is not a fool (the prying sort seldom are!), will draw his own conclusion from your silence, and it will generally be the right one. A refusal is very often only a slower way of telling the truth.

Now I cannot see that any one who has no rightful claim to a piece of knowledge is owed the truth about it. The man who pries into what does not concern him is not asking a favour; he is trying to take something that is not his. It is a kind of theft, and a mean kind, since what he steals cannot be given back. We do not think it wicked to lock our doors against the burglar, or to mislead him, if we can, about where the silver is kept; and I confess I do not understand why we should consider it wicked to mislead the gossip, whose intrusion is of precisely the same character. Very well, then. He has put himself outside the bargain on which truthfulness rests; and he can hardly complain if the bargain does not protect him.

But there is a distinction to be drawn here, and it is not a small one. To lie in order to save one's own face is another matter altogether. If the thing he is hunting does one no credit, one must endure its discovery; vanity is not a good enough reason for deceit, and I should not like to defend the person who thought it was. It is quite different when the secret belongs to some one else--to a friend who trusted us, or to some one who cannot speak for himself. Then, I think, the lie may be forgiven. It is not told for our own sake, and that makes all the difference.
