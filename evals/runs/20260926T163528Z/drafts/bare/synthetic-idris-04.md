Most of what is written about saying no concerns confidence. We are encouraged to be firm, to stop apologising, and to recognise that requests from other people do not automatically become our obligations. This is useful advice when the request is one we never wanted in the first place, and there are certainly situations in which it applies. It seems to me, though, that it addresses the easier case. The harder one is the request that is entirely reasonable, from someone we respect, which we would genuinely like to help with and simply do not have the time for.

Suppose a colleague whose work you trust asks late in the day whether you might review a design they are about to share more widely. It is a sensible thing to ask; you are well placed to help, and you would probably enjoy doing it. The difficulty is that the evening is already committed, and not to anything that could easily be moved. No amount of confidence resolves this, because the problem is not that you lack the nerve to refuse but that refusing feels like a small betrayal of something you value: the colleague, the work, and perhaps your own sense of being the kind of person who helps.

Agreeing therefore seems the generous choice, and in the moment it usually is the easier one. The cost of agreeing, however, does not disappear; it is merely deferred, and it tends to fall later on someone, quite often on yourself. The other commitment of the evening is kept badly or not at all, the review is done with less care than it deserved, or the tiredness carries into the following day and is paid for there. Perhaps the more honest kindness is to say plainly that you would like to help and cannot do so tonight, and to offer what you genuinely can, even if that is rather less than was asked. A colleague worth respecting will usually understand, and the refusal might in the end serve the work better than a reluctant yes.
