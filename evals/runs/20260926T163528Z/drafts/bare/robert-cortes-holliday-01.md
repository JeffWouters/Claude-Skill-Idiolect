I like hotels.  I like them more, I suspect, than most of the people who inhabit them.  Listen to the conversation of any lobby and you will hear one of two things.  Either the guest is complaining of the service, the deliberation of the elevator, the indifference of the clerk, the coffee that arrived cold; or he is boasting of his hotel as though he had personally constructed it.  It is the finest in town.  The beds are the most comfortable in the country.  Nobody of any discernment stops anywhere else.  I have never been able to do either.  A hotel seems to me an agreeable, somewhat theatrical arrangement, where one is attended by strangers and asked no questions, and I am grateful for it without feeling any obligation to defend it.

I once knew a young man, extremely wealthy, who carried this appreciation much further than I have ever ventured.  He was not attached to any particular hotel.  He was devoted to hotels in general, and he could not endure being bored by one of them.  So he moved every day.  He would breakfast in one, lunch somewhere about the city, and by evening his trunks would be following him into another lobby, with another clerk, another prospect of another street.  Variety, he explained, was the entire point.  Why should a man with the means of living everywhere be content to live in one place?  I did not have an answer for him; I doubt there is one.

Some years afterward I heard that he was in prison in France.  [detail needed: how he came to be there]  It was, naturally, a considerable shock to his friends.  But the letters he wrote them were not what anybody had expected.  He did not complain of his cell.  He wrote, instead, in warm and particular praise of the food.  He had discovered, at last, an establishment that satisfied him--and one he couldn't exchange every morning.
