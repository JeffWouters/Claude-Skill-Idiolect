My dear friend,

I have read your novel with the attention it deserves, and I must tell you frankly what I think is the matter with it. The events are loose enough, but that is not the trouble. The trouble is that nothing which happens to your hero seems to matter, because your hero himself matters so little that the reader cannot be persuaded to take his fortunes more seriously than he would take the fortunes of a stranger overheard in a railway carriage. I followed him with a mild good will that never rose to anxiety. A small man may have large misfortunes; we do not believe in them.

Consider how the good biographies manage this. The biographer of a great man has no need to invent significance, for his hero is fighting for something that was worth fighting for, and every trivial incident takes on the color of the cause. We read with interest what he had for breakfast on the morning of the battle. Without the battle we should not read it at all.

What I would urge upon you is that you choose your protagonist as a prudent man chooses an investment. You are about to put a considerable part of your life into him, and you had better see that he is the kind of security which will pay a return. Take a major figure. Let him be one who is fighting for a great cause and who has something to lose besides his own comfort, so that your realism will have something substantial to be realistic about. The small details of his life will then not be small, since they will be the details of a life that counted.

If you cannot find such a man, or cannot quite bring yourself to believe in him, there remains an honorable way out. Turn your material into a short story. A short story may be about a little man, since it does not ask the reader to stay with him long enough to discover how little he is. It is a good deal kinder to both of you.

Believe me, with all good wishes for the next book,

Yours faithfully,
