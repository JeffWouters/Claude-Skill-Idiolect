The snow came down upon New York with a thoroughness that the city does not as a rule permit to anything but the rush hour.  By evening the avenues had gone, and in their place lay long, soft, rolling drifts in which the old landmarks stood about like lost strangers.  The bus companies, to do them justice, had seen what was coming.  Their buses had been fitted out with plows, and they sallied forth to do battle.  It was a gallant sight, and a brief one.  One after another the plow-fitted buses put their noses into the drifts and stopped, and there they stayed, snorting and helpless.  Upon the sidewalks the bystanders gathered, as bystanders in this city will gather for any trouble which is not their own, and cheered.  Whom they were cheering I cannot say; the snow, it may be.

The next morning the town had gone back to work, after a fashion.  Lanes had been cleared down the middle of the main streets, and through these the traffic crawled in single file, taxicab behind truck behind limousine, with the slow dignity of a funeral.  The statues had all put on hats in the night.  The lions in front of the library wore tall white caps, cocked at a rakish angle, and looked rather pleased with themselves.

But the most instructive sight of all was the commuter.  He came up out of the subway shod in huge overshoes, bulky and buckled, and he walked in them without the least trace of shame.  Here is a real revolution in manners.  There was a time, and it is not so very long ago, when a man would have gone about half blind rather than be seen in spectacles, and would have endured wet feet to the edge of pneumonia rather than own to galoshes.  Such things were hidden, apologised for, whisked off in the hall.  Now they're shown.  The glasses are worn large and plain upon the nose; the galoshes go clumping down the avenue with a certain swagger.  The very things that fashion once told us to hide, fashion now tells us to exhibit.  [example needed: a particular commuter or pair of overshoes observed]
