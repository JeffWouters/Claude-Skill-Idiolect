Why the man who buries us should ever have been called an undertaker is a question to which I have never found a satisfactory answer.  An undertaker, by the plain sense of the word, is one who undertakes something.  Everybody undertakes something.  The physician undertakes to cure you, the lawyer undertakes to keep you out of gaol, or at any rate to get you out again; either of them has as good a claim to the title as the gentleman in the black gloves.  Yet the word went, by some obscure process of language, to the last of these, and there it stuck.  [example needed: when or how the word took on this meaning]

Having stuck, it gathered about itself a curious set of associations.  The undertaker became a figure of fun, and a gloomy figure of fun at that.  He was the long face at the foot of the stairs, the man with the professional sigh, the stock character of comic sketches.  Nobody, I imagine, was particularly anxious to be one at dinner.

The trade then did what trades will do when their name has grown shabby.  It got a new one.  The undertaker became the funeral director, very much as other callings have promoted themselves with a grander title.  [example needed: other upgraded job titles the writer has in mind]  "Funeral director" has a pleasant, executive sound; it suggests a desk, a telephone, an office.  It suggests that somebody is in charge, which on such occasions is a comfort.

Already, however, the funeral director is perhaps a little out of date.  The fashionable word now is mortician.  It has a rather scientific air, and I suppose that is the point of it.  Whether it will last is another matter.  Words of this kind wear out quickly; the fresh paint soon has the old associations showing through it.  And I should not be surprised if, in a generation or so, somebody in the trade proposes, as a bold and refreshing novelty, that we go back to calling it undertaking.
