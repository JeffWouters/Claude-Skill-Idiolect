It is a hazardous thing to say what an American is. The people of this country have come from so many lands, and have scattered themselves over so many different regions, that almost any statement about them is contradicted before the ink is dry. A generalization that fits the one will be resented by the other, and with good reason.

Yet there is a way of getting at the matter, if we do not insist upon a precision which the subject will not bear. We can look at the caricatures. Every nation has its figure in the cartoons, and the figure is never a portrait of any living person, but it tells us something about what the people like to think of themselves. The American in these drawings is not the merchant of the seaboard, nor the scholar, nor the recent immigrant. He is a man from the backwoods, lank and weathered and a little uncouth. Americans of every origin, whatever their own manners and whatever the country of their grandfathers, are willing to accept him as their representative and are secretly rather proud of him.

What is curious is how differently this figure has been received. A refined American poet, a man of letters and of delicate tastes, could look upon him with genuine affection. The crudeness did not offend him, for he saw beneath it something familiar and friendly. The backwoodsman slapped him on the back, and he took it as a mark of fellowship. On the other hand, an English novelist who visited the country found the same type quite repellent. What struck him most was the boasting. These patriots, he thought, talked too loudly of their country's greatness and listened too little to anything else.

Both were right. The poet saw the neighbor and the novelist saw the stranger, and it is the same man who is a good neighbor and a trying guest.
