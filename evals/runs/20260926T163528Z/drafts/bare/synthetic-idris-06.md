Anyone who has spent time in a software team will have witnessed, and probably taken part in, an argument about what something should be called. These disputes are often dismissed as trivial, the kind of distraction that consumes an afternoon while the real work waits, and there is some justice in that view. It seems to me, however, that they persist precisely because they are not trivial at all. Whoever's name is adopted gains a quiet and lasting influence over how everyone else thinks about the thing, since a name carries with it a particular account of what the thing is for and where its boundaries lie. The person who named the service, the module or the concept has, in a sense, written the first chapter of its history, and later contributors will tend to read everything that follows in the light of that chapter.

Conventions help, up to a point, and a shared agreement about casing, prefixes and the order of words can remove a whole category of squabbles; teams that have one are generally spared the most tedious of these debates. What a convention cannot do is settle purpose. It can tell us how to write a name once we have decided what the thing is; it cannot tell us what the thing is, and that, I suspect, is usually what the argument was about in the first place.

Perhaps the more useful approach is to treat naming debates as what they often are: discussions of scope in disguise. Rather than asking which name sounds better, we might ask what each candidate excludes, since a good name draws a line as much as it describes what lies inside it. We might also check, before anyone commits to a choice, whether the people in the room actually share an understanding of the thing being named, because a disagreement about words is sometimes the first visible sign of a disagreement about meaning. When the thing itself changes, as things in software generally do, it may be worth renaming it despite the cost, which is real but rarely as great as the cost of a name that has quietly stopped telling the truth.
