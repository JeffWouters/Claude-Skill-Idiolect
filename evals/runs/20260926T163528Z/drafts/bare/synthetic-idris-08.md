Much of the work of a distributed team is carried out in writing, and it is easy to underestimate how much of our ordinary tact depends on things that writing cannot carry. In a room, a proposal arrives with a tone of voice, a pause, a glance around the table to see whether anyone looks uneasy; in a message, it arrives alone, and the reader must supply all of that for themselves. The usual advice is to write more thoroughly, to anticipate objections and explain the reasoning in full, and this is sound as far as it goes. What it tends to overlook is that a thorough message can read as a closed decision. The more complete the argument, the less room it seems to leave for anyone else, and a colleague reading it some hours later may reasonably conclude that the matter has already been settled without them.

It seems to me that the remedy lies less in the length of our messages than in what we choose to leave visibly unfinished. We might share the questions we have not yet answered alongside the conclusions we have reached, so that others can see where their contribution would actually count. Decisions made while part of the team is asleep could be treated as tentative by default, open to revision once everyone has had a chance to read them, rather than presented as settled simply because the people who happened to be awake agreed. Where a meeting is genuinely necessary, the awkward hours might be rotated, so that the inconvenience does not always fall on the same people, who would otherwise have every reason to suspect that their time matters rather less than that of their colleagues.

Perhaps the most useful thing to keep in mind, though, is simply that each reader is alone when they read. There is nobody beside them to soften a curt phrase or to reassure them that a question was not meant as criticism; there is only the text, and whatever mood they bring to it. Writing with that solitary reader in view is, I suspect, the better part of what inclusion in a distributed team actually requires.
