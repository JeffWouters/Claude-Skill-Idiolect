There is no better observatory for the study of Providence than the top of a London omnibus. From that elevation the street below presents the appearance of a tragedy perpetually on the point of happening. The pedestrians are diminutive creatures, and they move with a confidence entirely unwarranted by their circumstances. A woman steps from the curb with her attention fixed upon a shop window; a cab bears down upon her from one direction and a dray from the other, and I brace myself for the catastrophe. It does not come. When I look again she is on the opposite pavement, and cab and dray have continued upon their errands, unconscious that they have narrowly missed becoming instruments of destiny.

I have watched this spectacle for many miles. Every one of them, as seen from above, is doomed. Not one of them is ever struck.

The explanation lies in the point of view. If I were to descend from my eminence and take my place beside the driver, I should see what is concealed from the upper deck: the little sidestep, the quickened pace, the sudden halt of the horse, and all those last-second escapes which at my altitude are swallowed up in the general confusion. What appears a miracle from above is only good footwork at the level of the street.

It is a comfortable reflection, and I am inclined to carry it beyond the traffic of London. We look down upon a great many lives, our own among them, and we see nothing ahead but disaster, because from where we sit we cannot observe the narrow margin that remains, nor the assistance that arrives when there seems to be no time left for any assistance whatever. There is an old verse about a rider who fell from his horse and yet found mercy on the way to the ground. Rescue need not be early to be in time. The last instant is still an instant, and a great deal may be accomplished in it.
