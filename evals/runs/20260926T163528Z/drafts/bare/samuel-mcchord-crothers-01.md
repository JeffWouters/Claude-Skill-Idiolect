There was once a theologian whose reasoning I have always admired, not for its conclusions but for its manners. He built his argument with great care, one proposition resting upon another, and at the critical point he added a small qualifying clause. It was a modest thing, a few words of caution tucked into the middle of a sentence. Yet it saved him. Whenever a stubborn fact came down the road, his logic stepped politely aside and let it pass. The two never met, and so neither was the worse for the encounter.

I have thought of that caveat when listening to those who reason about great nations. The sprawling empire and the continental republic are, on paper, very irrational things. They are too large to be governed, too various to agree, and too scattered to be defended. The imagined commonwealth of the philosophers is a much tidier affair. It is small enough to be seen from a hilltop; its citizens know one another and share the same opinions, and its laws follow from first principles as neatly as the steps of a proof. By every rule of right reasoning it ought to last, and the unwieldy giant ought to fall apart.

But the facts have a way of declining the invitation. The tidy commonwealth remains where it was born, in a book. The improbable nation goes on, and not only goes on but grows stronger, as if it had never been told how improbable it was. Perhaps we should add a caveat of our own and say that large nations are irrational, as a rule.

I would apply this to America. Her dangers are plain enough, and nobody need hunt for them. They are printed every morning. A people who have survived so many obvious perils must have strengths that are not so obvious, and it is these hidden resources that the gloomy reasoner leaves out of his account. That the house has not fallen is itself a kind of evidence about the foundations.

I would not, however, silence the prophets. Their warnings are valuable even when their predictions fail, and perhaps most valuable then, for it may be that the warning is the reason for the failure. A nation that is told it is in danger has at least a chance to look about it.
