There is a particular silence after snow falls overnight. The street is muffled, the cars are still, and the first sound of the morning is often the scrape of a shovel on someone's path. This morning it was mine.

The shovel I use is [shovel type needed]. There is an older alternative in the shed, inherited from [whom needed], which [description needed]. It still works, but for most snowfalls I reach for the newer one.

Before starting, dress for the job rather than for the cold. Shovelling is warm work, so layers that can be shed are better than one heavy coat. Gloves that keep their grip when damp and boots with a good tread matter more than anything else, because the path will be slippery before it is clear.

Start at the house end. It is tempting to begin at the gate, but clearing outward from the door means you are never trudging back over snow you have already packed down, and the most used stretch is done first if you are interrupted. Cut a single line down the centre of the path first, one shovel wide. That gives you a firm footing and a clear edge to work from. Then work outward from that line towards each side, pushing the snow away from the middle.

When you throw snow, throw it downwind. Throwing into the wind just brings it back into your face and onto the path you have cleared. Pay attention to where it lands, too: not onto the neighbour's path, and not in a heap that will melt and refreeze across your own.

Take partial loads. A full shovel of wet snow is far heavier than it looks, and it is the lifting and twisting that hurts the back. Half a blade at a time, bending the knees and turning the whole body rather than the waist, is slower in the moment and much kinder the next day.

When the bulk is gone, sweep the path with a stiff broom to take off the thin layer the shovel leaves behind. That last film is what turns to ice overnight. Finally, spread grit along the cleared path, a little more on any slope or step. Then go inside, and listen to the quiet again.
