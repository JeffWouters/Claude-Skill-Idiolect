The snow came down on New York hard enough to stop the city in its own streets. The buses had been fitted with plows for exactly this kind of storm, and for a while they pushed on bravely. Then, one after another, they ran into drifts they could not shift and simply stuck there, engines running, wheels turning to no purpose. Nobody seemed especially upset. A crowd gathered around each stranded bus as if it were a show put on for their benefit, and when a driver managed to rock his machine an inch forward the bystanders cheered him the way they might cheer a runner on the last lap. When he slid back again, they cheered that too.

By the next morning the city had dug itself narrow lanes, and traffic went through them single file, crawling. Cars that would ordinarily have fought for every foot of the avenue now waited politely behind one another, since there was nowhere else to go. The statues had come through the night with snow caps on their heads. The library lions sat under theirs with a patient, slightly foolish dignity, like large animals who had been dressed up by children and decided not to object. [example needed: another statue]

The commuters were dressed for the occasion, and the occasion called for overshoes. Everyone was clumping along in them, bulky and black and unlovely, and nobody seemed in the least embarrassed. That is the real change. There was a time when anyone with a claim to style hid such things. Glasses were taken off before a photograph and galoshes were left at home even when the gutters ran, on the principle that it was better to be half blind and wet than seen to be sensible. Somewhere along the way the principle turned around. Glasses became something to choose with care and wear on purpose, and the overshoe has followed them out into the open. People now look at one another's galoshes on a snowy morning the way they once looked at hats, and it is the person without them who looks as though he did not quite think it through.
