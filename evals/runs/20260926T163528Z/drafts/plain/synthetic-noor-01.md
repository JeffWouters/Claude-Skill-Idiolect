A basic white loaf needs very little. Strong white flour, [quantity needed]; water, [quantity needed]; fresh or dried yeast, [quantity needed]; and salt, [quantity needed]. That is the whole list. I buy my flour from [where the flour is bought needed] and keep it in [how and where it is stored needed], which matters more than it sounds, because flour that has sat open in a damp cupboard never behaves quite the same.

Before anything goes in, I warm the bowl. A cold bowl pulls the heat out of the dough and slows the yeast right at the start, when you want it waking up. A splash of hot water swirled round and tipped out is enough.

The water itself I judge by feel rather than with a thermometer. Run it over the inside of your wrist: it should feel barely warm, closer to nothing than to a bath. Too hot and you kill the yeast before it has done any work; too cold and you will be waiting all afternoon.

Salt and yeast go into the bowl on opposite sides, with the flour between them. Salt dropped straight onto yeast draws moisture out of it and holds it back, so I keep them apart until the water arrives and everything is mixed together at once.

Then comes the kneading, which is where most of the bread is really made. Tip the dough out onto a clean surface. Push it away from you with the heel of your hand, fold it back over itself, turn it a quarter, and do it again. Keep that rhythm going for [length of time needed]. At first it will be rough, shaggy and sticky, and it will cling to your fingers.

This is the moment people reach for the flour bag, and it is a mistake. Every handful you add changes the recipe, drying the dough and giving you a tighter, heavier loaf. The stickiness is not a problem to be fixed. Keep working, and as the gluten develops the dough will tighten, smooth out and come away from the surface on its own.
