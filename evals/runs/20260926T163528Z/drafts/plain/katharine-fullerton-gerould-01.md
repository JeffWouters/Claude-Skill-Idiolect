It is easy, standing on the shoulders of modern science, to look back at earlier ages and see mostly error. Their astronomers had the heavens wrong, their physicians misread the body, and their natural philosophers explained the world with notions we now teach only as curiosities. From there it is a short step to the conclusion that what they thought is no longer worth our time. That conclusion is a mistake.

Consider what those same civilisations managed to build while holding their mistaken science. They produced art that still moves people who know nothing of the beliefs behind it. They worked out systems of law that we continue to draw on, and ideas of liberty that we still defend. None of this depended on getting the orbit of a planet right. Wisdom about how people should live together, what is beautiful, and what is just is not cancelled by a wrong theory of matter. A society can be deeply mistaken about nature and still see clearly about human beings.

This is what culture is for. Culture, properly understood, means keeping the accumulated experience of the past in view, so that each generation does not have to start from nothing. Progress that forgets where it came from tends to repeat itself: it spends its energy rediscovering what was already known, making old mistakes in new clothes, and congratulating itself on insights that earlier thinkers had and wrote down. Keeping the record open is not nostalgia. It is simply efficient.

There is a further point. We like to say that we have surpassed the thinkers of earlier ages, and in many fields we have. But surpassing someone requires knowing what they understood. You cannot go beyond a position you have never grasped; you can only ignore it, and ignoring is not the same as outgrowing. The person who dismisses the past without studying it has not moved past it at all. He has merely stopped looking, and is more likely than anyone to walk back into the very ground he believes he left behind.
