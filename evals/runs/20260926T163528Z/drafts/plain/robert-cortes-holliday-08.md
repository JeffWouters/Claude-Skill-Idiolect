Nobody seems quite sure how the undertaker came to be the man who buries us. The word itself promises nothing of the kind. To undertake is simply to take something on, and a great many people take things on for a living. A doctor undertakes to cure you; a lawyer undertakes your case. Either of them could have been called an undertaker with as much reason, and perhaps more. Yet somewhere along the line the word settled on the trade that deals with the dead, and there it stayed. Once it had settled, it began to collect associations, and very few of them were flattering. The undertaker became a stock figure, long faced and dressed in black, rubbing his hands and measuring the company with his eye. He was gloomy, and because he was gloomy he was funny. Writers and comedians found him irresistible. The word could hardly be said without a certain smile, and a trade that asks people to trust it at the worst moment of their lives cannot afford to be smiled at.

So the trade did what many trades have done, and gave itself a better name. The undertaker became the funeral director. It is the same move that turned other ordinary occupations into something that sounds grander on a business card [example needed: other upgraded job titles]. The new title does its job. It is calm, it is respectable, and it suggests someone who organizes and manages rather than someone who carries and digs. Nobody has yet found a way to make it comic.

Even that, it seems, is not the end of it. "Mortician" is now the fashionable word, with its faint ring of the laboratory and the profession, as though the work were a branch of medicine. Whether it will last, or whether it too will gather its own jokes and have to be replaced, remains to be seen. The work itself has not changed much. Only the name keeps moving, a step ahead of the laughter.
