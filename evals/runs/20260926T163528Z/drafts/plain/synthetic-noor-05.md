Every autumn the compost heap gets turned, and every autumn I am reminded why. A heap is not a pile of rubbish left to rot on its own; it is a crowd of small organisms that need air to do their work. Pack the material down, starve it of oxygen, and the busy, warm decay stalls into something slow, wet and sour.

This year the system is two bays side by side. The job is simple in outline: fork everything out of the full bay into the empty one, loosening it as it goes, so the inside of the heap ends up on the outside and air gets back into the middle.

When a heap is working, the inside tells you at once. The finished material from the bottom goes through a sieve first. What passes through is ready for the beds; what stays behind goes back onto the new heap to carry on breaking down. As the new heap is built up, I water any layers that are dry, because a heap needs moisture as well as air, roughly as damp as a wrung-out cloth. Then it gets a cover, to keep the warmth in and stop the winter rain from soaking it through.
