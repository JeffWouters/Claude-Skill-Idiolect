Few arguments in a software team last as long, or feel as petty from the outside, as arguments about names. What should this service be called? Is it an account, a customer or a tenant? Is the thing we ship a feature, a module or a product? Onlookers roll their eyes, but the people involved rarely feel they are arguing about nothing, and they are right.

A name is not just a label. Whoever's name is adopted gains lasting influence. The word they chose shapes how others think about the thing, what gets added to it, what seems to belong elsewhere, and who is consulted when it changes. Months later, new colleagues learn the concept through that word and inherit the frame that came with it. Winning a naming debate can quietly mean winning a design debate that nobody explicitly held.

Teams often try to escape these fights with conventions. Style guides say how to case identifiers, which prefixes to use, whether to prefer nouns or verbs. Conventions help. They curb the squabbles about form and let people spend their energy elsewhere. What they cannot do is settle purpose. No rule about casing will tell you whether the thing you are naming is meant to handle one job or three.

That is why I find it more useful to treat naming debates as scope discussions. When two people disagree about a name, they usually disagree about what the thing is for. So ask a different question: what does this name exclude? A good name draws a boundary. If a proposed name would comfortably cover responsibilities the team does not want the component to take on, that is worth knowing before the code grows.

Then check shared understanding. Ask a few people to describe, in their own words, what the name means. If the answers differ, the problem is not the word but the missing agreement behind it, and choosing a word will not fix that on its own.

Finally, accept that names age. When the thing changes, rename it, even though renaming costs effort, touches many files and irritates people. A name that no longer fits misleads every reader who trusts it. Paying the cost once is usually cheaper than paying for the confusion over and over.
