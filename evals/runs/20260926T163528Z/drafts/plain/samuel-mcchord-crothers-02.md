Being a good citizen used to be a fairly modest undertaking. You obeyed the law, paid what you owed, and minded your own business. Do that and your neighbours thought well of you. Nobody expected you to have opinions about everything, still less to act on all of them. Respectability was within reach of anyone willing to keep their head down and their accounts in order.

That arrangement has quietly come to an end. The conscientious person today looks around and finds a whole crowd of new duties waiting. There are obligations to [example needed], to [example needed], to causes and communities that previous generations never had to think about. Each one on its own is reasonable. Most of them are probably right. The trouble is that they have arrived all at once, like a pile of debts that happen to fall due in the same week. You could settle any one of them. You cannot settle all of them, and every one you leave unpaid is noted.

What strikes me most is the cost. We talk a great deal about how expensive it has become to live: the price of housing, of food, of the plain necessities. But I suspect ethical living has grown costlier still. Being good now takes time, attention, money and a kind of constant vigilance that the old model of citizenship never demanded. The material necessities at least have a price you can look up. The moral ones keep expanding, and there is no receipt that says you have done enough.

And that is where the change really bites. Under the old rules, you were judged on what you did. If you kept the law and did no harm, you had passed. Under the new rules you are judged on what you neglect. There is always a critic ready to point out the duty you overlooked, the cause you did not support, the obligation you failed to see. The standard is no longer whether you behaved well but whether you did everything, and nobody does everything.

I do not think the new duties are false. Many of them were overdue. But we should be honest that we have made good citizenship much harder than it used to be, and that the people most likely to feel the weight are the conscientious ones who take it seriously.
