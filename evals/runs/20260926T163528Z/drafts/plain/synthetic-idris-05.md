Most teams I have seen carry a backlog far larger than they could ever finish. Hundreds of items sit there, some untouched for so long that nobody remembers who asked for them or why. Everyone knows the list is fiction, yet it keeps growing, and the periodic promise to clean it up quietly fades.

The reason is less about process than about feeling. A long list comforts the people who keep it. It says that nothing has been forgotten, that every request was heard, that the team is busy and in demand. Deleting an item threatens that comfort. Closing a ticket feels like reneging on a promise, even when the promise was never really made. So the item stays, and the list becomes a place where good intentions go to wait indefinitely.

The cost of that comfort is paid by others. Requesters see their ticket marked open and reasonably assume it is on its way. Planning meetings wade through noise to find the few things that matter. New team members cannot tell which entries are live commitments and which are leftovers from a strategy that changed long ago. The backlog stops being a tool for deciding and becomes an archive of things nobody wanted to say no to.

A more truthful alternative starts with separating two kinds of entries. Real commitments are things the team has agreed to do and expects to deliver. Noted ideas are things worth remembering but not promised. They can live in different places, or at least carry different labels, so that nobody mistakes one for the other.

Next, close stale items, and close them with a reason. "Not pursuing because priorities shifted" or "superseded by a different approach" is far kinder than silence. A reason turns a deletion into a decision that someone can read, question or reopen.

Finally, tell requesters early when something won't happen. Waiting until they ask, months later, only makes the disappointment worse and the trust thinner. An early, honest no lets them find another path while it still helps.

A shorter backlog will feel less reassuring at first. It will also be one that people can believe.
