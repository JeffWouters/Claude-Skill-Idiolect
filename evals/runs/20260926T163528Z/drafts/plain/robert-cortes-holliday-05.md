I went to hear G. K. Chesterton lecture when he came to America, half expecting to be disappointed. The critics had been at him for some time. They said he had repeated himself, that the paradoxes had worn thin, that the great man had shrunk into a habit. I had read enough of them to arrive a little wary, the way one goes to see a famous building after being told it is smaller than the postcards.

He was not smaller than the postcards. Whatever the critics had taken away from him, they had not managed to take any of the size. He filled the platform the way weather fills a sky, and when he moved it seemed less a man crossing a stage than a landscape shifting its position. I found I had stopped thinking about the reviews within a minute of his arrival.

The talk itself was an argument against fatalism. He would not have it that men are simply one more product of nature, pushed along by the same forces that push the tides and the seasons. Man, he said, stands apart. He is the one creature that looks at nature from the outside, judges it, and refuses it when he likes. The rest of the world goes where it is driven; man chooses, and the choosing is the whole point of him. Afterward I was taken backstage. Our host led the way, and the way was not clear. A cluster of eager young women had gathered near the door, the sort who come to lectures as much to be near the lecturer as to hear him, and they closed in the moment he appeared. The host dealt with them with the practised calm of a man steering a large ship through small boats. He murmured, he smiled, he kept moving, and he brought Chesterton through them and into a dressing room without once appearing to hurry.
