I have read your novel through twice now, and I owe you a plain answer rather than a polite one. The trouble is not your sentences, which are often fine, nor your eye for the ordinary business of life, which is sharp. The trouble is your hero. He does not matter enough, and because he does not matter, nothing that happens to him matters either.

You have given us a string of loose events: a quarrel here, a journey there, a disappointment, a small recovery. In a realistic novel that is exactly what life looks like, and you are right to refuse the tidy plot. But loose events only hold together when they happen to someone whose fate we care about. Think of how a good biography works. Its incidents are often trivial in themselves, a dinner, a letter, a bad week, yet we read every page because the person at the centre is important. The weight of the life lends weight to each detail. Your book has the details without the weight, and so they stay trivial.

My advice, then, is to begin again with a larger figure. Choose a protagonist who counts for something in the world, and set him fighting for a cause worth the fight. I would urge you to treat that choice the way a careful person treats an investment. You are about to spend a year or more of your working life on this book. Put that time into a subject that will repay it, one whose stakes will carry the reader through the quiet chapters as well as the loud ones. A great cause gives even the smallest scene a reason to be there.

If you cannot find such a figure, or if this particular material will not bend to one, then do not stretch it. Cut it down to a short story. What reads thin across three hundred pages can be sharp and complete across twenty, and there is no shame in that form. There is only shame in a long book about a man nobody needed to read about.

With every good wish for the next draft,
