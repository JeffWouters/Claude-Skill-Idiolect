There is a hotel on lower Fifth Avenue which is, in the manner of its living, French.  It is not, you understand, in France; it is very much in New York.  But once inside its doors you are, for the price of a dinner, somewhere else.  And the dinner, it should be said at once, is the thing.  The cooking is French cooking, the real article, not the kind which is French only on the bill of fare.  People who know about these things speak of it with a certain lowering of the voice.

It is a place of artists.  They come to it as to their own.  You see them at the little tables, talking, gesturing, disputing, in no hurry whatever to be gone.  A good many of the diners wear beards--not the neat, businesslike beard of the bank director, but the other kind, the kind that has opinions.  The waiters are of a piece with the cooking.  They glide; they bow; they murmur; they appear to regard your choice of a sauce as a matter of some moment, which, in that house, it is.  And, among the beards and the paint-stained sleeves, sit the fashionable.  Very fashionably gowned ladies, very correctly tailored gentlemen, who have come down from uptown to look at the artists, and who are themselves, to be sure, looked at in return.  The whole room has an air, pleasant and a little foreign, which you feel before you can name it.

Outside, at the curb, stand the old horse carriages.  You might think that the motor-car had never been invented.  There they wait, the horses dozing, and on the boxes sit the drivers, costumed, in hats and coats of another day, patient as the horses.  They wait for the fashionable, who will presently come out, well dined, and be driven away uptown in the manner of their grandparents.  It is a picture, and I suspect that everybody concerned knows it.

The place has got into a book.  A novel, [title needed], depicts it under another name, but nobody who has dined there could mistake it.  There it all is: the cooking, the artists, the beards, the waiters, the ladies from uptown.  And the novelist has not forgotten the barbershop in the basement.  I confess I had not myself thought much about the barbershop in the basement.  One goes to the hotel, after all, to dine, not to be shaved.  But it is there, in the book, as it is there in fact, down below, going quietly about its business while the artists dispute and the fashionable dine overhead.  It takes a novelist to go down into the basement.
