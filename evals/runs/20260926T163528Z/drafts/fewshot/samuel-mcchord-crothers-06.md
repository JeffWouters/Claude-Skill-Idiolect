It rained in Wales, as it has a way of doing, and I was glad of any excuse for standing under a roof. The excuse was furnished by a servant at the hotel, who, seeing me at the window, pointed across the way to a low stone building and informed me that it was the cell of St. David. I asked him what he knew of the saint. He knew that he was a very holy man, and that he was some relation to King Arthur. When I pressed him as to the degree of kinship he became vague. He thought it was on the mother's side, but he would not commit himself. It was enough for him that the two were in the family, and I have since come to think that it was enough for anybody.

For later, in a more studious mood, I turned to [title of Welsh biographical compendium], which I had supposed would settle the matter. It did nothing of the kind. It did something much better. It treated the question with that large hospitality which is the charm of the older kind of learning. Some authorities, it appeared, made St. David the nephew of Arthur; others, with equal confidence, made him the uncle. The compiler weighed the evidence on both sides with great fairness and did not presume to decide between them. He was, moreover, candid enough to admit that there were scholars who held that Arthur was not a king at all, but a constellation. This did not seem to him to be a fatal objection. A saint might very well be related to a constellation; there are families in which stranger things have happened.

As for the dates, which in the case of a nephew and an uncle might be thought to have some bearing on the matter, he dismissed them with a gesture. Chronology, he intimated, was a pedantic science, and not to be allowed to interfere with the affections of a people.

I confess that I closed the book with a feeling of gratitude. There is a kind of history which tells us precisely what happened, and leaves us nothing to do but to believe it. There is another kind which leaves the doors open and the windows unlatched, so that the imagination may come and go as it pleases. In a country where it rains so much, one needs a history of the second kind. The servant at the hotel had been, after all, a very sound historian. He had told me all that was known, and he had not spoiled it by knowing more.
