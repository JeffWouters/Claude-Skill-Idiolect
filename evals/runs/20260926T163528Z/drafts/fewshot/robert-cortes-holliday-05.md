He has been cut down to size, we are told.  The critics have been at him for some time now, with the small, sharp instruments of their trade, and the word has gone round that the great man is not, after all, so very great.  Well, we went to hear him.  We sat among the American multitude in [lecture hall needed] and waited, somewhat in the spirit of one who goes to see whether a famous mountain has actually been lowered by the surveyors.  It had not.  He came out upon the platform, and he was enormous.  He was enormous in the plain physical sense, which everybody knows about, and he was enormous in some other sense, difficult to denote, which fills a hall as a tide fills a harbour.  The critics, you perceive, had been measuring the wrong thing.

His subject, as nearly as we could keep hold of it, was fatalism, and he would have none of it.  Man, he said (and he said it in a good many delightful ways), is not simply a part of nature, carried along with the leaves and the weather.  Man stands apart.  He looks at nature; nature does not look at him.  [example needed]  It was the old doctrine, stoutly argued, and the audience, which had perhaps come to see a celebrity, found itself, somewhat to its surprise, listening to a philosopher.

Afterwards we went round behind.  There is always a crowd behind, and this one consisted very largely of young women, eager young women, earnest young women, young women with programmes and young women with fountain pens, all pressing in upon the great man in the narrow passage.  A host, a brisk and capable person evidently practised in this sort of navigation, took him by the arm.  "This way, this way," he said, very nicely, very firmly.  And through the press of admirers, as a tug brings a liner through the small craft of the harbour, he steered the enormous figure, gently but without pause, past the programmes, past the fountain pens, into the dressing room; and the door shut.  We followed, having, as it happened, some slight claim to be admitted.
