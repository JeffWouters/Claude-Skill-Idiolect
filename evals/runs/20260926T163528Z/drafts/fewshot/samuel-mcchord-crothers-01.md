There was once a theologian, [name needed], whose reasoning I have always admired for its prudence. He did not ask his logic to do more than logic can safely do. Having proved a thing to his own satisfaction, he was careful to add, "so far as the nature of the case permits." It was a modest phrase, and it saved him from no end of embarrassment. For whenever his argument came to the edge of a stubborn fact, it did not dash itself against it. It simply bowed, murmured its little caveat, and went round.

I would recommend the same courtesy to those who reason about great nations. By all the rules of political science, a very large state ought not to exist. It is too sprawling to be governed and too various to agree. The philosopher sits down and draws the plan of a commonwealth as it should be. It is small and compact. Its citizens know one another by sight. They meet in one place and settle their affairs by the light of Right Reason. Everything about it is tidy, and everything about it is probable. The only thing to be said against it is that it does not happen.

Meanwhile the improbable empires and the unreasonable republics go on. They stretch across mountains and deserts that were never meant to be crossed by a single law. They take in peoples who have nothing in common but a flag and a grievance against the tax collector. They ought to fall apart, and it must be confessed that now and then they look as if they would. Yet somehow they endure, and not only endure but grow stronger. The logician, finding his conclusions contradicted, would do well to fall back on the theologian's phrase.

So with America. Its dangers are not hidden; they are plain to every one who passes by. Any one can make a list of them, and a good many people have. But I am disposed to think that a nation which has so many obvious reasons for failing, and has not yet failed, must have some reasons for succeeding that are not so obvious.

This is not to say that we should stop our ears to the prophets. A prophet of evil is a useful person to have about the house. He keeps us from growing too comfortable. Only we need not believe that his warnings are the whole of the truth, so far as the nature of the case permits.
