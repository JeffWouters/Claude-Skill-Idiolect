I turn the heap in autumn. Once a year, from one bay into the other. It is heavy work. It is also the only thing that keeps the heap alive.

A heap needs air. That is the whole point of turning it. The things that break it down need to breathe, the same as we do. Take the air away and the heap does not stop. It just goes the wrong way. I know this because I left mine alone for [length of time needed]. I told myself it would sort itself out. It did not. When I finally put a fork in, it was [example needed]. Cold, wet and packed flat. And the smell. I will not forget the smell.

A working heap is different. You feel it before you see it. Push the fork into the middle and there is warmth coming up the handle. Pull a forkful out and the inside is dark and crumbly, with a faint steam on a cold morning. There are worms all through it. The outside edge is always behind. Dry stalks, whole leaves, the odd eggshell. That goes into the middle of the new heap for another go.

The finished stuff I sieve. A frame of mesh over the barrow, rub it through with the flat of my hand. The lumps go back on the heap. Then the new heap gets a watering. Not a soaking. Damp, like a wrung-out cloth. Then a cover over the top to keep the rain off and the heat in.
