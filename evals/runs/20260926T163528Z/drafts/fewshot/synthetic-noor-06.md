It snowed in the night. I knew before I opened the curtains. The street was quiet in the way it only is after snow. No cars. No footsteps. Just a soft, flat hush over everything. Then I went to find the shovel.

The shovel I use is [type of shovel needed]. Light, wide, does the job. There is another one in the shed. It came with the house, or rather with [who it was inherited from]. It is older and heavier, with a [example needed] blade. It is a good shovel. It is also a shovel for someone with a stronger back than mine. I leave it where it is.

Clothing first. This matters more than you think. Layers, not one big coat. You warm up fast once you start and a heavy coat is soon too hot. Gloves that grip. Boots with a proper sole. A hat.

I start at the house end. Always. That way I am never walking on snow I have yet to clear and pressing it into ice. The first job is a centre line. One shovel width, straight down the middle of the path to the gate. That gives me a way out if I have to stop. It also splits the job in two. Then I work outward from the line. Left side, then right side. Short strokes, pushing the snow to the edge.

Throw downwind. Check the wind before the first throw, not after. Otherwise it comes straight back at you, in your face and down your collar and back onto the bit you just cleared.

And do not fill the shovel. This is the big one. Half a load, sometimes less. Snow is heavier than it looks, and wet snow is heavier still. A full shovel lifted and twisted is how backs go. Half loads take longer. They also mean I can stand up straight the next day.

When the path is clear I sweep it. A stiff broom, down to the paving. The thin layer the shovel leaves is the layer that freezes. Then the grit. A thin scatter, edge to edge, and a bit extra on the step. Then back inside.
