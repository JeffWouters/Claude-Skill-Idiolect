Philadelphia keeps a great many of her treasures in her side streets.  The broad avenues are for the visitor; the little lanes are for the person who has time.  And the person who has time will do well to begin with Camac Street.  Camac Street is the artists' lane.  It has been restored, and restored, which is not always the case, with taste.  The old houses stand as they stood, only rather cleaner, and within some of them are clubs, where artists and those who like to be near artists gather in the evening.  It is quaint, but not too quaint; it has been improved, but not improved away.

And then there is South Carlisle Street.  I have made some inquiries about South Carlisle Street, and the chief thing to be learned about it is that nobody can find it.  Ask a policeman, and he will direct you, with great confidence, to somewhere else.  Ask a lifelong resident of the town, a man who was born and bred within a mile of it, and he will think a moment and tell you that there is no such street.  There is.  It lies [location needed].  I set this down plainly, for the benefit of the natives.

It is a very narrow street.  It is so narrow that one feels it would be a kind of rudeness to walk down the middle of it, and so narrow that a person standing upon one doorstep could, with no great effort, hand a cup of sugar to a person standing upon the doorstep opposite.  But in that small space is gathered, as in a little museum, everything that the city does best.  There are the doorways, those classic Philadelphia doorways, with their white steps and their fanlights.  There is the brick, the old red brick, mellowed by the years to the colour of a good apple.  There are the shutters, folded back.  And there are the knockers, brass knockers, iron knockers, knockers in the shape of this and of that, each one polished and each one, so to say, waiting.  The whole city, one might say, is here in small.
