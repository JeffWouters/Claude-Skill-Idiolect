Most of what is written about saying no concerns requests we would rather not accept. The advice is to be confident, to decline without excessive apology, to recognise that our time is our own. This is reasonable counsel, and for the unwanted request it is probably sufficient. But it leaves untouched a harder and, I think, more common case: the request we would genuinely like to accept, from someone we respect, for something that is plainly worth doing, at a moment when we simply do not have the time. Confidence is of little help here, since the difficulty is not that we lack the nerve to refuse but that we do not really want to.

Consider a colleague whose judgement you trust, who asks late in the day whether you might review their design before a decision is taken. The request is sensible; the design matters; your view would probably improve it. And the evening is already committed, perhaps to other work, perhaps to something outside work altogether. Nothing about the situation invites a firm refusal. Everything about it invites a quiet yes, followed by the private rearrangement of whatever had been planned.

The trouble is that the cost of agreeing does not disappear; it is merely deferred, and it tends to fall somewhere we did not intend. Sometimes it falls on the work we had already promised, which now receives less attention than it deserved. Sometimes it falls on the people who were expecting our evening. Most often, I suspect, it falls on ourselves, in the form of tiredness, of resentment we would prefer not to feel towards a colleague we like, and of a gradual erosion of the boundaries that allow us to be useful at all. The review itself may be done less well than it would have been on another day.

What would it mean to refuse such a request well? Perhaps it means acknowledging openly that we want to help, explaining that we cannot tonight, and offering whatever we honestly can instead. That is not a lack of generosity. It is, arguably, the more respectful answer, since it treats the colleague as someone who would rather have a truthful no than a grudging yes.
