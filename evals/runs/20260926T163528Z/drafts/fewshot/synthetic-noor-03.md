Bitter oranges come in once a year. For a few weeks. I buy mine from [shop needed]. They cost [price needed]. They do not look like much. [appearance needed]. They are not for eating. Bite one and you will know why. They are sour and bitter at once. That is what makes the marmalade.

The pan was my [relative needed]'s. [pan description needed]. It is the only pan I own that is big enough. Marmalade needs room. It rises when it boils.

First I cut the oranges in half and juice them. All the juice goes into the pan. Then the pips. There are a lot of pips. I scrape them out with a teaspoon, along with the pith and the loose membrane. All of that goes into a square of muslin. I tie the corners up into a bag. That bag is where the set comes from. The pectin is in the pips and pith, not in the juice.

Then the peel. This is the slow part. I slice it thin. As thin as I can manage. Long strips, a few millimetres wide. Thick peel stays chewy however long you cook it. Thin peel goes soft and clear. A sharp knife helps. So does the radio.

Peel, juice and water go into the pan. The muslin bag goes in with them. Then I leave the lot overnight. The soaking softens the peel before it ever sees heat.

In the morning I bring it to a simmer. A slow one. And I leave it there until the peel is soft. Properly soft. Squash a piece between finger and thumb. It should give with no resistance at all. That takes [time needed]. Longer than you think.

I learned this the hard way. [past failure needed]. I rushed the simmer. The sugar went in too soon. Sugar sets the peel hard. It does not soften after that. You cannot undo it.

When the peel is done I lift out the bag. I let it cool a little on a saucer. Then I squeeze it over the pan. Hard. A thick, cloudy gloop comes out. That is the pectin. Scrape it off the outside of the bag too. Every bit.
