Consider the undertaker.  I have lately given some thought to this word, and the more I think about it the less I understand it.  Why the undertaker should have been the one to undertake is not at all clear.  Everybody undertakes something.  The doctor undertakes to cure you, and the lawyer undertakes to get you off, and either of them, it would seem, had quite as good a claim to the title.  But somehow, by what accident of history I do not know, the word came to rest upon the man who undertakes to bury you, and there it stayed.

And having stayed, it took on a character.  The word grew long in the face.  It put on black gloves and a tall hat.  It came to mean, in the popular mind, a thin, pale, solemn person, rubbing his hands and speaking in a low voice, a figure out of a comic paper, at once gloomy and faintly ridiculous.  [example needed]  You could not say "undertaker" in company without somebody's smiling, and you could not say it in a sickroom without somebody's shuddering.  This is a hard thing for a man in trade.

So the word was retired.  The undertaker became a funeral director.  He was not the first man to be promoted in this manner, nor will he be the last.  [example needed]  In the same way, I imagine, a great many callings have gone up in the world by the simple process of going up in the dictionary.  A funeral director directs.  There is authority in it, and efficiency, and nothing whatever of the tall hat.

But fashion does not stand still, even in this sober business.  Funeral director, it appears, is already a little behind the times.  The word now is mortician.  Mortician has a learned sound, rather like physician, and it is, so they tell me, very much the thing.  So the old undertaker is gone.  You are not buried; you are, I suppose, morticed.
