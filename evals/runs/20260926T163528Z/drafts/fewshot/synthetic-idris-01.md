Much of the advice on one-on-one meetings urges the manager to let the report set the agenda, and there is real wisdom in it. A conversation that begins with the report's concerns is more likely to reach them than one that begins with the manager's. Yet the advice, however sincerely followed, cannot dissolve the imbalance on which the meeting rests. One person in the room will write the other's review, influence their pay and shape their prospects; the other knows this, and knows that the first knows it too. Handing over the agenda does not change who holds the pen. It may even obscure the matter, by suggesting an equality that neither party quite believes in.

I think it is better, on the whole, to name the imbalance than to pretend it away. To say plainly that the relationship is unequal, and that this makes certain things harder to say, is not an admission of failure but an acknowledgement of the conditions under which honesty has to be earned. Having said it, the manager can then do the slower work of making the imbalance matter less. That means welcoming correction rather than merely permitting it, so that a report who points out an error is thanked rather than tolerated; it means allowing pauses to run their course, since the thing that most needs saying often arrives only after an uncomfortable silence; it means returning to remarks made in earlier meetings, which shows that they were heard and that they counted; and it means being willing to admit doubt, to say that one does not know, or has not decided, or may have been wrong.

None of these gestures will produce trust on its own, and certainly not quickly. Trust in this setting accumulates in small deposits over many conversations, and a single careless response can withdraw a good deal of it. But the alternative, a meeting in which both people perform an openness that neither feels, seems to me a poor substitute. Better, surely, to begin from the truth about the relationship and build from there.
