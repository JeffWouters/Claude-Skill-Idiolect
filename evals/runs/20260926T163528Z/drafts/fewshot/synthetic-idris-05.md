Anyone who has spent time on a software team will know the backlog that never shrinks: the list of several hundred items, some of them years old, which everyone agrees is too long and which nobody ever quite gets round to pruning. We usually explain this as a failure of discipline, as though the list had simply grown while our attention was elsewhere. I am not sure that explanation is adequate. A long list is, among other things, a source of comfort to the people who keep it. It records that every request was heard, that no idea was lost, that nothing has been definitively refused; and so long as an item remains open, the question of whether it will ever be done can be quietly deferred. Cleanup rarely happens, I think, not because it is laborious but because closing a ticket feels uncomfortably like reneging on a promise, even when the promise was never really made.

What would a more truthful arrangement look like? The first step, perhaps, is to distinguish between two quite different kinds of entry that the backlog tends to blur together: the commitments the team genuinely intends to honour, and the ideas it has merely noted in case they prove useful. Both are worth keeping, but they are not the same thing, and treating them as though they were allows the second to borrow the weight of the first. The second step is to close stale items deliberately, and with a reason attached — not in a silent purge, which only teaches people that their requests disappear, but in a way that says plainly why the work will not be done. The third, and arguably the hardest, is to tell requesters early when something is not going to happen, rather than letting the item linger until its silence becomes its own answer.

None of this is especially sophisticated. It asks only that we give up the reassurance of the long list in exchange for a shorter one that means what it says. That exchange is less comfortable than it sounds, since it obliges us to disappoint people sooner and more directly than we are used to. But the disappointment was always coming; the only question is whether it arrives honestly or by neglect.
