Writing for a distributed team is, in some respects, an exercise in writing without a face. In a room we rely on a great many small signals to soften what we say — a tone of voice, a pause, the expression that tells a colleague we are thinking aloud rather than delivering a verdict — and we rarely notice how much work those signals do until they are taken away. An asynchronous message has none of them. It arrives stripped of its context and is read, often hours later, by someone who must supply the tone for themselves.

The natural response is to write more thoroughly, anticipating questions and setting out the reasoning in full, and this does help. A careful message spares its readers a great deal of guesswork. Yet thoroughness has a curious side effect: the more complete a message appears, the more it tends to read as a decision already taken. Readers who were not present for the thinking may reasonably conclude that there is nothing left for them to contribute, and so they say nothing, and the silence is then mistaken for agreement.

What might we do differently? It seems worth sharing open questions explicitly rather than leaving them implied — saying plainly which parts of a proposal are settled and which are still genuinely uncertain. Decisions made while part of the team is asleep might be treated as tentative until those colleagues have had a chance to respond, since otherwise the working hours of one location quietly become the hours in which everything is decided. Where meetings are unavoidable, the awkward times could rotate, so that the burden of the early call or the late one does not always fall on the same people.

Underneath all of this lies a simpler observation, and perhaps the most important one. However many people a message is addressed to, each of them reads it alone. There is no one beside them to catch their eye or to say that the phrasing sounded harsher than it was meant. If we could keep that solitary reader in mind as we write, I suspect a good deal of the rest would follow.
