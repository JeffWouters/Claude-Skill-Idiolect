My dear friend, I have read your novel through, and I would not have you think that I read it grudgingly. There were pages I enjoyed. But when I laid it down I found I could not remember what had happened, and on reflection I am not sure that anything had. Your events were loose, and loose events are not in themselves a fault. Life is made of them. The trouble was that they happened to a man who did not greatly matter, and so they did not greatly matter either. A trivial hero has a way of making everything about him trivial. He drops his hat, and it is only a hat.

Now consider how the good biographer goes about his work. He has no more power than you to invent a plot. He must take the days as they came, with all their errands and dinners and small misunderstandings. Yet we read him with a kind of breathless interest, because the man whose days they were was doing something large. The dinner is not merely a dinner; it is the evening before a great decision. The errand is part of a campaign. The importance of the man runs backward into every detail and lends it a borrowed dignity.

I would have you take a leaf out of the biographer's book. Choose for your hero a person of consequence, one who is fighting for a great cause, and let the little happenings of realistic fiction gather about him. I do not say this is easy. It is rather like choosing an investment. You must put a good deal of capital into your man before he will pay you anything, and you must be reasonably sure he is sound. But if you choose well, the returns come in on every page, and the reader is glad to follow him even to the barber's.

And if, after looking about you, you find no such figure in your material, I would not have you despair. There is an honourable alternative. Cut the thing down to a short story. What will not bear the weight of a life will very often carry an afternoon.
