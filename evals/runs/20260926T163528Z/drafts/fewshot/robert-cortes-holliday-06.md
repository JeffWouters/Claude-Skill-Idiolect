Now, the buses of the great city have been fitted with plows.  This is efficiency.  A bus with a plow upon its nose, so the idea runs, is not a bus at all, but a kind of conquering engine, before which the snow must fall back in rout.  With a roar of the motor and a spin of the wheels the bus advances.  It advances perhaps a yard.  With still further roaring and a still more furious spinning of the wheels it advances again.  No, it is stuck fast in the drift.  And upon the sidewalks, bundled to the ears, stand the bystanders, who have nowhere in particular to go, and who cheer.  They cheer the bus when it moves an inch, and they cheer the drift when it does not.  It is the finest free entertainment of the winter.

Next morning the town is another place.  Lanes have been cut through the white, and the traffic creeps along them single file, taxicab behind truck, truck behind limousine, in a long and humble procession, like a line of penitents.  Nobody is in a hurry, because nobody can be.  The statues, too, have been touched by the storm.  Each wears a cap of snow, set on at a jaunty angle, and the lions before the Library, those grave and dignified beasts, look for once as though they might be persuaded to enjoy themselves.

And then there are the overshoes.  The commuter comes up out of the ground in overshoes of an astonishing bulk, clumping along like a diver on the floor of the sea.  Fashion in these matters has turned clean round.  There was a time, and not so very long ago, when a person who wore glasses took them off in company, and a person who wore galoshes would sooner have got his feet wet.  Such things were hidden, as one hides a patch.  All that has changed.  Glasses are now large, and dark-rimmed, and worn as a sort of decoration.  Galoshes flap open and are displayed.  The overshoe, as its wearer's easy stride plainly tells you, is nothing to apologise for.  It is the thing.
