The technical interview presents itself as a test of ability. A candidate is set a problem, asked to solve it under observation, and judged on the result, and the whole arrangement carries the air of an examination. But it seems to me that this description, though accurate as far as it goes, misses what the occasion actually is. Both parties are playing roles. The applicant performs the part of someone who is composed, capable and unhurried; the interviewer performs the part of someone who can tell, from a single conversation, whether that performance is genuine. Each knows the other is performing, and each proceeds as though the exercise were straightforwardly what it claims to be.

If the interview measured what it purports to measure, this would matter less. The difficulty is that it rewards a quality only loosely related to the work. What the format favours is calm under scrutiny: the capacity to think clearly while being watched, to speak fluently about a half-formed idea, to recover gracefully from a false start with a stranger taking notes. These are real abilities, and in some roles they are valuable. Yet the work itself mostly asks for something different. It asks for patience with problems that do not yield in an hour, for the willingness to read and reread, for collaboration with colleagues whose trust has been built over months rather than minutes. Someone may excel at all of this and still falter in the interview room, and the reverse is equally possible.

The interviewer's position is hardly easier. They must reach a consequential judgement on the basis of one contrived session, knowing that the conditions bear little resemblance to those in which the candidate would actually work. Whatever confidence they feel in the verdict is, I suspect, borrowed largely from the ritual itself.

Why, then, does the practice persist? Perhaps because it serves purposes other than the one it names: it offers a shared procedure, a way of making a difficult decision feel orderly, and a defence against the charge of arbitrariness. Those are not trivial needs. But we might at least be honest that they are the needs being met, rather than the one we claim.
