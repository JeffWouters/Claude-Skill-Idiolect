What is a one-on-one meeting actually for, when one of the two people in the room decides the other's pay, prospects and daily work? The usual advice is that the manager should let the report lead, setting the agenda, choosing the topics and doing most of the talking, and that advice is sound as far as it goes, since a meeting conducted this way is considerably better than one in which the manager simply works through a list of status questions. It cannot, however, remove the imbalance built into the relationship. The report knows who writes the review, and no amount of attentive listening will make them forget it.

It may be that the more honest course is to name the gap openly rather than pretend that a change of format has dissolved it. A manager who acknowledges plainly that the difference in authority exists, and that it probably makes certain things harder to say, has at least given the report permission to notice it too. This is not a confession so much as a description of the conditions under which both people are speaking; it treats the report as someone perfectly capable of understanding their own situation.

It seems to me that the rest follows from how the manager behaves across many meetings rather than from anything said in one. They should welcome being corrected, and show that they mean it, since a correction received with visible irritation is unlikely to be offered twice. They should tolerate pauses instead of hurrying to fill them, because the thing a report most needs to say frequently arrives only after a silence. They might return to something mentioned weeks earlier, which demonstrates that it was heard; and they might admit doubt about their own decisions, which is the clearest signal available that disagreement is safe. None of this is quick. Trust in this relationship grows slowly, I suspect, and only when the manager continues to behave in the same way long after the gesture has stopped feeling novel to either of them.
