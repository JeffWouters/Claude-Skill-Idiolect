What is a backlog actually for? Most teams would say that it records the work they intend to do, and yet almost any backlog that has lived for long enough suggests something rather different, a list that has grown well beyond anyone's capacity to deliver it and is kept not because it guides the work but because its existence is reassuring. It may be that the long list serves its keepers more than its readers, offering the comfort of knowing that nothing has been forgotten even when much of it will never be touched. Cleanup, when someone proposes it, tends to be postponed and then quietly dropped. I suspect this is because closing a ticket feels less like tidying than like reneging on a promise, as though removing an item were an admission that someone's request had been abandoned without their knowledge.

It seems to me that a more truthful alternative begins with separating two kinds of entry that most backlogs mix together: the commitments a team has genuinely made, and the ideas it has merely noted. The first deserve planning and attention; the second might live in a different list, one that nobody mistakes for a promise. Stale items can then be closed deliberately, each with a short reason recorded alongside it, so that the decision is visible rather than silent and anyone who returns to the item later can understand why it was set aside. Perhaps the most important practice of all is telling requesters early when something will not happen, rather than allowing their request to sit unanswered in the hope that the question will somehow resolve itself. This is uncomfortable, since it denies everyone involved the gentle fiction that everything is still possible. It is also, arguably, a form of respect, because it allows people to make other plans while there is still time to make them.

Perhaps a shorter backlog is not a sign of lower ambition but of greater honesty about what a team can actually carry.
