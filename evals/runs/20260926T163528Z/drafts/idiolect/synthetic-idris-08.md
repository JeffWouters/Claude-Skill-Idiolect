What becomes of tone when nobody is in the room? In a conversation held face to face, much of what we mean is carried by cues we hardly notice ourselves giving: a pause, a raised eyebrow, the softening of a voice when a suggestion is still only a suggestion. Asynchronous writing strips almost all of this away, and a distributed team that relies on it must find other ways to signal warmth, uncertainty and openness. The usual advice is to write more thoroughly, to anticipate questions and supply context, and this advice is sound as far as it goes. It may be, however, that a very thorough message carries a risk of its own, since the more complete it appears, the more easily it can read as a decision already taken rather than an invitation to think together.

It seems to me that a few habits can soften this without sacrificing clarity. The first is to share open questions explicitly, naming what the writer does not yet know and would genuinely like others to weigh in on, so that the reader can see where the thinking is still unfinished. The second is to treat decisions made while colleagues elsewhere are asleep as tentative until those colleagues have had a chance to respond; a decision reached by whoever happened to be awake is not quite the same as a decision reached by the team. There is also the matter of meetings, which a distributed team can rarely avoid entirely, and whose awkward hours might be rotated rather than always falling on the same people.

The most important thing to remember, I suspect, is that each reader is alone when they read. They cannot see the writer's face or hear the tone that was intended, and whatever the message leaves ambiguous they will resolve by themselves, sometimes generously and sometimes not. Perhaps the kindest thing a writer can do is to write as though that solitude were always in view.
