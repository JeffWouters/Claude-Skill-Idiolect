I have read your novel with the attention it deserves and with more patience than you suppose. You ask me what is wrong with it. The events are loose, and they remain trivial to the end, not because nothing happens in them but because nothing that happens matters very much to anybody, least of all to the reader who is asked to follow them through chapter after chapter. Your hero is an amiable young man who might have lived next door to any of us for a lifetime without our ever having found it necessary to learn his name.

I am not one of those who would banish the ordinary from realistic fiction. But the ordinary must happen to somebody who counts. The good biographies understand this without being told. Nobody reads the life of a great man for the sake of his breakfasts, and yet his breakfasts become interesting because they are his and because we know what business awaited him when he rose from the table. Small things take their importance from the large thing they lead up to.

My advice, then, is that you choose a major figure and set him to fighting for a great cause. I would have you treat that choice as a prudent man treats a worthwhile investment. You are about to put a considerable portion of your life into this hero, and you have every right to expect some return on the capital. It is poor economy to lavish your best observation on a character who can pay no dividend. Choose a man whose defeats would be losses to the world and whose victories would be worth the reader's while to share; then the trivial details will earn their keep along with the rest.

If this cannot be done, and I grant that the material may refuse it, there remains an honourable way out, which is to turn the whole thing into a short story. A small hero is quite at home in a small space. What wearies us through a long book will often charm us in a few pages. Not every acquaintance can be invited to stay the week.
