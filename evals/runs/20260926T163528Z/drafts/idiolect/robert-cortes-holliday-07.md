Philadelphia keeps much of its charm tucked away where the visitor, hurrying along the broad avenues with his guidebook, will hardly stumble upon it.  The little side streets are what is meant.  They are not advertised; they are scarcely even acknowledged; and the Philadelphian himself, questioned about them, looks at you with mild surprise, like a man asked after some distant and uninteresting relative.

Camac Street is the best known of them, and it deserves to be.  It has been restored, and restored (which is by no means always the case) with admirable taste.  The artists have discovered it, and have settled there, and have founded their clubs there, and the result is a lane which looks as a lane in an old city ought to look and so seldom does. South Carlisle Street is another matter.  It is not so much hidden as mislaid.  Philadelphians born and bred, living within easy walking distance of it, could not find it with a map.  For the benefit of the curious, then, it lies [location needed: where South Carlisle Street runs].  Having found it, you will perhaps wonder how anybody could miss it; having looked away for a moment, you will perhaps understand.

It is extraordinarily narrow.  Two persons of generous proportions, meeting in the middle of it, would have to come to some arrangement.  But in this narrow compass the whole character of the old city is gathered up and displayed, as it were, for inspection.  Here are the classic Philadelphia doorways, with their modest dignity.  Here is the brick, mellowed to the colour that only brick and time together can manage.  Here are the shutters, and here, polished or neglected as the case may be, are the knockers.  Nothing is grand.  Everything is right.  And one comes out again into the traffic of the broad street feeling that one has been, for a few minutes, a guest in a house where nobody thought to mention that visitors were expected.
