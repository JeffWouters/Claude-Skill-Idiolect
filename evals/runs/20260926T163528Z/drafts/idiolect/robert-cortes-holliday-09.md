There is no predicting, so far as I can discover, when a writer will work and when he will not.  Consider the case of a certain author.  He was engaged, and paid, to produce a series of articles.  Nothing particularly ambitious was expected of him.  But the articles declined to remain articles.  They expanded, installment by installment, into something longer and weightier, and presently they were a book.  The book then took possession of him entirely.  He breakfasted with it, slept with it, carried it about the streets with him; it consumed him, as it were, body and soul.  Nobody had commissioned a book, and I doubt whether he could have stopped it had he tried.

Some time later he accepted a commission of quite another character.  It was an easy undertaking, the sort of assignment a man could accomplish, you would suppose, with his left hand of an afternoon.  There it stopped.  Weeks went by, and then months, and all the while he wrote to his publishers one excuse after another ([example needed: the excuses he gave]), each more elaborate than the last, while the publishers waited, not always patiently, for a manuscript that did not arrive.  The work simply would not come, and he could not have explained why.  Here was a man who had surrendered himself to a task nobody required of him, and who could not now be persuaded to finish a trifling one he had promised.

Then there is the case of a gentleman who formerly shared lodgings with me.  When he lived sensibly and attended conscientiously to his health he could not produce a sentence worth preserving.  He would sit at his table, virtuous and vacant, the entire morning through.  Let him be out half the night in riotous company, however, and come home at an hour I shan't specify, and the following day the pages fairly poured from him.  He was, in a manner of speaking, a man with whom dissipation agreed, at least at the writing desk.  I don't recommend his method; I merely report it.  What is one to make of such contradictions?  Perhaps some psychologist has investigated them and recorded everything in a learned monograph; if so, I should very much like to read it.  It might explain a good deal, to the writer as much as to anybody.
