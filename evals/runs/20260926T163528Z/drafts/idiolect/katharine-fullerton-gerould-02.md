We are all taught, in the nursery, that a lie is a lie, and that there is an end of it. But the nursery did not reckon with the person who asks what is none of his business. Every one has met him. He wishes to know [example needed: the sort of private question he puts]; and he asks it with an air of perfect innocence, as though the answer were owed him like change at a shop. What is one to do? The moralist says: refuse to answer. It sounds very well. Yet in practice a refusal is nearly always an answer, and often a louder one than the truth would have been; the silence, the change of subject, the heightened colour, tell him precisely what he came to find out.

Let us look into the matter a little more squarely. Truth is owed, surely, to those who have some rightful claim upon it--to a judge, to a partner, to a friend who must act upon what we say. It is not owed to every one who happens to put a question. The man who pries into a private matter has no more title to the answer than a thief has to your purse; he is, in plain words, trying to take what does not belong to him. I do not see that we are bound to hand it over merely because he has asked politely. One does not, after all, feel obliged to tell a burglar where the silver is kept.

Granted; but there is a limit, and I think it lies here. If I lie only to save my own face, to spare my own pride or cover my own foolishness, I have done a shabby thing, and no amount of casuistry will make it otherwise. My dignity is my own affair; I must bear the cost of it. It is quite another matter when the secret is some one else's. To lie in order to shield another person--one who trusted us, and who cannot defend himself--may not be admirable. But it can be excused. That is the whole of the distinction.
