Nature is not afraid of repeating herself. She brings round the same seasons in the same order, and the sun rises every morning with no attempt at novelty. Nobody accuses her of poverty of invention. The reason is simple enough: we cannot get away from her. The writer enjoys no such security. His audience is free to leave at any moment, and it takes only a very slight motion of the hand to close a book. So he lives in dread of saying the same thing twice, and his critics encourage the dread.

There is a kind of criticism which finds fault with a novelist because his characters repeat themselves. The comic person, it is observed, has one way of speaking, and he speaks in that way whenever he appears; he learns nothing, he changes nothing, and he goes out of the story as he came in. This is set down as a defect of art. I think the critic mistakes what the audience wants. We do not wish our old acquaintances to improve. When a familiar figure comes back upon the scene, the pleasure lies precisely in finding him unaltered, and in hearing him say the thing which we knew he was going to say before he opened his mouth.

The critic may call this a weakness in the reader. Perhaps it is. But it is a weakness which Nature has been indulging in us for a long time, and the novelist who shares it is in good company.
