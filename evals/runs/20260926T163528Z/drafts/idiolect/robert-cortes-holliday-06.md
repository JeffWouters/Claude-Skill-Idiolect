The snow came down upon New York as though it had been saving up for the occasion, and by nightfall the city had the look of a place which has been handsomely and completely surprised.  The buses, fitted out with plows for exactly this emergency, went forth valiantly to meet it.  They did not get far.  One after another they stuck in the drifts, snorting, grinding, backing a little, lunging a little, and settling deeper.  And around each of them a crowd collected, as a crowd will in this town around anything whatever that is in difficulty.  The bystanders did not help; they watched.  When a bus made a gallant heave and gained a foot they cheered; when it lost the foot again they cheered also, with perfect impartiality.  It was, so to speak, a sporting event, and no admission was charged.

The next morning the town was a different spectacle.  Lanes had been cleared through the principal streets, and through these the traffic crawled along in single file, with the patient, dejected air of a procession which has forgotten where it was going.  The statues, meanwhile, had all acquired headgear.  The lions before the library sat there with snow caps drawn over their brows, looking rather less majestic and considerably more comfortable than usual. On foot the commuters came plodding through it in bulky overshoes, and this reminded me of a curious revolution in fashion.  There was a time, not so very long ago, when a person of any refinement concealed his glasses whenever he could, and would sooner have gone about with wet feet than be seen in galoshes.  Both were regarded as confessions of infirmity, like an ear trumpet.  That time, it seems, has gone.  Glasses are now worn conspicuously, with large and fashionable rims, as an ornament; galoshes are flapped about unbuckled, with an air, as it were, of having chosen them.  What was once hidden is now displayed.  The snowstorm merely gave the new fashion a particularly fine morning on which to show itself off.
