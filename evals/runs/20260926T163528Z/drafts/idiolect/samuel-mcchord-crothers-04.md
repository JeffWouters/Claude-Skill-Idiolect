It is a hazardous thing to generalize about Americans. They have come from every quarter of the globe, they live under every variety of climate, and they differ among themselves in occupation, temper and belief quite as much as the peoples of Europe differ from one another. Any statement that begins "The American is" had better be accompanied by a list of exceptions longer than itself.

And yet there is a figure whom Americans of every origin recognize and, what is more remarkable, cheerfully accept as their own. You will find him in the caricatures. When the nation is drawn by its own cartoonists, or by those of other countries, it seldom appears as a merchant or a scholar or a citizen of any settled town. It appears as a man from the backwoods, lank and weather-beaten, careless of his dress and confident of his opinions. He is not a portrait of anybody in particular. He is a symbol, and the descendants of immigrants who never saw a forest clearing have adopted him as readily as the descendants of the pioneers.

The curious thing is that he is not at all refined. He is crude, familiar, loud and fond of slapping strangers on the back. One would expect cultivated people to disown him. But a refined American poet could look upon this rough fellow with genuine affection, perceiving beneath his manners something generous and sound.

Not every observer was so indulgent. A visiting English novelist, meeting the type in the flesh, found such boastful patriots altogether repellent; it was the boasting, more than the bad manners, that the visitor could least endure. It is perhaps a question of distance. The poet was related to him. The novelist was only introduced.
