There is no better seat for a moralist than the top of a London omnibus. From that elevation the street below takes on the appearance of a battlefield in which the combatants have not been informed that they are in danger, and cabs, carts and ponderous vans bear down upon a multitude of little figures who go darting across the way with no apparent consideration of what is coming. Every moment I expect a catastrophe. I hold my breath. Yet, so far as my observation extends, none of them is ever struck.

The explanation, I suppose, lies in the point of view. If I were to take a humbler position beside the driver, I should see what the loftier passenger misses: the quick step aside, the sudden halt, the hand upon the bridle, and the innumerable small escapes which are accomplished in the last second and never recorded by anybody. From above, all is doom; from below, it is only a crowded thoroughfare in which people are managing rather well. The tragedy is in the altitude.

It has occurred to me that a considerable part of our despair about the world comes from riding too high. We look down upon the course of human affairs and see nothing but collisions about to happen, because we are too far removed to perceive the swerve that comes in time. But the swerve is there. The old verse about the rider who fell from his horse puts the matter in a couplet, [quotation needed: the old verse about a falling rider], and it is a comfortable doctrine. There was not much time left for the rescue, but there was time enough.

I do not pretend that nobody is ever run over. I only say that the view from the top of the bus is not the whole of the evidence. Hope may be a matter of descending a little nearer to the street.
