Why is it so much harder to refuse a good request than a bad one? Most of the advice written about saying no concerns the unwanted ask: the meeting that should never have been scheduled, the favour requested by someone who asks for favours rather too readily. For these cases the prescription is confidence, and it works well enough, since there is little to lose by declining something one never wished to do. It may be that the harder case, and the more common one among conscientious people, is the request that is entirely reasonable, made by someone one respects, at a moment when one would genuinely like to help but simply has no time.

Consider a trusted colleague who asks, late in the day, whether you could review their design that evening. The request is sensible; the design matters; the colleague is someone whose judgement you value and whose work you would be glad to improve. Your evening, however, has already been promised to something else. No amount of confidence helps here, because the difficulty was never a lack of nerve. It lies instead in the fact that both the request and the prior commitment are legitimate, and that refusing one of them feels like a small betrayal of the kind of colleague you would prefer to be.

One agrees, then, and the review is done, perhaps a little hurriedly, and the colleague is grateful. The cost does not disappear, though; it is merely deferred, and it tends to fall later on whoever has the least power to object, which is frequently oneself. The commitment that was displaced must still be honoured somehow, whether by an evening lost to catching up or by a promise quietly broken to someone who was not in the room when the decision was made.

It seems to me that a considered refusal, offered with an honest explanation and perhaps an alternative time, is usually the more generous answer to a respected colleague. Perhaps the kindest yes is sometimes a no that has been properly explained.
