There has been, of late, a considerable amount of talk to the effect that G. K. Chesterton is not what he was.  The critics have taken his measurements and pronounced him diminished.  We went to hear him lecture with this solemn verdict in mind, half expecting to find a smaller man.  We did not.  Whatever the critics may have accomplished against his reputation, they have accomplished nothing whatever against his person, which still seems enormous.  He fills the platform as a ship fills a dock; he moves about it, when he moves at all, with the leisurely deliberation of something which understands that it cannot be hurried and sees no particular reason why it should be.  The voice, too, is large, though it is not loud.  It comes out of him easily, somewhat breathlessly, and appears to be enjoying itself enormously.  And the audience, which had come partly to discover whether the critics were right, very soon forgot the critics altogether.

His subject was fatalism, and he was against it.  The fashionable doctrine that man is merely one more product of nature, pushed about by invisible forces he can neither understand nor resist, he would have none of.  Man, he held, stands apart from nature.  He is in it, but he is not of it in the manner in which the tree and the beast are of it.  He can contemplate it, pass judgment upon it, and (what is more to the point) refuse it.  This was argued with a generous allowance of paradox and a generous allowance of laughter, some of it his own. Afterward we went round behind.  In the passage leading to the stage door there was a formidable congregation of young women, extremely eager, some carrying books and some carrying nothing but expectation.  Our host, who evidently had experience of these occasions, took the great man by the elbow, as it were, and navigated him through them, very much as one might pilot a liner through a flotilla of small boats.  The young women gave way reluctantly, and closed in again behind.  Then a door opened, we were in a dressing room, and the door was shut upon the flotilla.  It was, one felt, the only room in the building sufficiently spacious for the purpose.
