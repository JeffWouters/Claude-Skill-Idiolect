I like hotels.  I like them, I suppose, a good deal better than most of the people who inhabit them.  Listen to the guests in any lobby of an evening.  Half of them are grumbling about the service; the soup was cold, the bell-boy was dilatory, the clerk was insolent, the chambermaid was nowhere to be discovered.  The other half are boasting about their hotel, which is incomparably the finest in the city, and which they appear to have constructed with their own hands.  Neither party, so far as I can observe, is having a particularly agreeable time.  It seems to me a pity.  A hotel is a delightful institution, if you will only let it alone and permit it to entertain you.

I once knew a young man, very wealthy, who carried this principle rather further than anybody else I have heard of.  He had discovered that he enjoyed hotels--and he did not see why he should confine himself to one.  So he changed his hotel every day.  He inhabited, as it were, the whole city at once.  He'd pack in the morning, and by evening he would be unpacking somewhere else, among unfamiliar carpets and strange waiters, with a different view from a different window, and with the pleasant sensation, which I imagine never quite deserted him, of a traveller who has arrived without the tiresome necessity of having gone anywhere.  It was variety he wanted, and he got it.  I don't know that I ever met a more contented man.  Some time afterwards he was put in jail in France.  Here, you would say, his experiment came to an end.  Not at all.  He wrote to his friends, and what he wrote about was the food.  The prison food, he informed them, was excellent.  I have never been able to decide whether this was the soundest criticism of hotels ever delivered, or merely the final triumph of a thoroughly good-natured traveller.
