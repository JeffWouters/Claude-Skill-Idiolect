It is the fashion, just now, to look back on the earlier ages with a sort of indulgent pity. They were wrong about nearly everything a schoolboy now knows; and we are right. Granted. But it is a curious thing that those same benighted people made art we cannot equal, framed laws we still live under, and won for us a liberty we have done very little to enlarge. Their science was wrong; their art, their justice and their freedom were not. One does not need a correct theory of nature to know what a just judge is, or what a free man owes his city. The two kinds of knowledge, in short, are not the same kind, and the second does not go out of date, as the first does, with each new discovery.

What, then, is culture? It is not--as one sometimes hears--a polite acquaintance with old books for the sake of seeming well read. It is simply the habit of keeping the whole of our accumulated experience in view, so that we need not learn every lesson over again, at our own expense and with a great deal of unnecessary pain. A people without it is like a man without a memory; he may be very clever, but he will spend half his life rediscovering what his grandfather could have told him. Progress that forgets the past is not progress at all; it is merely going round in a circle rather fast. I fancy that a good deal of what passes for novelty to-day is of this sort. The point is that no one can surpass the earlier thinkers without first knowing what they understood. Otherwise, being ignorant of the ground they covered, we shall merely walk it again and call it new country. You cannot climb past a man on the ladder unless you have found the ladder. And the ladder, one must admit, was built by them.
