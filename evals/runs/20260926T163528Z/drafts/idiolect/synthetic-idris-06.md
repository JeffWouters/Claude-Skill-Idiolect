Why should a question as small as what to call something provoke such stubborn disagreement among people who otherwise work together quite happily? Anyone who has sat through a long argument about the name of a service or a field will recognise the peculiar intensity of it, the sense that far more is at stake than the words themselves, and it may be that far more usually is. A name, once adopted, shapes how everyone else thinks and talks about the thing it describes, so whoever's name is adopted gains a quiet and lasting influence over the conversations that follow. Naming conventions help, and teams that have agreed on them tend to squabble less over case and prefixes and plurals; but a convention can only govern the form of a name, not the question of what the thing is for, and it is that second question which most naming disputes are really about.

It seems to me that the more useful approach is to treat a naming debate as what it often is, a discussion of scope conducted in disguise. Rather than asking which name sounds best, we might ask what each candidate excludes: which responsibilities it would place outside the thing, and which requests it would make easier to decline. We might also check, perhaps more often than feels necessary, whether everyone involved actually shares the same understanding of what is being named, since two people can agree on a word while imagining quite different things behind it. When the thing itself changes, as things in software tend to do, there is a case for renaming it despite the cost — the broken references, the migration, the period in which both names are in circulation — because a name that no longer describes its object will mislead everyone who reads it. None of this is likely to make the conversation any shorter, though it does, I suspect, make it a conversation about the right thing.

Perhaps the argument over a name is never really about the name at all, but about what we are willing to agree the thing should be.
