It has never been altogether clear to me why the word "undertaker" should have come to mean what it does.  An undertaker, one would suppose, is simply a person who undertakes something, and nearly everybody undertakes something or other in the course of a working day.  The doctor undertakes to cure you; the lawyer undertakes to get you out of your difficulty, or (as the case may be) into a larger one.  Either of them might, with perfect propriety, have claimed the title.  Neither did.  By some obscure process of which the dictionaries give no satisfactory account, the word settled upon the gentleman who conducts burials, and there it has stayed. Having settled there, it began to gather associations, and not agreeable ones.  The undertaker became a stock figure of comedy, long of face and black of coat, rubbing his hands in a manner not quite suited to the occasion.  At the same time he became a figure of gloom.  The very word, pronounced, was apt to cast a chill over a dinner table.  It was, so to speak, both a joke and a shudder, and a man cannot conduct a dignified business under a name which makes people either laugh or shiver.

So the name was changed.  The undertaker became a "funeral director," which sounds considerably more substantial, and rather suggests a board meeting.  In this he was only following a general movement.  All about us, occupations have been quietly promoted in title without any noticeable alteration in duties; [example needed: an upgraded job title] and the funeral trade, perceiving which way the wind blew, did not intend to be left behind.  Now, I understand, even "funeral director" is losing its lustre.  The fashionable word is "mortician."  It has a pleasantly scientific ring, like physician or electrician, and it contains nothing whatever that anybody could laugh at.  Whether it will keep its dignity longer than its predecessors did remains to be seen.
