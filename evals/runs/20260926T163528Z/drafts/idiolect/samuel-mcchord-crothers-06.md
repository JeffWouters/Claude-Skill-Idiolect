It was raining in Wales, and I had given up the hope of seeing anything but the inside of the hotel. The traveler who is kept indoors by the weather becomes grateful for small historical mercies, and is disposed to accept whatever antiquity is offered him without asking too closely for its credentials. So when a servant of the house, observing my disconsolate attitude at the window, pointed across the wet street to an unremarkable building and informed me that it had been the cell of St. David, I received the information with more enthusiasm than it perhaps deserved. I asked what else was known of the saint. He considered the question with the gravity of a man upon whom the reputation of his country depended, and replied that St. David was some relation of King Arthur. Beyond that he would not go.

I did not press him. A vague kinship is often the most satisfactory kind, for it cannot be disproved and it confers a certain dignity upon both parties.

Some time afterward I fell in with a Welsh biographical compendium, [title needed: the compendium], and turned to it for a more exact account. The compiler was a conscientious person who approached the problem in a thoroughly scholarly manner. Was St. David the nephew of Arthur, or was he his uncle? The authorities, it appeared, were divided, and he weighed their claims with an impartiality that did him credit, setting nephew against uncle with as much care as though the honor of both houses hung upon the result. Then he made a concession which I thought extraordinarily handsome. It was possible, he admitted, that Arthur was not a man at all, but a constellation. This would have daunted a less resolute biographer. It did not daunt him. As for the objection that the dates would not agree, he disposed of it in a sentence; chronology, he intimated, was a pedantic consideration which need not detain a serious inquirer into the lives of saints.

I closed the book with a new respect for the servant. He had said that the saint was some relation of the king, and he had said no more; the compendium, after much labor and a good deal of astronomy, had arrived at precisely the same conclusion, with the added uncertainty as to whether the king was in the sky. On the whole I preferred the servant's version. It was shorter.
