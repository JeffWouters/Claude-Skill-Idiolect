One is ready to grant, I say, that truth is a very fine thing. Let us praise it, by all means; let us teach it to our children, and punish them when they forget it. But I cannot think that any one of us lives by it quite so strictly as he pretends. Look about you at the next dinner-party. The lady on your left has hair that did not grow on her head, and a colour in her cheeks that did not come from her blood. She has not said a word, yet she is telling you something that is not so. False hair and paint are, frankly, a silent untruth; they are made for nothing else. And I do not find that the lady is the worse for them, or that you are.

Then there is the host. He has given you a dull evening--a very dull evening--and at the door you take his hand and thank him for it. Is that a vice? I do not believe it. It is a kindness, and a small one, and he would be a churl who withheld it. The man who looks his host in the eye and says, "I have been bored," has told the truth, I suppose; but he has told it for his own satisfaction, and not for anybody's good.

The matter, it seems to me, is simple enough. If you wish to live in society, you must take society's rules along with its dinners. You cannot have the one without the other. And the rules (I am speaking, you see, of the harmless ones) include a certain number of lies that everybody expects and nobody is deceived by. To refuse them is not to be honest; it is merely to be rude. If one does not care to pay the price, one is quite free to stay at home--and nobody, I fancy, will greatly miss one.
