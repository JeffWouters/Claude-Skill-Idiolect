Anyone who has written a progress report will know the particular discomfort of the exercise. The template asks what has been done, and so we list it: the tickets closed, the milestones reached, the meetings held and the documents circulated. What the template rarely asks is what we have learned, and this omission matters more than it might first appear. A project is not simply a sequence of tasks moving from one column to another; it is a gradual process of discovering what the work actually involves, and much of that discovery is invisible in a list of completed items. We may have closed a dozen tasks in a week and learned nothing of consequence, or closed none and learned that the whole approach needs to be reconsidered. The report, as usually constructed, treats these two weeks as though the first were the better one.

What might a more honest report look like? One possibility is that it would state, alongside whatever has been completed, how confident the team actually is in the remaining plan, and which risks remain open. Not the risks that have been neatly logged and assigned an owner, but the ones that still keep people uneasy: the dependency nobody quite understands, the assumption that has not yet been tested, the estimate that everyone privately suspects is optimistic. A report of this kind would be less tidy and, in some respects, less reassuring. It would also give its readers something closer to the information they need in order to make decisions, which is presumably why reports exist at all.

The difficulty is that the obstacle here is not really the template. Most organisations exert a steady, often unspoken pressure on people to appear more certain than they are. Confidence is rewarded; hesitation is read as weakness, or as a sign that someone has not done their homework. Under these conditions it is entirely rational to report activity rather than insight, since activity can be demonstrated and insight can only be asserted, and to present risks as managed rather than open, since an open risk invites questions that nobody wants to answer in a status meeting. The report becomes a kind of performance, and those who read it learn, over time, to discount it accordingly.

Changing this would require something more than a new format. It would require those who receive reports to welcome uncertainty when it is honestly stated, rather than treating it as a failure to be corrected. That is a harder shift than it sounds, because it asks leaders to tolerate the discomfort of not knowing. I suspect, though, that it is the only change likely to make progress reports describe the project rather than the mood around it.
