Due dates often begin life as something close to a guess. Someone needs a date for a plan, a date is offered, and the conversation moves on. What happens next is curious. Once the date has been announced, and particularly once it has been repeated in a meeting or written into a slide, it ceases to be a guess and becomes something more like a reputation. The person who named it is now associated with it, and to miss it would be to be seen as unreliable. So people find themselves defending dates they never quite believed in, long after the evidence has turned against them, because the date has become a question of standing rather than of scheduling.

A useful discipline, when a date begins to look unrealistic, is to ask plainly what missing it would really cost. Sometimes the answer is significant: a contractual obligation, a dependency in another team, [example needed]. Surprisingly often, though, the honest answer is that very little would happen at all, beyond a certain awkwardness and the need to explain. The date was arbitrary when it was set, and it remains arbitrary now; only the social weight around it has grown. Naming the actual consequence, rather than the imagined one, tends to shrink the problem to its proper size.

Where a date does need to move, there is a strong case for renegotiating it early. A week's notice gives others room to adjust their own plans; a day's notice merely transfers the crisis to someone else. Most people understand this perfectly well, and yet the conversation is deferred again and again. The obstacle, I suspect, is rarely a lack of information. It is the fear of disappointing others — of watching a manager's face fall, or of admitting that one's earlier confidence was misplaced. That fear is entirely human, and no process will abolish it. But it is worth recognising that the disappointment of an early renegotiation is almost always smaller than the disappointment of a late one.
