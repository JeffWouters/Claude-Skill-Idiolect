Ask most software teams whether they believe their own estimates and the answer, given privately, is usually some version of no. Yet the estimates continue to be produced, week after week, with considerable ceremony and very little conviction. Why should this be?

Part of the explanation, I think, is that estimates are rarely only what they claim to be. On the surface they are forecasts, attempts to say how long a piece of work will take. Underneath they function as a kind of bargaining between the business and its engineers: one side hoping for a smaller number, the other quietly padding against the unknown, and both aware that the figure eventually agreed upon reflects the negotiation at least as much as the work. There is nothing especially sinister in this. Bargaining is a reasonable way of reconciling competing pressures, provided everyone understands that it is taking place.

The trouble begins when the number outlives the conversation that produced it. An estimate offered tentatively, with all its caveats, has a way of reappearing weeks later as a commitment, stripped of its qualifications and attached to someone's name. At that point the engineers are no longer being held to a forecast but to a promise they never quite made, and it is hardly surprising that they approach the next estimate with even greater caution.

Beneath all of this lies a fact that is widely known and rarely said aloud: forecasting unfamiliar work is inherently unreliable. If a team has never built something before, it cannot know in advance where the difficulties will lie, and no amount of careful decomposition will change that. Perhaps the most useful thing an organisation could do is simply to acknowledge this openly — to treat an estimate for novel work as a statement of present ignorance rather than future certainty. The numbers would be no more accurate. They might, however, finally be trusted for what they are.
