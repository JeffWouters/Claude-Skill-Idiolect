When I am out of sorts I go and look at rooms.  I do not want a room.  I have a room.  But there is no pastime known to me so certain to sweeten a sour mood as the looking at rooms to let.  At home it is good; abroad it is better.  In a strange city, where everything is a little absurd to begin with, it is the best thing I know.

You begin with the advertisements.  An advertisement of a room to let is a kind of literature, and, like other kinds of literature, it is not to be taken too literally.  A room which is "spacious" will be found to admit of one chair.  A room which is "sunny" has been sunny, it is to be supposed, at some time.  "Every convenience" means that there is a bath somewhere in the house, on some floor or other.  And in England one must learn, besides, a new language.  What we at home call by one name, they call by another; and what they call by the name we use for something else is something else again.  [example needed]  I have climbed a good many stairs to find that I did not know what I had come to see.

The most charming rooms of all, to my mind, are in the old legal inns of London.  You go in through a gate, out of the roar, into a kind of quiet that does not seem to belong to a great city.  There are old walls, and old stairs, and old doors with names painted beside them.  The rooms are panelled, and low, and look out on a bit of grass.  They are altogether delightful.  They have, so far as I could discover, no comforts whatever.

And that is the lesson of the thing.  If you would get pleasure from looking at rooms, go modestly.  Do not expect a great deal.  Expect, indeed, very little.  Then you will not be disappointed--and you may very likely come away in a better humour than you went.
