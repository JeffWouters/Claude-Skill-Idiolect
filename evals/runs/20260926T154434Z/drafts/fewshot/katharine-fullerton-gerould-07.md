I do not know how other people feel about the American college, but I am exceedingly sorry for it. It is being pulled in two directions at once, and by people who do not seem to notice that they contradict each other. On one side we are told that the college is useless: that it teaches nothing a man can sell, and that four years spent in it are four years lost. On the other side we are told that it is too hard: that it asks too much of the young, and drives away the boy who would do very well in business. Both critics, you will observe, judge it by the same measure. They ask what it pays--socially, or in hard cash--and when it does not pay enough, they condemn it. That the college might exist for some other end does not, apparently, occur to them.

This is not snobbishness; it is a matter of what our children are to become. For the students themselves have changed. Two generations ago, a fair number of the young men who went to college went because they cared for scholarship, for its own sake and for no other. There are fewer of them now--far fewer. I cannot think who will deny it. The boy of to-day wants his degree as he wants his motor-car, and for much the same reasons [example needed].

But the trouble does not begin at college; it begins long before. Let us look, by all means, at the schools where very young children are taught. The methods in fashion there are designed, it would seem, to spare the child any effort he does not enjoy; and a child who has never been asked to make an effort will not know how to make one later. Then, when he is grown, he finds that he may enter college without examination--that nobody means to ask him, at the door, whether he has learned anything at all. Is it any wonder that standards fall? They have been undermined at the foundation and knocked away at the top. I do not see how they could do anything else.
