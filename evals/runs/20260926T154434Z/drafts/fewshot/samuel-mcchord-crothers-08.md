In a mining town, fame is not a thing to be hoarded by a few. It is common property, and the town has a generous way of passing it round, so that every resident, sooner or later, has his turn. It may be for a day or a week; it is seldom longer. But while it lasts it is complete.

Consider the turn that came to [name needed]. He was a prospector of no particular distinction until he struck rich ore. The next day he was the most important man in the camp. He had decided to be married, and he was determined that the whole town should share his happiness. So he invited everybody. Not content with that, he hired every carriage the place afforded, so that nobody should have the excuse of staying at home for want of a conveyance.

The wedding was to be at his cabin, which lay at a considerable distance from the town, in a spot better adapted to finding ore than to holding a wedding. Thither we set out in a long procession. I have seen many processions, but never one so representative. The officials of the town rode side by side with the gamblers, and the clergy were packed in with the miners. It was not a time for social distinctions. Everybody was a guest, and a guest of a man who had just struck it rich is on a level with every other guest.

On the long road there was abundant opportunity for conversation, and the conversation was of the most democratic kind. The official and the gambler found that they had a good deal in common, and the clergyman was not above learning something from the miner at his elbow.

As for the bridegroom, he bore his honors modestly. He knew, as we all knew, that his fame was a loan from the town, and that next week it would be somebody else's turn.
