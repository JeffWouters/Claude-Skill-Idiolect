Every few years, it seems, a team discovers a new way of sizing its work. Hours give way to points, points give way to shirt sizes, shirt sizes give way to some further scheme that promises at last to make the future legible. The new method is introduced with some enthusiasm, the ceremonies are adjusted, and after a period of adaptation the forecasts turn out to be about as reliable as they were before. It would be easy to conclude that the techniques themselves are at fault, but I suspect the more honest explanation lies elsewhere. What rarely changes when a new sizing method arrives is the organisation's attitude to uncertainty, and it is that attitude, far more than any unit of measurement, that determines whether an estimate is useful.

An estimate is, or ought to be, a statement about what we currently know. It is a snapshot of understanding at a particular moment, and it should become more accurate as that understanding improves. The difficulty is that many organisations treat it instead as a promise, fixed at the point when knowledge was at its thinnest and defended thereafter as though revising it were an admission of failure. Under those conditions no technique can help, because the problem is not how the number was produced but what happens to it afterwards. A team that is permitted, and indeed expected, to revise its estimates as it learns will produce better forecasts with the crudest of methods than a team that must stand by its first guess with the most sophisticated.

It may also help to distinguish more carefully between kinds of work. Familiar tasks, the sort a team has done many times before, can often be estimated with reasonable confidence; novel work, where nobody quite knows what will be encountered, cannot, and pretending otherwise merely hides the risk. Forecasting the two separately is a modest step. It asks nothing more than that we be candid about where our knowledge ends.
