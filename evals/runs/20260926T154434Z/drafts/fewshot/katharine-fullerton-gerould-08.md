One is ready to grant, I say, that a lie is a bad thing. Let us condemn it, by all means, when it cheats a man of his money or a woman of her good name. But there our respect for veracity bids us stop. There is a kind of truth-telling which is not a virtue at all, but a fault; and it is the more dangerous because those who practise it are so very sure of their own righteousness.

I do not know how other people feel about the blunt person--the person who "always says what he thinks"--but I have never been able to like him. He tells you that your new book is dull, that your hat is unbecoming, that your children are spoiled; and he tells you so with a curious glow of satisfaction. That glow is the point. His honesty is, frankly, a pleasure to him, and a selfish one. He is not trying to help you; he is enjoying himself at your expense. And he pays for his enjoyment, in the end, as such people always do: one by one, his friends drift away from him, and he cannot for the life of him think why. He has told the truth, has he not? He has; and he has lost, in the telling, a good deal more than he has gained.

Consider, on the other hand, the thousand small untruths that make daily life endurable. Some one asks you how you are, and you say that you are very well, thank you. Perhaps you are not very well. Perhaps you have slept badly and your head aches. But the question was not a request for information, and your answer was not meant to deceive. It is a courtesy, like taking off one's hat; and to answer it with a full account of one's symptoms would be, not honesty, but a kind of bad manners. Nobody is harmed by the formula. Everybody would be harmed--a little--by its abolition.

It is interesting, I think, to watch how the argument against cosmetics has shifted. Our grandmothers (I am speaking, you see, of the respectable ones) objected to paint because it was wicked: it belonged to a certain sort of woman, and no decent woman would be mistaken for her. That objection has very nearly disappeared. What one hears now is something quite different. Paint, we are told, is wrong because it deceives--because the cheek pretends to a colour that is not its own [example needed]. The moral scruple has become a scruple about truth. And I cannot help thinking that it is a weaker scruple than the old one; for if we are to forbid every harmless deceit, we shall have to forbid half the civilities of life along with the rouge-pot.
