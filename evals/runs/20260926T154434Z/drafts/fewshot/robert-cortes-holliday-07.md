Of the workings of a shop the novelist had, it was soon plain to us, only the haziest notion.  It was his idea, so far as we could make it out, that the salesman who had first waited upon him was, in effect, the firm.  This one man, he appeared to suppose, took the order, and wrapped the parcel, and addressed it, and carried it to the post, and kept the books, and sent out the bill.  That there might be a shipping department, a mail-order department, a credit department, a gentleman somewhere upstairs whose whole life was spent among accounts--of all this he had not the faintest suspicion.

Once he came in to order some books.  They were copies of his own works.  He wished them sent to a relative of his, a lady.  He named the titles, he told us how many, he was very clear and very pleasant about it; and then, with a nod and a smile, he went out of the door.  He had not said who the lady was.  He had not said where she lived.  The order stood upon the pad complete in every particular except the two which were necessary to fill it.

It was not simple to set right.  Every detail had to be drawn out of him, one at a time, as you might draw a timid animal from under a porch.  What was the lady's name?  Ah, her name.  Yes, of course.  He gave it.  And her address?  Her address--he thought a moment--her address was [address].  Each particular came forth separately, after a little pause, as though it were being fetched from some distant room of his mind where it was not often wanted.  And at each step he was sorry.  He apologised.  He apologised very sincerely, with a genuine distress, for giving so much trouble.  He really was very sorry.

We did not mind.  One does not, somehow, mind that kind of thing in that kind of man.  It was only that one saw, at such a moment, how the world of affairs looks to one who does not live in it: a single obliging person behind a counter, who knows everything, and does everything, and to whom it is quite unnecessary to explain.
