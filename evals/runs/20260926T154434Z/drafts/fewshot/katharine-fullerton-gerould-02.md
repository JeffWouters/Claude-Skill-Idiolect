Let us grant, to begin with, that truthfulness is not a very heroic virtue. For most of us, most of the time, it is simply the easiest course. The truth has the great merit of requiring no memory; one need not keep a ledger of what one has said, and to whom. And it makes, I suppose, the only sound footing on which two people can build anything at all. A friendship founded on pleasant fictions is a house on the sand--charming, perhaps, until the first high tide.

But there our praise of candour bids us pause. Consider the man who has made it his boast that he can never, under any circumstances, tell a lie. He is an admirable person; I should not care to tell him a secret. For a secret is not kept by silence alone. Sooner or later some one asks the direct question--it is always some one who has a right to ask it!--and the man who cannot lie must either answer or so conspicuously refuse to answer that the refusal tells everything. His friend's confidence is safe with him only so long as nobody is curious. That is a very precarious safety. I do not know how other people feel, but I would rather trust a man who could lie for me, and would not do it lightly, than one who could not do it at all.

And there are cases, rare though they be, in which the lie seems not merely excusable but right. The old example is the hunted man who has fled into your house, and the pursuer at the door who asks where he has gone. [example needed] Frankly, I cannot think who would send the pursuer upstairs. The purist may tell us that the truth must be spoken though the heavens fall; but it is not the purist's heavens that are falling. In such an hour, the lie is the only honest thing left to say.
