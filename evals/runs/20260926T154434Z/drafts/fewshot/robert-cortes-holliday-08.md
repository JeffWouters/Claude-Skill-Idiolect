If you wish to find out what the world thinks of you--not your friends, who are prejudiced, nor your enemies, who are prejudiced the other way, but the plain, disinterested stranger--go looking for a room.  I have done a good deal of it, and I have been told a number of things about myself which I had not before suspected.

There was the landlady who was a doctor.  She had a room, and she was willing to let it.  But she explained, very pleasantly, that she would wish to keep in it her surgical equipment.  It would be there, in the corner.  It would not be in my way.  I looked at the corner, and I thought of the evenings, and of the lamp, and of the instruments glinting in the shadow while I tried to read, and I said I would think it over.  I am thinking it over still.

There was the landlady who looked me up and down and asked me what I did.  I told her.  She did not believe me.  Or rather, she believed that whatever I did it was not quite the sort of thing one talks about.  Her manner said that she had seen my kind before.  She did not say what my kind was.  I have wondered since.

Then there are the respectable houses.  In a respectable house you do not merely take a room; you are admitted to it, as to a club.  You must have references.  Who knows you?  Who will say that you are what you say you are?  You feel, as you give names, like a man applying for a position of trust.

And there are the fashionable districts of London, [district], where the streets are quiet and the doors are painted and the knockers shine.  Here no one asks what you do, or who knows you.  They do not need to.  They need only to know one thing, and that is whether you have money--a great deal of money.  You have not.  They know it at once.  It is, in its way, the most honest judgment of all.
