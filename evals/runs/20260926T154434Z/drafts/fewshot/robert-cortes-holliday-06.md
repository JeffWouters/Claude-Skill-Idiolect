They went out, the three of them, or however many there were of them, in a manner which we have not forgotten.  They linked arms.  Arm in arm, the novelist in the middle, they swung through the door, and, once upon the pavement, they fell into step and marched up the street like a little company of soldiers going off to a war of no great danger.  We all watched them out of sight.  It was, somehow, a very happy exit.

He came back.  He came back a number of times after that.  And it was then that we saw him as a browser.  There are browsers and browsers.  There is the browser who glances along a shelf as a man glances along a line of faces in a crowd, looking for one he knows.  There is the browser who picks up a book, weighs it in his hand, and puts it down again.  The novelist was not of these kinds.  He browsed with the whole of himself.  He bent to the lower shelves; he stretched to the upper ones; he put his face close, very close, to the backs of the books, and peered at them, one after another, as a small boy peers into the window of a toy shop.  Nothing else existed for him.  You might have dropped a stack of dictionaries behind him, and he would not have turned his head.  It was a total, a childlike, absorption, and it was very pleasant to see.

Always he asked for the same assistant.  And here, perhaps, it is in order to say a word concerning the lot of the assistant in a bookshop, or in any shop.  His lot is to be polite.  That is the whole of it.  He is to be polite to the customer who knows what he wants and to the customer who does not; to the customer who is gracious and to the customer who is not.  The customer may look at him as though he were a piece of furniture; he may speak to him as though he were something less than that.  The customer may scorn him--openly, elaborately, with a curl of the lip.  The assistant bows.  "Yes, sir," he says.  "Certainly, sir."  It is what he is paid for.  And so a customer who asks for him by name, and seems to be glad to see him, is a thing he remembers.
