Of the way in which a shop is carried on he had, so far as one could discover, no notion whatever.  It was not that he held any wrong ideas about it.  He simply had not given the matter a thought, and saw no reason why he should.

A bookshop of any size is a fairly complicated affair.  There are the men who sell, and the men who wrap, and the men who keep the accounts, and the men downstairs who send the parcels out, and somewhere behind a door a person who orders the books in the first place.  None of this had reached him.  In his mind the firm consisted, I believe, of one salesman: the salesman he happened to be talking to.  That salesman took the order, found the book, made out the bill, tied up the parcel, carried it to the post, and, for all the novelist knew, wrote the book too.  It was a simple and a trusting view of commerce, and there was something rather charming in it.

One day he wished to send copies of his own works to a relative of his.  A handsome thought.  He chose the books with care, and said that they were to go to her, and thanked the salesman, and went away.  Well, to whom were they to go?  He had not said.  Where did she live?  He had not said that either.  The books sat upon the counter, done up and addressed to nobody, while the shop considered what to do.

When at length the question was put to him, the information had to be got out of him a piece at a time.  Her name, first.  Then the street.  Then the number of the house.  Then the town.  Each item came out slowly, as though it had to be fetched from some distant room of his mind where it was not often wanted.  And with each one he apologised.  He was very sorry.  He had quite forgotten.  It was stupid of him.  He apologised, I think, more often than there were details to supply.
