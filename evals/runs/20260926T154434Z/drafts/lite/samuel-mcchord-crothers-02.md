There is a kind of affection which attaches itself only to places that have no claim upon it. I have it for a new silver camp in Nevada. It was not beautiful, it was not healthy, and it was not, by any standard I had been taught, respectable. I think of it with more tenderness than of many towns that were all three.

The smelter stood over the camp and poured out its smoke day and night. A stranger might have supposed this a misfortune. The inhabitants knew better. The smoke, they told you, was the finest disinfectant in the world; no germ could live in it. This was undoubtedly true. Nothing else could live in it either. There was not a green thing within reach of the fumes, and the hills stood about as bare as the day they were made. But the people were well, or said they were, and in a new camp what a man says is a large part of the evidence.

All the life of the place was gathered along one street. There were the saloons, which were many, and the gaming halls, which were prosperous. There were the churches, which were feeble, and which carried on their work with the patient air of missionaries among a people who had not yet decided whether they wished to be saved. And there were two newspapers, which were at war. Each regarded the other as the chief obstacle to the advancement of the camp, and each said so in every issue. The feud gave the town a sense of public life. A community which can support two editors who despise each other is not without resources.

A small train connected us with the outer world. It came and went with a certain informality, and its arrival was an event. It brought letters, and supplies, and now and then a newcomer who looked about him with visible misgiving.

Over everything lay the dust. It was the one citizen who never left. It was in the food, in the bedding, in the ledgers and in the sermons. We did not fight it; we had come to terms with it, as one comes to terms with a relative.

I do not know that I should wish to live there again. But I should not wish to have missed it.
