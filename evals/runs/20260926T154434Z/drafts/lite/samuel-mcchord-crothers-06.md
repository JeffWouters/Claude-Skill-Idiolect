The rogues of fiction are seldom left to themselves. However gaily they go about their business, there is always the author at their elbow, and the author has his own opinion of them. He may admire their wit and enjoy their escapes, but in the end he sees to it that they are found out, or repent, or at the very least are made to look a little foolish in the last chapter. The reader is let off with a moral, and the rogue with a sentence.

But the real rogue writes his own book, and there is no one to stand at his elbow. I have lately been reading the memoir of an early American swindler, [name needed], and what strikes me most is not his wickedness but his entire satisfaction with it. He does not judge himself, because it never occurs to him that there is anything to judge.

His career was of a pleasing variety. He was a soldier, and then a preacher. He forged money, and then he taught school. At intervals he was in prison. To the ordinary mind these callings do not follow naturally one upon another. To him they were all simply openings, and he entered each of them with the same cheerful readiness and left it with the same absence of regret. When he was preaching he preached, and when he was forging he forged, and in neither case does he seem to have done the work badly.

What sustained him through it all was a good conscience. It was not the kind of conscience that troubles its owner in the night. It was, rather, a confidential adviser who could always be relied upon to see his side of the case. Public Opinion was against him, and said so in the courts. But Public Opinion was a stranger, and his Conscience was an old friend. It is natural that he should have preferred the friend.

There is something almost enviable in such a state of mind. Most of us go through life with a divided allegiance, half wishing to do as we please and half afraid of what the neighbors will say. He had no such difficulty. He approved of himself from the beginning, and he never found reason to change his mind.
