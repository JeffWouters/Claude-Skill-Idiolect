Of all the pastimes open to a man of small means and a sour temper, I know of none so reliable as looking at rooms to let.  You need not want a room.  You need only go and look at one.  Before you have climbed the second flight of stairs your grievance against the world has somehow mislaid itself, and by the time the landlady has shown you the cupboard you are in excellent spirits.  Abroad the cure is surer still.  In a strange city every door opens on a small play, and you are admitted free.

The advertisements, of course, are works of the imagination.  A room described as "sunny" has at one time or another been visited by the sun.  A room described as "commodious" will hold a bed and a man, provided he stands.  "Every convenience" means that there is a tap somewhere on the premises.  And the English, it should be said, do not speak our language in this department at all.  [example needed: a British term for a room and what an American would call it]  The seeker must learn a new vocabulary before he can learn to disbelieve it.

Well, the most charming rooms of all, to my mind, are in the old inns of the law in London.  Here are staircases worn hollow by generations of lawyers, doors with names painted over names, windows that look upon a quiet court and a tree, a silence that the roar of the town outside does not quite get into.  Here, too, are no comforts whatever.  The bath is a matter for negotiation.  The heating is a matter for faith.  The kitchen, when there is one, is a matter for regret.

The wise seeker, then, goes modestly.  He does not expect the advertisement.  He does not expect America.  He expects four walls, a view of something old, and a landlady with a story.  He usually gets them.  And if he does not take the room, why, he has had his afternoon.
