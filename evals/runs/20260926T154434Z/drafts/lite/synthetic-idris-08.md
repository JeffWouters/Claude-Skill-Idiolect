There is a particular kind of meeting in which everyone knows the date is not going to be met and nobody says so. The date was announced some weeks earlier, perhaps casually, perhaps to reassure someone senior, and since then it has acquired a weight quite out of proportion to the thinking that produced it. It may be that the difficulty with arbitrary due dates is less about planning than about reputation.

Once a date has been said aloud, it stops being an estimate and becomes something closer to a public statement about the person who made it. Missing it feels like being found out. People will therefore defend dates they no longer believe in, working longer hours, trimming quality where nobody will immediately notice, and hoping the gap closes of its own accord. It rarely does.

It seems to me that the most useful question in such a situation is a very plain one: what would it actually cost to miss this date? Sometimes the answer is considerable, because something outside the team genuinely depends on it. More commonly, I suspect, very little would happen beyond some mild disappointment and a revised plan. Is it any wonder that the question is so seldom asked, when asking it means admitting that the date was never as fixed as everyone behaved as though it was?

The sensible course is to renegotiate early, while there is still time to change scope or expectations without drama. The obstacle is rarely practical. It is, in the end, the fear of disappointing others, and of being the first to say what everyone else already knows.

Perhaps the date was never the real commitment; the real commitment was to tell each other the truth about it.
