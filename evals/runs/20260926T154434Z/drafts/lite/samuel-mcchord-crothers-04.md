There is a Victorian poem in which the speaker looks out upon the peace of his country and finds it intolerable. It is not the peace of the saints he sees. It is the peace of the shopkeeper. Commerce has become the religion of the land, and Money is its god. Men cheat one another and call it business. They neglect the poor and call it economy. The whole society seems to him to have grown soft and mean together.

From this he turns with longing to the thought of war. He does not want it for its own sake. He wants it because it will break the spell. When the drums beat, he believes, men will forget their ledgers. They will find something to live for that is larger than themselves, and something to die for that is worth the dying. The selfish will be shamed, and the noble will come into their own.

It is easy for us to smile at this. We know, or think we know, what war is, and we are not inclined to take lessons in nobility from the battlefield. But I should not dismiss the poet too quickly. He has seen something true, even if he has drawn the wrong conclusion from it. A peace that means only that every man is left free to grow rich is not a very inspiring peace. It is no wonder that a generous young man should tire of it.

Nor is the mood peculiar to one poet or one age. It comes back with every generation. The young find the comfortable world of their fathers dull and a little shabby. They long for a cause that will ask everything of them. If no better cause is offered, they will take up the oldest one.

That is why the enthusiasm deserves to be taken seriously. It is not to be answered with a sneer. It is to be answered with a peace that asks as much of men as war does.
