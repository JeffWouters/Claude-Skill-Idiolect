I have long held that if you would know the true character of a celebrated man you should ask the fellow who sold him his collars.  The shop assistant sees him when there is nothing whatever to be gained by performing.  The great man does not play to the gallery before a clerk.  The clerk is not the gallery.  He is scarcely in the house.  So what the clerk gets is the man himself, with the paint off.

I had this opportunity once, behind the counter of a bookstore, with a novelist whose name was then on every lip.  I had braced myself for the manner, the lordly condescension, the weary patience of genius with the tradesman.  I did not get it.  He talked to me as one man talks to another who happens to know something about the matter in hand.  He talked earnestly.  He leaned upon the counter (it was not a very clean counter) and put his questions as though my answers might be worth hearing.

What did he want to know?  He wanted to know, very politely, what share of our sales went to British fiction.  How did it compare with the native article?  Was it rising or falling?  I told him what I knew, which was [detail needed: what the clerk told him], and he received it with the grave attention of a man taking down evidence.

Now, here is the curious part.  Not once, in all that talk, did he mention his own books.  They stood in a handsome row within reach of his elbow.  He did not glance at them.  He did not ask how they were selling.  A lesser man will contrive to find his own volumes in a strange shop within the first two minutes, and will turn them face outward before he leaves.

He did mention one other novelist, a popular rival, whose books were then going out of our door by the armful.  He disposed of him in a sentence.  [quote needed: the novelist's dismissal of the rival]

Then he thanked me, and went.  It is not often that the people who write books treat the people who sell them as equals.  He did.
