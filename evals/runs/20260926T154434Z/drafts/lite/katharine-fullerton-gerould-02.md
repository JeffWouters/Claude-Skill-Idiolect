Truthfulness is, for most of us, the easiest of the virtues. One has only to say what is so; it asks no memory, no invention, and very little courage. And it makes the soundest footing for any relationship. A friend whose word one can take is worth a dozen who merely amuse. I have no quarrel with the plain truth-teller. I only wonder whether he is quite so safe a friend as he supposes.

For consider his position when he is trusted with a secret. Some one asks him a direct question. He cannot lie; he has made a principle of it. He may refuse to answer--but a refusal, as every one knows, is very often an answer in itself. The man who can never lie cannot safely guard a friend's confidence. He is honest at his friend's expense. That is not integrity. It is vanity.

You may say that this opens the door to every convenient falsehood. Granted: yet the door is a narrow one, and I do not propose to widen it. The lie told for one's own ease or profit remains what it always was. My point is only this: there are rare cases in which the truth does harm and the lie does none.

The classic instance--the locus classicus, if you like--is the hunted man. He has fled past your gate; his pursuers come up and ask which way he went. Is one bound to tell them? The strict moralist says yes (God save the mark!), and would send them down the right road with a clear conscience. Most of us, I suspect, would point the other way, and sleep none the worse for it. The instinct is sound. Truth is owed to those who have a right to it, and the pursuer has forfeited his.

Such cases are rare. Most of our lives need no lie at all. But a rule that cannot bend for the hunted man is not a rule. It is a trap.
