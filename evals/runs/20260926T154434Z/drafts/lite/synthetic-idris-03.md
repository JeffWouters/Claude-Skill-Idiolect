How often has a team adopted a new way of estimating and found, some months later, that nothing much has changed? It may be that the trouble with estimation lies less in our techniques than in how our organisations feel about uncertainty.

New sizing methods tend to arrive with considerable promise. [example needed: a sizing technique a team adopted] replaces whatever came before, the vocabulary shifts, and for a while there is a sense of progress. Yet the pressures underneath remain as they were. If an organisation treats every estimate as a promise, then any method will eventually be bent towards producing numbers that feel safe to promise, and the new technique will inherit the distortions of the old one.

There is also the question of what an estimate is for. An estimate is, arguably, a statement of what we currently know, and what we currently know changes as the work proceeds. An estimate that is never revised is therefore not a sign of discipline but of a reluctance to learn. Who among us has not watched a figure produced at the very start of a project be defended long after everyone involved had stopped believing it?

It seems to me that the most useful step is to separate familiar work from novel work before forecasting either. Work a team has done many times can be estimated with reasonable confidence, because the team has, in some sense, already measured it. Work that is genuinely new cannot, and pretending otherwise only hides the uncertainty where nobody can see it. Treating the two differently, and saying openly which is which, does more than any new method to make a forecast honest.

Perhaps the question is not how to estimate better but how to become comfortable saying what we do not yet know.
