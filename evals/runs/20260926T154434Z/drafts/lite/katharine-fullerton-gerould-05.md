We have decided, it seems, that every American shall have a degree, and we have gone about the business in the simplest way: by making the degree cost nothing in particular. Our schools and colleges hand out their credentials to any one who will sit in the appointed chairs for the appointed number of years. Real study is not required of the candidate; it is merely permitted him, if he has a taste for it. The diploma has become, not a certificate of learning, but a receipt for attendance.

I do not blame the institutions alone. They have been pushed, and pushed from both ends of the social scale. The wealthy parent does not wish his child to work hard--he has not, perhaps, worked very hard himself, and he sees no reason why his son should. The poorer parent, on the other hand, wants for his child exactly the prestige the rich man's child enjoys, and he wants it on the same easy terms. Between the two, the college that asks for genuine effort is left with very few friends. Some one will say that this is democracy. Granted; but it is democracy of a curious sort, which levels by lowering the mark rather than by raising the pupil.

Nor is the lowering confined to the matter of effort. It extends to the very notion of what a school is for. One finds, more and more, that the crafts and the practical subjects are given the place of honour, and that the training of the intellect is treated as a quaint survival (to be indulged, like Latin verse, by the few who insist!). I have nothing against carpentry. I fancy a boy may learn a good deal from it. The point is that a school exists to discipline the mind, and a school that has forgotten this has forgotten its reason for being. We shall not make every one educated by calling every one so. We shall only make the word mean nothing.
