Why does a weekly meeting exist? You may have sat through one recently and found yourself unable to answer, not because the meeting was bad, exactly, but because nobody present could remember what it was for. It may be that most standing meetings are born useful and simply outlive the circumstance that called them into being.

The pattern is familiar enough. A meeting begins in response to something: [example needed: the trigger that started a standing meeting]. For a while it earns its place, because the problem it addresses is live and everyone in the room can see it. Then the trigger fades. The problem is solved, or absorbed into ordinary work, or quietly forgotten, and the meeting carries on regardless, since nothing about its calendar entry records why it was created. The drift goes unnoticed, because it happens gradually and because attending has become a habit rather than a decision.

The obvious response is to scrap such meetings wholesale, and there is considerable satisfaction in imagining the empty calendar that would follow. I suspect this is riskier than it looks. Meetings that have lost their original purpose do not always lose their function; they may have acquired another one along the way, without anyone deciding that they should.

There is also the matter of contact. A standing meeting, whatever its agenda, guarantees that a group of people will see one another at a regular interval. It supplies, in some sense, a rhythm to working together, and it offers occasions for the small exchanges from which a team's sense of itself is built. None of this appears in the meeting's stated purpose, and so none of it is counted when the meeting is judged. Is it any wonder that a team which cancels its standing meetings may find, some months later, that its members know one another rather less well?

The weekly meeting is, in the end, one of the few fixtures of working life that persists without anyone having to argue for it. Perhaps that is its weakness and its value at once: it survives not because it is needed but because it is there, and sometimes being there was the need all along.
