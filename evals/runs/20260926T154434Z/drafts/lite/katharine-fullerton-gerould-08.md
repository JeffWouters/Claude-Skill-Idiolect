There is a kind of person who prides himself on telling the truth at all times and to every one, and who expects to be admired for it. I have known a few of him. He will tell you that your new hat is unbecoming, that your friend's book is dull, that you look tired; and when you wince, he will remind you that he is only being honest. One is meant to be grateful. I confess I have never managed it.

For the plain fact is that strict truth-telling, in the small affairs of everyday social life, is not a virtue but a fault. It is very often a selfish pleasure. The blunt man enjoys his bluntness; it gives him a comfortable sense of his own integrity, and it costs him nothing. It costs other people a good deal. He says what he thinks because he likes saying it, not because any one is the better for hearing it--and he generally ends by saying it to an empty room. Friendships do not survive many such candours. Nor should they, perhaps.

Some one will object that a lie is a lie, whatever its size. Granted; but let us look at the lies in question. A neighbour asks how you are, and you say you are quite well, though you slept badly and your head aches. Is that a sin? You have not deceived him about anything he wished to know. He did not want a report on your health; he wanted a greeting, and you gave him one. The formula is a courtesy, not a statement, and both of you understand it so. To answer it with the literal truth would be as odd as answering "Good morning" with a forecast of the weather. These routine untruths (and there are dozens of them in a single day!) do no harm to any one. They keep the machinery of life turning without a grinding of gears.

It is instructive, I think, to watch what has happened to the old quarrel about cosmetics. The older objection to paint and powder was a moral one: the painted woman was a vain woman, if not a worse one. That objection has very nearly disappeared. What one hears now is something rather different--that rouge is a kind of deceit, that the woman who uses it is pretending to a complexion she has not got. The charge has moved from morality to honesty. I fancy the moralists of to-day are hardly more reasonable than those of yesterday. Nobody is deceived by rouge who has any wish to know the truth; and nobody who has no such wish is harmed by being spared it.

My point is only this: there is a truthfulness that serves people and a truthfulness that serves oneself. The second deserves no praise.
