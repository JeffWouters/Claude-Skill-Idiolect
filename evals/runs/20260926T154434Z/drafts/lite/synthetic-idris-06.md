What is a progress report actually for? In principle it tells those who are not close to the work how the work is going; in practice it tends to tell them something narrower, which is what has been finished. The weekly update lists the tasks closed, the milestones reached, the features delivered, and each item is true. It may be that the trouble with progress reports lies not in any individual line but in what the format leaves unsaid, so that a report can be accurate in every detail and still misrepresent the state of a project.

A team learns a great deal in the course of a week that never appears as a finished task: the discovery that an assumption was wrong, the realisation that a dependency is more fragile than anyone thought, the slow accumulation of understanding about what the problem really is. None of this fits comfortably in a list of things done. A week in which a team completed little but learned that its approach would not work may well have been the most valuable week of the project, and yet the report will make it look like the least productive.

There is also the question of certainty. Reports are written in the language of accomplishment, and that language carries an implied confidence that the people writing them rarely feel. You may have written such a report yourself, choosing words that sound settled because unsettled words would invite questions you could not yet answer. The pressure here is arguably organisational rather than personal: organisations reward the appearance of control, and people quite reasonably learn to supply it, presenting themselves as more certain than they are because certainty is what the format seems to ask for.

It seems to me that a better report would say less about what has been done and more about what is known. It would state plainly how confident the team is in its current plan and why; it would name the open risks, including the ones that are uncomfortable to mention; it would record what the team has learned, especially where that learning changed the plan. None of this is complicated. It requires only that those who read the reports treat uncertainty as information rather than as a failing, which is considerably harder than it sounds.

The progress report is, in the end, one of the few regular occasions on which a team describes its own work to the wider organisation, and what it chooses to leave out says a good deal about what it believes is safe to say. Perhaps the most honest report is not the one with the longest list of completed tasks but the one that admits how much remains unknown.
