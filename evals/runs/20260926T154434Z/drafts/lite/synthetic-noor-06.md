I darn my own socks. Badly, at first. Better now.

Nobody taught me. I had my grandfather's old darning tool and not much else. [example needed: how the tool came to me]. [example needed: what the tool looks like]. So I taught myself with it, one hole at a time.

Here is how I do it. First I turn the sock inside out and push the tool into the heel, under the hole. That stretches the knit flat. Not tight. Just flat. Then I thread a darning needle with wool as close to the sock as I have. [example needed: what wool]. I run a line of small stitches round the hole, a little way into the good knit. That is the frame.

Then the warp. Long stitches straight across the hole, side to side, close together. Then I turn the sock and weave the other way. Over one, under one. Back again, under where I went over. It builds up into a little square of cloth. A patch, made in place.

The whole job is tension. Pull too hard and the patch puckers. It feels fine in the hand. It does not feel fine in a boot. An overtight darn is a hard ridge under the heel, and by the end of the day you know about it. I have made that mistake more than once. I still make it. So now I leave every stitch a little slack, more than looks right, and let the wash settle it.

People ask why I bother. Socks are cheap. But the time does not feel wasted. It feels like the opposite. An evening in the chair, the needle going over and under. Nothing to decide. And a darned heel is stronger than the one that wore through. The patch is thicker than the knit around it. It is usually the last part of the sock to go.

This morning I put on a darned pair. The patch sat flat under the heel. The tool is back in the drawer, with the needle beside it.
