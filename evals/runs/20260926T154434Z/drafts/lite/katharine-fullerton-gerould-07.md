The colleges are in a curious position. They are attacked, and attacked from two sides at once, by critics who do not seem to notice that their charges cancel each other. One set of critics finds the colleges useless: the young man comes out of them no better fitted to make his way in the world than he went in. Another set finds them too hard: the young man is asked to learn things he will never need, and fails, and is made unhappy. Both complaints rest on the same assumption, which is that a college is to be judged by what it pays--in social advantage, or in hard money. I venture to say that no college worth the name was ever founded on that assumption.

Well: the assumption has won a great deal of ground. There are fewer students to-day who care for scholarship for its own sake than there were two generations ago. I do not say there are none. I say only that the boy who reads because he wishes to know has become something of an oddity among his fellows, whereas his grandfather's classmates would have thought him merely a good student. The college has not changed so much as the people who come to it.

And the trouble begins long before the college gate. It begins in the nursery school and the primary grade, where the methods now in favour ask a small child to do very little that is difficult and nothing that is dull. One can understand the kindly motive. Yet a child who has never been asked to work will not know how when the time comes. The same softness appears at the other end, in the practice of admitting students without examination [example needed: an institution or a figure showing how common this has become]. An entrance that tests nothing promises nothing. The standard is not raised by removing the bar.

Let us be plain about it. A school that will not ask for effort cannot give an education. It can only give a certificate.
