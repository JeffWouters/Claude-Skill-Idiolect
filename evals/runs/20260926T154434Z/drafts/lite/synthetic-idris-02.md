What would happen if every standing meeting had to make its case afresh from time to time? It may be that the simplest remedy for meetings that have outlived their purpose is not to abolish them but to require them, periodically, to justify their continuation.

Such a review need not be elaborate. It asks only that the people who attend a meeting consider whether they would create it today, knowing what they now know, and what they would lose if it stopped. Consider what it is like to raise the matter. You propose, at the end of a meeting that has run for as long as anyone can remember, that the group discuss whether it should continue. There is a pause. Someone wonders whether the suggestion is a criticism of whoever organises it; someone else, I suspect, is quietly relieved. The conversation is awkward, because it touches on habits people have stopped noticing and on a kind of ownership nobody quite admits to. Yet it is often in precisely this awkwardness that a team discovers what it actually wants from its time together, and whether the meeting provides it.

There is also a broader lesson here, since standing meetings are only one of the routines a team inherits. Most workplaces carry practices whose origins nobody recalls — [example needed: inherited routines in a real team] — and which persist largely because removing them would require a conversation that nobody has been asked to start.

It seems to me that the most useful way to approach such an inheritance is to ask, before deciding anything, what need the routine once met. Sometimes the need has gone, and the routine can go with it. Sometimes the need remains, and the routine is arguably still a reasonable way of meeting it. And sometimes the need remains but has quietly moved elsewhere, in which case the question becomes how to serve it better, rather than whether to keep the form it used to take.

Perhaps the point of reviewing a habit is not to end it but to remember why we have it.
