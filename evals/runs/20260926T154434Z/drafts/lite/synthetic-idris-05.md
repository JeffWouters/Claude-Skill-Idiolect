Why is it so difficult, in a room full of engineers, for anyone to say that they do not understand? The question is less trivial than it sounds. Most teams would claim to value learning, and most would say, if asked, that there is no such thing as a stupid question. Yet the moment in a design review when someone hesitates, decides that everyone else must already know, and says nothing, is familiar enough that you have probably lived it from both sides. It may be that the openness of a team's learning depends less on its stated values than on how safe it feels to be seen not knowing.

There is a practical cost to that silence. When one person admits confusion in public, they are rarely the only one confused; more commonly the admission gives several others permission to ask what they had been quietly wondering, and the group arrives at a shared understanding far sooner than it would through a series of private conversations. Confusion expressed openly becomes a kind of map of where the team's knowledge is thin. Confusion kept private simply persists, and each person pays for it separately.

There is also the matter of status. In many engineering organisations expertise is not only a capability but a currency, the thing that earns respect, influence and, in some sense, the right to be listened to. When knowing things is what makes you valuable, confessing that you do not know something feels like spending that currency, and the more senior the person, the higher the apparent price. Is it any wonder, then, that the people whose questions would most help others are frequently the least willing to ask them?

It seems to me that the remedy lies in a different understanding of what competence actually is. We tend to picture the competent engineer as someone who has accumulated answers; I suspect it is more accurate to picture someone who has remained curious, who continues to learn long after the point at which they could comfortably stop, and who treats the edge of their own knowledge as interesting rather than embarrassing. A team that holds this view can let its most experienced members say that they do not follow without any loss of standing, and in doing so they make it easier for everyone else to say the same.

Such openness is, in the end, one of the few habits in working life that costs almost nothing to adopt and yet changes a great deal. Perhaps the most expert people on a team are not those who never need to ask, but those who have understood that asking is how expertise is kept.
