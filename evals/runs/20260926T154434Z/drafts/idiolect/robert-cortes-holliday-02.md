There is no more reliable remedy for a sour temper than looking at rooms. I do not mean taking them; that is a different matter, and a serious one. I mean looking at them. You wake with a grievance against the universe, you read the advertisements over breakfast, and by noon you are climbing somebody's staircase with the agreeable, irresponsible feeling of a man who has committed himself to absolutely nothing. Abroad the remedy is even more effective, for unfamiliar staircases are better medicine than familiar ones.

The advertisements, naturally, are not to be believed. Every room is "bright," every neighbourhood "select," every landlady "refined"; a "sunny front room" faces a brick wall, and a "quiet house" stands next door to a livery stable. One learns to read these notices as one reads the testimonials of a patent medicine, with interest and without faith. In London there is the additional complication of the vocabulary, since the British terms for rooms are not the American ones, and a man who imagines he knows what he is asking for will discover, on the doorstep, that he has asked for something else. What, precisely, is a "set"? What are "chambers"? Well, you find out by climbing the stairs.

The old legal inns are the place for this sort of excursion. Their rooms are charming--panelled, low, crooked, overlooking a small courtyard and a solitary tree--and they are entirely without comforts. The water is somewhere below; the heating is a coal fire of your own construction; the light, in winter, is largely a matter of opinion. Nobody who wants a bath in his own quarters should apply there. But nobody who has seen them is perfectly content afterwards with an apartment.

The seeker, in short, should be modest in his expectations. If he wants luxury, he had better remain at an hotel. If he wants a room that has been inhabited for a long time, by people who were not in any particular hurry, he may perhaps find one, and he will pay for it in inconvenience. That is the fair price.
