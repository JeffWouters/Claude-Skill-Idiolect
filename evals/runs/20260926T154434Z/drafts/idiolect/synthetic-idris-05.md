What is it that we are really asking of an engineer when we expect them to know the answer? Most teams would describe themselves as places of learning, and most would mean it sincerely, yet the daily texture of engineering work tends to reward those who appear to understand rather than those who admit that they do not. It may be that the quickest way for a team to learn is also the most uncomfortable one for the people in it, which is to say aloud, in front of colleagues, that something makes no sense to them. This happens, I suspect, far less often than any of us would like to believe.

When someone does confess their confusion in a meeting or a shared channel, the question more often than not turns out to be one that several others were quietly holding, and the answer, once it is given, becomes part of what the whole team knows rather than something each person has had to reconstruct alone. The confession is not a private cost but a public contribution. The incentives of most engineering organisations, however, point in the opposite direction, because expertise tends to be treated less as a body of knowledge than as a form of status; to admit ignorance is, in some sense, to surrender a little of that status, and the more senior the person, the more there seems to be at stake. Is it any wonder that people learn to nod along?

There is also the question of what we mean by competence in the first place. You may have worked alongside engineers whose authority rested on always having the answer ready, and you might also have noticed that the answers aged considerably faster than the authority did. Real competence is arguably something different; it lies not in having finished learning but in continuing to learn, in remaining curious about the parts of the system one does not yet understand, and in being willing to be seen doing so by the people who look to you for guidance.

It seems to me that the most useful thing a team can do is to make this visible, so that confusion is treated as information about where understanding is thin, and so that those with the most standing are the first to say when they are lost. None of this requires an elaborate process; it requires only that someone with credibility goes first, and that the response they receive is gratitude rather than surprise. Perhaps the measure of an expert team, in the end, is not how much it already knows but how openly it admits what it has yet to learn.
