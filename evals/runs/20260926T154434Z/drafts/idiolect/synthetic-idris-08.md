What happens to a date once it has been announced? Before anyone says it aloud, an arbitrary due date is little more than a convenient point on a calendar, often chosen for reasons that have nothing to do with the work itself. It may be that the moment of announcement changes its nature entirely, because a date that others have heard becomes attached to the reputation of whoever named it, and people will defend their reputations far more stubbornly than they defend any plan.

This is why unrealistic dates are so rarely abandoned at the point when they ought to be, even by people who privately doubt them. The person who announced the date, and arguably everyone who repeated it, now has a stake in its survival; to let it slip is to admit, in some sense, that a public judgement was mistaken, and that the confidence with which it was delivered was never quite deserved. You might have watched a team work evenings and weekends to protect a date that, on reflection, mattered to almost nobody.

It seems to me that the most useful question to ask of any such date is what missing it would really cost. Sometimes the answer is considerable — a contractual obligation, a dependent team, a launch that cannot move — and the date deserves the effort spent defending it. More often, I suspect, the honest answer is that very little would happen at all, beyond a few awkward conversations and a revised line in a plan. Once that is clear, the case for renegotiating early becomes difficult to resist, since a date moved weeks in advance disappoints far fewer people than one missed on the day. The obstacle is rarely practical; it is the fear of letting others down, which is an entirely understandable reason for holding on, though not a particularly good one. Perhaps the date worth keeping is not the one we announced but the one we can still honestly stand behind.
