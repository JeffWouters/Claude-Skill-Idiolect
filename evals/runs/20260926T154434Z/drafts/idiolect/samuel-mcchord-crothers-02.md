I remember with a certain affection a new silver camp in Nevada which in those days considered itself a city and was prepared to argue the point. It had one street, and that street had to accommodate everything the community required for its material and spiritual welfare.

The smelter stood at the edge of town and poured out its smoke with a liberality that nobody thought of resenting. On the contrary, it was generally regarded as a public benefactor. The citizens held, with the confidence of men who had never consulted a physician on the subject, that the fumes were a powerful disinfectant, and that no germ could live in such an atmosphere. It is likely they were right, for nothing else could live in it either. There was not a blade of grass, a shrub or a geranium within reach of that smoke. The inhabitants accepted the loss of vegetation as the price of health, and considered the bargain an excellent one.

Along the street the institutions of civilization were arranged in their customary proportions. There were bars, and there were gaming halls, and between them, somewhat apologetically, stood a few feeble churches that seemed to have wandered in by mistake. There were also two newspapers, which were not content merely to report the news but made a point of supplying it, chiefly by denouncing each other. Their feud was the one enterprise in town that never showed signs of exhaustion.

A small train connected the camp with the outer world. It came and went with a leisurely air, as though it had no great confidence that anybody would wish to leave, and some doubt whether anybody ought to arrive.

But the true sovereign of the place was the dust. It rose with every wagon and settled on every surface; it seasoned the food, it thickened the whiskey, and it gave the sermons in the feeble churches a gritty quality they might otherwise have lacked. One did not live in the camp so much as under it. Yet I think of it kindly. It had no illusions about itself, except the illusion that it was going to last.
