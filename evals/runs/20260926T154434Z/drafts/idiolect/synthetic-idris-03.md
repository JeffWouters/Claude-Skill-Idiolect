Why does each new method of estimation arrive with such promise and leave so little changed? It may be that the method was never really the problem, and that what shapes our forecasts is less the technique we use than the attitude of the organisation towards uncertainty itself. A team can move from hours to points to some other unit of sizing, and for a while the novelty suggests that something has improved; underneath, however, the same expectations persist, and the same pressure to treat a guess as a promise quietly reasserts itself.

There is also the question of what an estimate is meant to be. If it is understood as a commitment, then revising it looks like failure, and people learn to pad their numbers or to defend them long after the evidence has moved on. If it is understood instead as a statement of what we currently know, then revising it is simply what happens as we learn more, and a changed estimate becomes a sign of attention rather than of error. The second view is, I suspect, the more honest one, though it asks a good deal of those who have to plan around the result.

It seems to me that the most useful step is to distinguish more carefully between work that is familiar and work that is genuinely new. Familiar work can arguably be forecast with some confidence, since the team has done something like it before and can draw on that experience; novel work cannot, and pretending otherwise merely hides the uncertainty inside a confident-looking figure. Treating the two separately allows the organisation to plan firmly where firmness is justified and loosely where it is not. Perhaps the aim of estimation is not to eliminate uncertainty but to be truthful about where it lies.
