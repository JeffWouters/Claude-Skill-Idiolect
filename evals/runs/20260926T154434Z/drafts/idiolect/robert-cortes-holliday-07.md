Of the manner in which a bookshop is actually conducted he possessed, I think, only the haziest conception.  It was not that he was indifferent to the subject; he was, on the contrary, unfailingly courteous and attentive whenever it arose.  But it seemed never to have occurred to him that an establishment of any consequence might employ more than one individual, or that the gentleman who had just found him a book was not also the gentleman who kept the accounts, wrapped the parcels, wrote the letters, ordered the stock, swept the floor and, for all one knows, owned the building.  In his mind, so far as one could make it out, there was the shop, and there was the salesman, and the salesman did everything.

One afternoon he announced that he wished to order some copies of his own works.  They were to be sent to a relative of his, a lady.  This was entirely agreeable, and the instructions were recorded with appropriate ceremony: so many copies of this title, so many of that, the bindings chosen, the whole matter settled.  He thanked us warmly, took up his hat, and went out into the street.

He had not left her name.  He had not left her address.  He had not so much as mentioned the city in which she resided.

When he next appeared, the omission was presented to him as delicately as possible.

"Your relative, sir.  Where are the books to go?"

"Why, to her, of course."

"Yes, sir.  And her name?"

He supplied it, after considerable reflection.  The street had to be drawn out of him separately, and then the number, and then the town, each particular arriving separately, like a bashful guest who must be coaxed into the drawing-room.  At every stage he apologised.  He was exceedingly sorry to occasion so much inconvenience.  He had supposed, it appeared, that we should somehow know, the salesman being, after all, the universal functionary who knew everything.  It was impossible to be exasperated with him.  One simply wrote it down.
