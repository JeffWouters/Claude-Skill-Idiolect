Why do software teams go on producing estimates that almost nobody involved really believes? The usual answer is that estimation is simply difficult, which is true, but it may be that the persistence of the practice has less to do with accuracy than with negotiation. An estimate is, in some sense, the opening position in a conversation between the business, which wants to know what it can promise, and the engineers, who want room to do the work properly; each side adjusts its number with the other in mind, and both understand, without quite saying so, that the figure is a bargaining token rather than a prediction.

There is nothing especially harmful in this, so long as everyone remembers what kind of number it is. The trouble begins when the estimate is written down, passed along and quietly converted into a commitment, at which point a guess made under uncertainty acquires the moral weight of a promise. You may have seen a team held to a date that no one on it ever thought realistic, simply because the date had been said aloud once and could not afterwards be withdrawn.

It seems to me that the more honest course is to acknowledge what most engineers already know, which is that forecasting unfamiliar work is inherently unreliable. We can estimate with some confidence the things we have done before; we cannot estimate the things we have not, because the very reason the work is unfamiliar is that we do not yet know what it contains. This is rarely said in planning meetings, arguably because saying it feels like an admission of incompetence. Perhaps the estimate we distrust is not a failure of engineering but a failure of candour.
