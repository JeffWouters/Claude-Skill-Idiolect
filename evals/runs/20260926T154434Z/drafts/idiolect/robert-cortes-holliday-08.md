There is no better way of discovering what strangers make of you than to go looking for a room.  Your friends have long since formed their opinion; they keep it politely to themselves.  A landlady has no such scruples.  She has a few minutes on the doorstep in which to decide whether you are honest, solvent, sober and quiet, and she forms her judgment with a candour which is, so to speak, bracing.

My own experiences in this line have been various and, now and then, decidedly peculiar.  One landlady, who turned out to be a practising doctor, was perfectly willing to let me the room, but explained that she would require to keep a certain quantity of her surgical equipment in it.  Where, precisely, it was to be kept, and what exactly it consisted of, I did not stay to learn.  Another, a woman of suspicious temperament, looked me over from hat to boots, inquired what I did for a living, and received my answer with an expression which made it clear that she did not believe a word of it.  Whatever my occupation might be, she evidently concluded, it was something shady.

Then there are the respectable houses.  These are the houses with clean steps and a card in the window; they want references.  They want, that is, a letter from somebody who will vouch for your character, your punctuality and your habits, and who is himself a person whom the landlady would consider respectable in turn.  Where the chain of references is supposed to end I have never discovered.

And there are, finally, the fashionable districts of London.  Here the question is not your character at all; that is beside the point.  Nobody inquires whether you drink, or sing, or keep surgical instruments.  They inquire only, and in the most civil manner imaginable, whether you are rich.
