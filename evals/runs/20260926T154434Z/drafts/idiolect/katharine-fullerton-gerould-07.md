The colleges are being attacked, at the moment, from two directions at once, and the curious thing is that the attackers do not appear to notice that they contradict each other. One party declares the college useless; the other complains that it is too difficult. Both, when you look into the matter, mean precisely the same thing. They mean that a degree does not pay quickly enough--in position, in money, in the respect of the neighbours--to be worth the trouble it costs. I do not know that any one has put it quite so plainly. But that is what the complaint amounts to, and the college is being judged by a standard it was never constructed to meet.

Let us consider the students themselves. Fewer of them, I fancy, care for scholarship for its own sake than cared for it two generations ago. I do not say none do; there are always a few. Yet the few were once a respectable company, and they are now something very like a remnant. The rest have come for whatever the degree will fetch in the market; and if it fetches less than they expected, they are not disappointed but indignant, as though they had been defrauded. You may say they have a right to be. Granted: yet a man who buys a violin in order to sell it again has no business to complain that it is difficult to play.

Nor does the trouble begin at the college gate. It begins in the nursery. The methods now fashionable for very young children [example needed: a named method or practice] require almost nothing of the child except that he be entertained; and a child who has never been asked for anything else will not easily learn to give it. Then, when he is older, he is admitted to the next school without an examination, and to the next after that. Nobody has tested him. Nobody, therefore, has failed him. The point is that a standard which is never applied is not a standard at all; it is merely an aspiration. We have aspired generously. We have not, I think, insisted on much. And the colleges, accepting whatever the schools send up to them, can hardly be blamed for beginning to resemble the schools.
