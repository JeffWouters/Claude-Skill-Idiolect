Where does a project deadline actually originate, if you trace it back through the plans that produced it? It may be that the answer is more often "from us" than we would care to admit. We tend to speak of due dates as though they were imposed from outside, by a customer or a regulator or some immovable event in the calendar, and some of them certainly are; a great many, however, turn out on inspection to have been chosen internally, by someone who required a date for a schedule and selected one that seemed reasonable. Once written down, such a date acquires an authority that its origins hardly justify, and the team begins to organise its work around it as though it were a fact about the world.

There is also the matter of what an approaching cutoff does to the people working towards it. You may have noticed that a deadline a few days away concentrates attention in a way that no amount of careful planning manages, and that decisions which had lingered indefinitely are suddenly settled in an afternoon. That sharpening of concentration is arguably the principal reason deadlines exist at all. The same pressure, though, invites shortcuts that would never otherwise be accepted, and it encourages a kind of overwork that is tolerated because it is supposed to be temporary. Who among us has not watched a temporary push quietly become the normal pace?

What strikes me most, in the end, is that nobody explicitly weighs these effects against one another. The benefits of concentration and the costs of haste are both genuine, and I suspect that in many cases the balance would favour the date if anyone paused to consider it; the difficulty is that the date is usually set before the question is asked, and by someone who will not bear the cost of the answer. Perhaps the most useful thing a team can do with a deadline is not to meet it or to miss it but to ask, before accepting it, who chose it and why.
