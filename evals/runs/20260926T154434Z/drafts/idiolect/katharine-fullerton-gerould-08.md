I have known a good many people who prided themselves on invariably telling the truth, and I have liked remarkably few of them. That is not, I hope, because I am particularly fond of falsehood; it is because their truth was so seldom told for anybody's benefit but their own. The person who informs you exactly what he thinks of your new house, or your new book, or your new hat, is not serving truth in any disinterested fashion; he is enjoying himself, and enjoying himself at your expense. There is a genuine pleasure in bluntness--it is almost a sensual pleasure--and like most pleasures it has eventually to be paid for. He pays for it, as a rule, in friendships. One by one his acquaintances discover engagements elsewhere, and he calls it the price of honesty. I call it the price of self-indulgence.

Well: let us be fair to him. Some one will object that a lie is a lie, and that the small deception opens the door to the considerable one. Granted; but the objection proves altogether too much. When an acquaintance inquires how you are, and you answer that you are very well, thank you, you have quite possibly told an untruth, since you may have a headache or may have passed a wretched night. Does anybody seriously suppose that he wished to hear about it? He was not requesting information; he was offering a courtesy, and you returned it in the same currency. Nobody was deceived, because nobody expected to be told anything whatever. The point is that a statement which nobody takes as a statement cannot reasonably be called a lie in any sense that matters. It is a form of greeting, like a bow.

It is instructive, I fancy, to observe what has happened to the ancient quarrel over cosmetics. Formerly the objection to rouge and powder was a moral one: the painted face was the mark of a questionable woman, and there was an end of it. That objection has worn exceedingly thin, and hardly any one troubles to make it now. But the quarrel did not die; it merely shifted its ground. We are told now, not that paint is wicked, but that it is deceitful--that the woman who uses it is pretending to a complexion she does not possess. Heaven forbid that I should defend every pot of rouge in the country. Yet I notice that the accusation has travelled from sin to fraud, and that the very people who bring it will assure their hostess, without a blush of any description, that they have had a perfectly charming evening. They're entirely right to tell her so. They are only mistaken in supposing the rouge is worse.
