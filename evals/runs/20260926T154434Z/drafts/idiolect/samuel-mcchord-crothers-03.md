He left the town a little after midnight, by the back road, with his conscience under examination. The night was dark and the road was rough, which suited him, for he had no wish to be recognized, and a man who is walking fast in the dark has an excellent opportunity for reflection.

The question he put to himself was a plain one. Had he done wrong? He had preached without ordination, prayed without conviction, and accepted the hospitality of people who believed him to be something he was not. On the face of it the case looked bad. But he was not a man to be satisfied with the face of anything, and he determined to go into the particulars.

He began by taking an inventory of his sins, as an honest merchant takes an inventory of his stock. He went over the shelves carefully and found them surprisingly bare. The sermons, when he examined them, were sound enough; he had borrowed them from better men, and they had done no harm in the borrowing. The prayers had been received with every appearance of satisfaction. Nobody, so far as he could discover, had been made worse by his ministry, and several had been made quite comfortable.

There remained, it is true, one item that would not be explained away. Years before, he had stolen a quantity of honey from a farmer. Here, at last, was genuine guilt, and he acknowledged it frankly, with something like relief. It is a great comfort to a man in his position to find one sin he can repent of without reservation, since it lends a certain credibility to his acquittal on all the other counts.

As for the word fraud, he rejected it altogether. A fraud, he reasoned, is a man who deceives others for profit, and he had never made a profit in his life. He had asked for nothing beyond his board and a bed. He had sought subsistence, not riches. Was it a crime to wish to eat? He thought not.

By the time the sky began to lighten he had reached a verdict, and it was a merciful one. He was guilty of the honey. On everything else the court found for the defendant.
