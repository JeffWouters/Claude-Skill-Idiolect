The rogues of fiction are never free. However gaily they cheat and steal through their adventures, there is always an author standing behind them, and the author, being a respectable person with a reputation to consider, sees to it that judgment overtakes them in the last chapter. We are allowed to enjoy their wickedness only on condition that we see it punished. The novelist is judge as well as biographer, and he cannot help it.

But the swindler who writes his own memoir is under no such constraint. In the recollections of one of these early American adventurers, [name needed], what astonishes the reader is not the length of the offences but the completeness of the self-approval. He was a soldier, and then a preacher. He forged money, and then he taught school. He spent a term in prison, which he treats as an enforced interval between engagements. Through all these changes of occupation he never once sat in judgment upon himself, for it did not occur to him that there was any case to try.

What is most remarkable is his cheerfulness. The ordinary sinner, when he is found out, becomes gloomy and apologetic and anxious to explain. This man was never gloomy. He had a conscience, and he consulted it regularly, and it always gave him an excellent character. Public Opinion might say what she liked; she was not, in his view, a competent authority. He had a higher court of appeal, and that court was always in session and always sat in his favour.

I would not hold him up as a model. Yet there is something wholesome in the spectacle of a man so entirely at peace with himself, when so many honest people spend their lives in anxious self-examination without ever being able to arrive at a verdict. He arrived at his verdict early. It was "Not guilty," and he never saw any reason to reopen the case.
