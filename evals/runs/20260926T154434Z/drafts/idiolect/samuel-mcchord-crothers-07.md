There is an old story in Scripture of a king who came to the throne and was asked by his people to lighten their burdens. He took counsel first with the old men who had served his father, and they advised him to speak gently. He took counsel next with the young men who had grown up with him, and they advised him to speak roughly. He followed the young men. The kingdom was divided, and the elders have used the story as a text ever since.

But the elders perhaps seldom read the story to the end of its meaning. It is not only that the young are rash. It is that every generation, at intervals that recur with a certain regularity, grows weary of the wisdom it has inherited and longs to prove itself in some more strenuous fashion than its fathers did. The nation that has enjoyed a long peace is precisely the nation in which this restlessness appears.

This is why the most reasonable plans for peace so often come to nothing. They are drawn up by sober persons who assume that everybody wants what is sensible. The plans are sound enough. Yet if those who hold the power of the nation are in a mood for conflict, the soundness of a plan is no recommendation to them; it is an argument against it. You cannot persuade a man out of an appetite by showing him that it is unreasonable.

This temper is not confined to barbarous ages. Kipling to-day speaks for young men who have come to despise a mercenary peace, a peace of shopkeepers who count their profits and call the total civilisation. The poet sings what the young feel. The old men listen, and shake their heads, and are not consulted.
