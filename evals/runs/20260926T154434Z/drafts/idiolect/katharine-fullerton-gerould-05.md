We have decided, it would seem, that every American child is to have a degree; and since not every child can earn one, the degree must be made easy enough to fit. That is the whole secret of the matter. Our schools and colleges hand out their credentials, to-day, for something that no honest person would call study. I do not say the certificates are forged. I say only, and I say it with regret, that they certify very little. The boy who has sat through his years of lectures (and not always through the lectures!) comes out with the same parchment as the boy who has worked. Well: the parchment is what was wanted, and the parchment is what he has.

Some one will object, of course, that this is a democratic triumph. Granted; but it is a triumph of the wrong kind. The rich parent does not wish his son to be worked hard--it is somehow not quite the thing--and the poor parent, very naturally, will not see his son denied the prestige the rich boy carries off without effort. Between them they have got what they asked for: not an education but the label of one. And the schools, on the whole, have not resisted. They have turned, I fancy, with a certain relief from the training of the mind to the teaching of crafts and practical subjects, which can be graded without anguish and failed by nobody. One can make a very pretty bookshelf without ever having learned to think; and it's the bookshelf, not the thinking, that the parents come to admire. The point is that the intellect is not trained by accident. It has to be drilled, patiently, and drilling is unpleasant; and we have made unpleasantness the one thing a school may not ask of a child. I do not, mind you, blame the teachers alone. We have all wanted the prize without the race. We shall get exactly that.
