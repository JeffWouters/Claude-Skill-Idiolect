When a prospector strikes rich ore, the whole camp feels that it has been let into the secret of Providence. It was so with [name needed], who came upon a vein that made him a man of consequence overnight. He did what a man of consequence ought to do in a mining town. He got married, and he invited everybody.

I do not mean that he invited his friends. That would have been a narrow and exclusive proceeding, unworthy of the occasion. He invited the town, and to make sure that the town could come, he hired every carriage in it. The ceremony was to be held at his cabin, which lay far out from the settlement, and the road to it had not been built with processions in mind.

It was a remarkable procession. The officials of the county rode side by side with the gamblers, and the clergy found themselves sharing a seat with miners who were perhaps more at home in a saloon. Nobody minded. For one day Respectability and Disreputability agreed to travel together, and each found the other better company than rumour had led it to expect. The carriages jolted over the ruts, and the company, which had never before been assembled in one place, rather enjoyed the novelty of it.

But the true meaning of the day was not in the wedding. It was in the town's sense of justice. A mining settlement has no aristocracy, and it cannot afford one; it has instead a rotation of celebrity. Each resident in turn is lifted up and made famous, for a week or a day, and then set down again so that his neighbour may have his share. The prospector's turn had come. He enjoyed it thoroughly, and so did everyone else, knowing that their own would come in due course.
