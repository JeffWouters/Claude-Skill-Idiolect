Why do so many teams still gather every week for a meeting that nobody can quite explain? It may be that the answer lies less in any decision than in the absence of one, since most standing meetings begin with a clear purpose and simply continue after that purpose has faded. A project needed coordinating, or a problem needed watching, and a weekly slot seemed the sensible way to handle it; the project ended or the problem settled, but the slot remained in the calendar, and the habit of attending outlived any memory of why it had been created. You may have sat in such a meeting yourself, dutifully working through an agenda that no longer corresponds to anything the team is actually doing.

There is also a temptation, once this drift has been noticed, to sweep such meetings away wholesale and reclaim the hours they consume. The impulse is understandable, and arguably it is sometimes justified, but I suspect it carries a risk that is easy to overlook. Standing meetings frequently do something that appears on no agenda: they supply a regular point of contact, a predictable occasion on which colleagues see one another and exchange the small observations that would otherwise go unsaid. A team that loses this may not notice the loss for some time; it may simply find, months later, that people know rather less about the work of their colleagues than they used to, and that the bonds which made collaboration easy have quietly weakened.

The meeting that has outlived its reason, in the end, may still be serving a purpose that nobody thought to name when it was first set up. That does not mean it should continue unexamined, since a meeting kept alive only by inertia is a poor way of meeting a need that could be met more deliberately. Perhaps the question worth asking is not whether a meeting still does what it was designed to do but whether it does something the team would miss.
