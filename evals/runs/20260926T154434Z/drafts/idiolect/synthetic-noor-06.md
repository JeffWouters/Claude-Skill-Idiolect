I darn my own socks. Badly, at first. I taught myself, and it showed.

The tool came from my grandfather. An old darning tool, worn smooth. Nobody showed me how. I worked it out from [example needed] and a lot of wasted wool.

Here is how it goes now. First the sock goes over the tool, hole on top. That stretches the wool flat. Not tight. Just flat. Then I run a line of stitches round the hole, well into the sound wool. That is the frame.

Then the warp. Long stitches straight across the hole, side to side, close together. Each one catches the good wool on both edges. Then the weft. I turn the sock round and weave across the first rows. Over one, under one. Back the other way. Under one, over one. It is slow. [number needed] rows for a hole the size of [example needed].

Tension is the whole job. Pull too hard and the patch puckers. It turns into a hard little knot of wool. You do not notice it on the tool. You notice it in your boot. A day on a patch like that and your foot is sore. [example needed] So now I leave each row a little slack. Looser than you think. The patch should lie flat and give a bit when you press it.

I used to think of this as time lost. An hour on a sock worth less than an hour of anything. I do not think that now. It is quiet work. Hands busy, head empty. I would call it restful.

And the heel is better for it. The patch is thicker than the wool round it. It lasts longer than the rest of the sock. [example needed]
