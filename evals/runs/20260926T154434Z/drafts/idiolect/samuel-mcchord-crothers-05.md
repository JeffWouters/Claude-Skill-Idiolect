In every mining settlement of the West there is one figure whom the whole community holds in reverence, and it is not the mayor. It is the Prospector. He comes into town at long intervals, with his pack and his burro and his accumulated opinions, and the town receives him as older cities receive a returning ambassador. The bar makes room for him and the papers quote him. Even the politicians, who flatter everybody as a matter of routine, flatter him with a peculiar sincerity. They have discovered that he is the recognised voice of Good Sense.

How he came by this office is not easy to explain. He has no fixed abode and no visible means of support beyond a pick and a hope. He is a restless wanderer who never stays long enough in one place to be found out. But it was the restless wanderers who built the West. The settled citizen came afterward, to put a fence around what the Prospector had discovered and then forgotten.

Good Sense, in these parts, is not the cautious creature she is in the East. She does not keep a bank account; she keeps a grubstake. She is not in the least disturbed when a town that was booming in the spring has become a row of empty shanties by the autumn. I should not like to call her reckless. I should rather say that she has taken the long view, and that the long view in a mining country, where fortunes are made and unmade between one snowfall and the next, is a good deal longer than elsewhere.

The veteran prospectors have watched so many towns rise and vanish that they no longer reckon time by the calendar. Ask one of them when a thing happened, and he will not tell you the year. He will tell you it was the season after the rush to [place needed], or just before the rush to [place needed]. His life is dated by rushes, as the chronicles of ancient nations are dated by the reigns of kings. The kings are forgotten. The rushes are remembered, perhaps because they were so much more entertaining.
