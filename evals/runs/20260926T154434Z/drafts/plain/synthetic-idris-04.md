Ask a team where their deadline came from and the answer is often vaguer than you would expect. Some dates are genuinely imposed from outside: a regulatory change, a contract clause, a trade show, a season. Many are not. They were chosen internally, by someone, at some point, for reasons that made sense in a planning meeting and were never written down. Over time the origin fades and the date takes on the weight of a fixed fact.

That matters, because a date has real effects on the people working towards it. The early effect is often good. An approaching cutoff concentrates attention. Side projects get parked, decisions that had been circling for weeks get made, and the team finds a focus that is hard to create any other way. A date gives a group something shared to aim at, and that can be energising.

The later effects are less comfortable. As the cutoff gets closer, the pressure shifts from focus to compromise. Tests get skipped, reviews get lighter, known problems get logged for later. People work late and at weekends to close the gap. [example needed] Some of those shortcuts are harmless and some become the next quarter's incidents. The overwork has costs too, in tiredness, in mistakes, and in how people feel about the next deadline.

What is striking is that nobody explicitly weighs these two sides against each other. The person who picked the date was usually thinking about the benefit, about getting something out, creating momentum, or lining up with another plan. They were rarely thinking about the shortcuts and the late nights that the date would produce further down the line. And the team, by the time it feels those costs, tends to treat the date as immovable, because it no longer remembers that it was a choice.

A useful habit is simply to ask, when a date is set, where it comes from and what happens if it moves. If the answer is that nothing external depends on it, the date is a tool, and the team can decide how hard to lean on it.
