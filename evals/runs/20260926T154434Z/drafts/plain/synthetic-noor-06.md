There is a particular kind of hole that appears in woollen socks, usually at the heel, and for a long time my answer to it was to throw the sock away. Now I darn it. I weave a small patch across the gap, thread over and under, until the hole is closed with new fabric of its own.

I taught myself how to do it. Nobody sat me down and showed me, although the tool I use came from my grandfather. It is an old darning tool [description of the tool needed], worn smooth from use, and holding it gives the work a sense of continuity I did not expect. I do not know exactly how he used it. I only know that it now does the same job in my hands.

The most important lesson I learned, mostly by getting it wrong, is tension. When you weave the patch, it is tempting to pull every thread tight so the repair looks neat and firm. That is a mistake. An overtight patch puckers the fabric around it and forms a hard ridge, and a hard ridge under your heel will make your foot sore by the end of the day. The patch needs to sit as flat and as soft as the knitting around it, stretching with the sock rather than fighting it. Loose enough to give, firm enough to hold: that balance is most of the craft.

A repair done this way does more than close the hole. It reinforces the heel, which is where the wear comes from in the first place, so the mended sock often outlasts the ones that were never damaged.

What surprised me most is how the time feels. I expected darning to feel like a chore, time spent on something I could simply replace. Instead it feels restful. You sit, you work slowly, row after row, and there is nothing else to think about. It is not time wasted. It is time spent making something last, with a tool that has already lasted a long while.
