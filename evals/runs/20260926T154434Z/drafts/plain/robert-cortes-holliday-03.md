Anyone who works behind the counter of a bookshop soon learns that the customers are more entertaining than most of the stock. They do not mean to be. That is what makes them so reliable.

The first type announces itself at the door. "Oh, I just love books," the visitor says, and then walks the length of the shop without looking at a single one. The declaration is the whole of the visit. Books, for these people, are something like sunsets: it is agreeable to say one admires them, and nobody expects a closer inspection. They leave, as a rule, without buying anything, having loved the books thoroughly from a distance.

The second type is so common that the staff could keep a tally. Sooner or later nearly every customer looks along the crowded shelves, pauses, and quotes the old biblical line that of making many books there is no end. They say it as though it had just occurred to them. They say it with a small smile, waiting for the clerk to share the joke. The clerk smiles back, because it is his job, and because he has heard it three times already that morning. I never met a customer who did not seem pleased with himself for thinking of it.

Then there are the friends of authors. One might suppose that a writer's friends would be his most dependable buyers. The opposite is true. Show such a person a new book by the friend in question and he will refuse it at once, often with a kind of pride. He knows the author, you see; he has no need to read him. Presumably he expects a copy as a gift, and presumably he is often disappointed.

Customers also have a gift for muddling the terms of the trade. They ask for one thing when they mean another, confuse an edition with a printing, or describe a book by its colour and expect the clerk to supply the rest [example needed: a specific muddled request]. The clerk learns to translate without comment.

Finally there are the visitors from abroad, certain of whom arrive with requests so specialised that nobody in the shop has heard of the subject, let alone the book [example needed: which customers and which titles]. They name some obscure technical work with complete confidence, as though it were a popular novel, and seem genuinely surprised to find it is not on the front table.

None of this is a complaint. It is simply the view from behind the counter, and it is a very good view.
