The dripping tap had been going for a while before I finally dealt with it, and the fix itself turned out to be a small one: a worn rubber seal that no longer closed the flow properly. Swapping it was the easy part. Everything around it took longer.

I bought the replacement seals from a local hardware shop. They were cheap, which is part of why a dripping tap is so annoying to put off. The part that solves the problem costs very little [price needed]. I bought more than one, on purpose, and I will come back to why.

Most of the job was preparation. Shutting off the supply, clearing the space under the sink, laying out the tools, putting a cloth in the basin so nothing small disappears down the drain, and working out how the tap actually comes apart before taking it apart. The repair itself, pulling out the old seal and fitting the new one, took a fraction of that time. I have come to accept that this is normal. With small repairs, preparation is the job and the fix is the last few minutes of it.

Reassembly deserves care. Each part goes back in the order it came out, seated properly, tightened firmly but not forced. It is easy to rush this bit because the problem feels solved, but a part put back crooked will leak just as surely as a worn seal.

Then the supply goes back on, and it goes back on slowly. Opening the valve all at once sends a rush of water and pressure through a tap you have just rebuilt, and if anything is not quite seated, that is when you find out. Turning it gradually gives you time to watch, listen and stop if needed. In my case the tap held, and the drip was gone.

The spare seals are now stored away for later. Next time a tap starts to drip, the part will already be in the drawer, and the preparation will be a little shorter.
