When the prospector struck rich ore, he did what a lucky man in a mining town was expected to do. He decided to get married, and he decided that the whole town should come.

He did not send out a few careful invitations. He invited everyone. Then, so that nobody would have an excuse to stay home, he hired every carriage in the place. Every buggy, wagon and hack that could be pulled by a horse was engaged for the day, and the town set out together for his remote cabin in the hills. [example needed]

It was a strange procession. The officials of the town rode in it, dressed for the occasion and conscious of their dignity. The gamblers rode in it too, in their best waistcoats, perhaps sharing a seat with the very men who had tried to close their tables. The clergy were there, along with the miners who made up most of the population and who had never before been invited anywhere in a hired carriage. For a few hours, the distinctions that mattered so much in town counted for very little on the road. Everyone was a guest, and everyone was going the same way.

What made the day possible was not only the prospector's generosity but the character of the town itself. A mining settlement had its own way of handing out fame. It took turns. One week the talk was all about a man who had found a good claim, the next about someone who had lost everything at cards, the next about a quarrel or a fire or a marriage. Each resident, sooner or later, had his moment at the centre of attention, and then the town moved on to someone else.

The prospector's wedding was his turn. He had the whole town at his door, in every carriage it owned, and for that day he was the most famous man in the district. By the next week, the town would have found someone new.
