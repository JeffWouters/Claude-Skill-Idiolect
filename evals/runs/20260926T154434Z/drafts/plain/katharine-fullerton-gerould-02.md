Most of the time, telling the truth costs us very little. We say what we think happened, what we believe, what we intend, and we move on. It is the easy course, and it is also the sound one, because every relationship worth having rests on the confidence that the other person means what they say. Friendship, trade and family life would all come apart quickly if we could not rely on each other's word. So the ordinary rule is plain enough: speak truly.

The difficulty comes when the truth is not ours alone. A friend tells us something in confidence, and later someone asks us directly about it. If we are the sort of person who simply cannot bring ourselves to say anything false, we are in a poor position to keep that confidence. Silence can help for a while, and so can turning the question aside, but a determined questioner will often read a refusal to answer as an answer. The person who can never lie turns out to be an unsafe keeper of other people's secrets, however honest they are about their own.

This does not mean that lying on a friend's behalf is generally allowed. Most of the time a careful silence, or a plain statement that the matter is not ours to discuss, does the work well enough, and we should reach for those first. But there are rare cases where nothing else will serve. The familiar example is the hunted man: someone fleeing for his life hides in our house, and his pursuers come to the door and ask whether we have seen him. To tell them the truth would be to hand him over. Here almost everyone feels that the lie is not merely permitted but right.

What marks such cases is that the lie is told for another person's protection, not our own advantage, and that the harm the truth would do is grave and immediate. Outside those narrow limits, the easy rule still holds.
