There is a kind of person who prides himself on always telling the truth, and who means by this that he never softens anything. He tells a friend that her new dress is unbecoming, a colleague that his speech was tedious, an acquaintance that he looks ill. He calls this honesty. I think it is more often a fault, and not a small one.

Look closely at the blunt truth-teller and you will usually find that he enjoys himself. The pleasure of saying exactly what one thinks is a real pleasure, and it is a selfish one. The speaker gets the satisfaction; the listener gets the wound. We rarely hear the plain-spoken man deliver an unwelcome truth with any sign of reluctance. He delivers it with relish, and then wonders why his friends grow fewer each year. Friendships are not built on a steady diet of candour. They are built on a thousand small acts of consideration, many of which involve leaving something true unsaid, or saying something kinder than the whole truth.

Consider the most routine courtesy of all. Someone asks how you are, and you say you are fine. Perhaps you have a headache, perhaps you slept badly, perhaps you are worried about something you would rather not discuss. The answer is, strictly speaking, untrue. But no one is deceived in any way that matters. The question was a greeting, not an inquiry, and the answer is a greeting in return. To reply with a full account of one's condition would not be more honest in any useful sense. It would merely be tiresome, and a little inconsiderate of the person who asked.

These harmless untruths are the ordinary grease of social life. They let people pass one another without friction. A society in which everyone insisted on literal accuracy at every moment would not be a more virtuous society. It would be a more quarrelsome one, and a lonelier one.

It is instructive to see how the argument over cosmetics has shifted. There was a time when objections to paint and powder were moral in character: the painted face was taken as a sign of vanity or of worse. That objection has largely faded. What remains is a different complaint, that cosmetics are a form of deceit, because they present a face that is not quite the real one. [example needed] But if that is the charge, it proves too much. The same could be said of a well-cut coat, a pleasant tone of voice, or a smile offered to a stranger. All of them present us a little better than we are.

The truth deserves respect, and there are moments when nothing but the plain truth will do. But everyday social life is not one of those moments, and the person who treats it as if it were is not more honest than the rest of us. He is only less kind.
