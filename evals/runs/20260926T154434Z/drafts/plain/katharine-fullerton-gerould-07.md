Our colleges are under attack from two directions at once, and the curious thing is that the attacks contradict each other. One set of critics complains that college is useless: that it teaches nothing a young man or woman will need in the world of business. Another set complains that college is too hard: that it demands more study than a reasonable student should be asked to give. Both judgments rest on the same assumption, which is that a college is to be measured by its social or financial payoff. If it does not lead to a better position or a larger income, it has failed; if it makes the road to those things steep, it has failed again.

What has quietly disappeared from this debate is the idea that learning might be worth having for its own sake. Fewer students today value scholarship for itself than did two generations earlier. The student who reads because the subject has seized him, who works at a difficult problem for the pleasure of mastering it, has become rarer. In his place stands the student who asks, of every course, what it will be worth to him afterwards. A college full of such students will soon be pressed to give them what they ask for, and what they ask for is rarely what is best.

The weakness does not begin at college. It begins much earlier, in the schooling of young children. The methods now favoured in the early years are designed, above all, to make learning painless, and in removing the pain they have too often removed the learning. [example needed] Children who have never been asked to struggle with anything do not arrive at higher schools ready to struggle.

Nor are they tested when they arrive. Admission without examination, adopted in the name of fairness, means that a school cannot know what its new students have learned, and so cannot hold them to anything. Standards that are never checked soon cease to be standards at all.

If we want colleges worth defending, we must first want students who value what colleges are for.
