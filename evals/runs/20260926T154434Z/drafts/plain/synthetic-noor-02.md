Our household runs on a single sheet of paper stuck to the fridge. It is not clever, and that is the point. Everything else we tried was cleverer, and none of it lasted.

The sheet has three sections. The first is groceries: whatever runs out gets written down, and it is there for whoever does the shopping. The second is quick tasks, the small jobs that take minutes but get forgotten if nobody writes them down. The third is large projects, the things that need a weekend, a plan or a budget. Keeping them apart matters. A list that puts a lightbulb next to repainting a room makes both look equally urgent, and neither gets done.

Each one broke in its own way, and each failure taught us something about what the paper list had to get right. It had to be visible to everyone, in the place everyone passes several times a day. It had to be easy to add to without unlocking a phone or finding the right page. And it had to keep a record.

That last part is why nothing gets erased. When something is done, it gets struck through, not wiped away. A crossed-out line still shows what was there, who dealt with it and how much has been handled since the sheet went up. By the end, a full sheet is a small picture of a busy stretch of family life, and there is a quiet satisfaction in seeing how much of it has a line through it.

Then it comes down, and a fresh sheet goes up.
