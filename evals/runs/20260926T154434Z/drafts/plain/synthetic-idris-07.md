Ask most software teams whether they trust their own estimates and you'll get a knowing laugh. Yet they keep producing them, sprint after sprint. The reason isn't that engineers are bad at arithmetic. It's that estimates are doing a different job from the one they claim to do.

In practice, an estimate is often a negotiation. The business wants to know when something will be ready and how much it will cost; the engineers want enough room to do the work properly. The number that ends up on the plan is less a forecast than a settlement between those two pressures. Everyone involved half-knows this, which is exactly why nobody quite believes the result.

That arrangement can work tolerably well as long as the number stays what it is: a rough, shared guess. The trouble starts when it turns into a binding commitment. Once a figure appears in a roadmap or a customer conversation, it stops being an estimate and becomes a promise, and every later discovery that the work is bigger than expected is treated as a failure rather than as new information.

Underneath all of this sits an uncomfortable fact. Forecasting unfamiliar work is inherently unreliable. If a team has never built something quite like this before, it cannot know in advance where the hard parts are. Much of software development is exactly that kind of work. Yet this limitation is rarely acknowledged out loud, because saying it feels like refusing to cooperate.

The honest move is to name what an estimate is for, keep it separate from commitments, and say plainly how uncertain it is.
