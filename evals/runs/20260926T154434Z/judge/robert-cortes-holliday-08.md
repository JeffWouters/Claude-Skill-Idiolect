## The passage (by the author)

Well, I told her, by way of making talk, that I believed I was held to be a reasonably honest person, and that I was frequently sober.

"Oh," she said, "I can see that you are a gentleman—in your way," she added, in a murmur.

So, you see, in hunting lodgings you not only see how others live, but how you seem to others.

It is certainly curious, the places in which to dwell which one is shown in hunting lodgings. Once I was given to view a room in which was a strange table-like affair constructed of metal. "You wouldn't mind, I suppose," said the lady of the lodging, "if this remained in the room?"

"Oh, not at all," I replied. "But what is it?"

"Why, it's an operating table," she explained. "Of course, you know," she added, "that I'm a physician. And," she continued, "of course I should want to make use of it now and then, but not regularly, not every day."

To a lady with a patch over her eye with lodgings to let in Broome Street I one time stated, by way of being communicative, that I was often in my room a good deal doing some work there. Ah! With many ogles and grimaces, she whispered hoarsely, with an effort at a sly effect, that "that was all right here. She understood," she said. Perfectly "safe place for that," it was. "The gentlemen who had the room before were something of the same kind."

As you know, "references" frequently are demanded of one hunting lodgings. To get into a really nice place one must really be a very nice person. "You know, I have a daughter," sighs the really nice landlady.

To obtain lodgings in Kensington one must be very well-to-do, particularly if one would be on the "drawing room floor." "I like these rooms very much," I said to a prim person there, and I hesitated.

## Draft A

Looking for a room to rent is one of the quickest ways to find out how strangers see you. You arrive at a door, a person you have never met looks you up and down, and within a minute or two they have decided what sort of tenant you are going to be. You do not get to explain yourself first. The judgement comes before the conversation, and the conversation is mostly spent trying to work out what the judgement was.

Some of the experiences are simply odd. One landlady turned out to be a doctor, and she was perfectly happy to let the room, with one condition: she wanted to keep her surgical equipment in it. The instruments would stay, and I would live alongside them. She seemed to think this was an entirely reasonable arrangement and could not quite see why anyone would hesitate. Another landlady took one look at me, asked what I did for a living, and plainly concluded from the answer that my work was something shady. She did not say so directly, but the questions that followed made it obvious she was wondering what exactly I would be doing in her house, and at what hours.

Then there are the respectable houses, which have their own way of sorting people. They want references. They want to know who can vouch for you, how long you have been known to them, and whether the people vouching are themselves the right sort. A stranger without letters in hand does not get very far past the front step, however polite he is.

And beyond the respectable houses are the fashionable districts of London, where the question is not character at all but money. There, nobody asks what you do or who can speak for you. The rent itself does the asking, and it only accepts one answer. If you are not wealthy, the matter is settled before you have rung the bell.

Put together, the search teaches you something you might not have wanted to learn: that to a great many people you are, at first sight, a set of guesses about your habits and your income.

## Draft B

If you wish to learn what the world makes of you, go and look for a room. Your friends will not tell you; they are too kind, or too used to you. But the stranger who opens the door to you, and looks you up and down on the step before she lets you in, will tell you plainly enough, though not always in words.

I have had some curious experiences in this way. One landlady, who was a doctor, showed me a room that seemed very suitable in every respect but one. She wished, it turned out, to keep her surgical equipment in it. Not in a cupboard, not in the room next door, but in the room itself, where I was to sleep. I was to be the tenant, so to say, of the instruments. I have wondered since what she took me for. A man of steady nerve, perhaps. Or simply a man who would not be in much.

Another landlady had quite made up her mind about me before I had got my hat off. She asked what I did. I told her. She did not believe a word of it. Her face said, as clearly as a face can say anything, that whatever my work might be it was certainly not honest, and very probably not legal, and she would thank me not to bring it into her house. I did not take the room. I was not, I think, offered it.

Now, the respectable houses are another matter. The respectable house wants references. It wants to know who you are, and who will say so, and whether the persons who say so are themselves respectable, and so on back, I suppose, to the beginning of things. And the fashionable districts of London want something simpler still. They want money; a great deal of it.

One goes home, after a day of this, with a clearer idea of oneself than one had in the morning. It is not always a flattering idea.

## Draft C

There is no better way of discovering what strangers make of you than to go looking for a room. Your friends have long since formed their opinion; they keep it politely to themselves. A landlady has no such scruples. She has a few minutes on the doorstep in which to decide whether you are honest, solvent, sober and quiet, and she forms her judgment with a candour which is, so to speak, bracing.

My own experiences in this line have been various and, now and then, decidedly peculiar. One landlady, who turned out to be a practising doctor, was perfectly willing to let me the room, but explained that she would require to keep a certain quantity of her surgical equipment in it. Where, precisely, it was to be kept, and what exactly it consisted of, I did not stay to learn. Another, a woman of suspicious temperament, looked me over from hat to boots, inquired what I did for a living, and received my answer with an expression which made it clear that she did not believe a word of it. Whatever my occupation might be, she evidently concluded, it was something shady.

Then there are the respectable houses. These are the houses with clean steps and a card in the window; they want references. They want, that is, a letter from somebody who will vouch for your character, your punctuality and your habits, and who is himself a person whom the landlady would consider respectable in turn. Where the chain of references is supposed to end I have never discovered.

And there are, finally, the fashionable districts of London. Here the question is not your character at all; that is beside the point. Nobody inquires whether you drink, or sing, or keep surgical instruments. They inquire only, and in the most civil manner imaginable, whether you are rich.

## Draft D

If you wish to find out what the world thinks of you—not your friends, who are prejudiced, nor your enemies, who are prejudiced the other way, but the plain, disinterested stranger—go looking for a room. I have done a good deal of it, and I have been told a number of things about myself which I had not before suspected.

There was the landlady who was a doctor. She had a room, and she was willing to let it. But she explained, very pleasantly, that she would wish to keep in it her surgical equipment. It would be there, in the corner. It would not be in my way. I looked at the corner, and I thought of the evenings, and of the lamp, and of the instruments glinting in the shadow while I tried to read, and I said I would think it over. I am thinking it over still.

There was the landlady who looked me up and down and asked me what I did. I told her. She did not believe me. Or rather, she believed that whatever I did it was not quite the sort of thing one talks about. Her manner said that she had seen my kind before. She did not say what my kind was. I have wondered since.

Then there are the respectable houses. In a respectable house you do not merely take a room; you are admitted to it, as to a club. You must have references. Who knows you? Who will say that you are what you say you are? You feel, as you give names, like a man applying for a position of trust.

And there are the fashionable districts of London, [district], where the streets are quiet and the doors are painted and the knockers shine. Here no one asks what you do, or who knows you. They do not need to. They need only to know one thing, and that is whether you have money—a great deal of money. You have not. They know it at once. It is, in its way, the most honest judgment of all.
