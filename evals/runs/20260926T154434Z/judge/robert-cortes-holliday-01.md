# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-01

## The passage (by the author)

It (he finds) injures him not, essentially; it ceases to particularly affect him, beyond his inward appraisement of the character before him. Toward him one acts simply in accordance with the instincts of one's nature. His status counsels no constraint, invites no display, has no property of stimulation. Thus the view of a famous man's character from the position of retail clerk is valuable. Mr. James's manner with Mr. Brownell would hardly be the same as toward us. But it was, exactly. There was present in his mind at the moment, was quite apparent, absolutely no consciousness of any distance of mind, or position, between him and us. He sought conversation (any suggestion of so equalising a thing as conversation with a clerk is not uncommonly repressed by the important as preposterous). In his own talk with us, he seemed to us to be a man consciously striving with the material of words and sentences to express his thought as well as he could.

He was very earnest. He looked up at us constantly (we are a little tall) with fixed concentration of gaze, and moved his hand to and fro as though seeking to balance his ideas. He asked questions with deference. Among other things, he desired very much to know what per cent. of the novels on the fiction table was the product of writers in England. "I live in England myself," he said, very simply, "and I am curious to know this." He expressed a little impatience at the measureless flood of mediocre fiction, making a fluttering gesture conveying a sense of impotence to give it attention. He barely glanced at the pile of his own book, and did not mention it. He did not seem at first (though we believe later he changed this opinion) to think highly of Arnold Bennett (this was at the first bloom of Mr. Bennett's vogue here), nor to have read him. "Oh, yes, yes; he is an English journalist," in a tone as though, merely a journalist. Clear artist in fibre. When he took his departure he bade us "Good day," and lifted his hat.

## Draft A

It has long been my conviction that the shop assistant sees the celebrated man as nobody else sees him. The reason is simple. There is nothing whatever to be gained by performing for a clerk: he cannot review your book, he will not invite you to dinner, and he is not, so far as anyone can calculate, going to put you into his memoirs. (I am aware that I am doing precisely that; but the principle holds.) So the distinguished visitor, standing at the counter, is apt to be whatever he really is. Sometimes that is not much. Sometimes it is considerably better than his photographs.

The novelist I have in mind was of the second kind. He talked to me earnestly, and as though my opinion were worth having, neither talking down to me nor talking over my head to some imaginary audience assembled at the back of the shop. He asked questions, and they were courteous questions: what proportion of the fiction we sold was British, how that proportion had moved of late, whether customers asked for British novels by name or merely accepted whatever was placed in front of them. I answered as intelligently as I could. Where I did not know, I said so; and he nodded, as one nods at a colleague who has been candid about the limits of his information. It was certainly the first time anybody famous had treated the statistics of our fiction table as a matter of serious interest.

What impressed me most was what he did not do. He did not look for his own books. Every author who comes into a shop looks for his own books; he does it sideways, as it were, with an elaborate air of examining the ceiling, but he does it. This man never glanced at them, although they were there, in a perfectly respectable pile, and he walked past them as if they belonged to somebody else entirely. When I mentioned a popular rival of his—a writer then selling, I should say, rather faster than he was—he dismissed him in a sentence. It was not a spiteful sentence. It was the sort of thing one says about the climate of a town one has decided not to live in.

Then he thanked me, and departed. I have met a good many famous people across a counter since then, and not many of them have troubled to ask me a question.

## Draft B

If you wish to know what a celebrated man is really like, do not ask his publisher, and do not ask his hostess. Ask the young man who sold him a book. Before the publisher and before the hostess the celebrated man is, naturally, on his best behaviour, or on what he takes to be his best behaviour. There is something to be got by it. But there is nothing whatever to be got by performing for a shop assistant. A shop assistant is not going to review him, or dine him, or put him in a book. A shop assistant is a sort of piece of furniture behind a counter. And so before the shop assistant the celebrated man is, for once, simply himself.

It was in this way, I feel, that I came to know [the novelist]. He treated me, I am happy to say, as an equal. I do not mean that he was condescending in a hearty manner, which is a thing quite different. I mean that he talked to me earnestly, as one man in the book business might talk to another man in the book business. He wished to be informed. He asked me, very politely, what share of our sales went to British fiction. He asked it as though my answer were a matter of real importance to him, and he waited for it. I gave him, I remember, a rather elaborate answer. He listened to all of it.

His own books were on a table not six feet from where we stood. There was a good pile of them. He did not look at them. He did not ask how they were going. I have since observed a considerable number of authors in bookshops, and I have observed that this is not the usual thing. The usual thing is for the author to drift, by a series of casual movements, toward his own books, and to stand by them, and to glance at the clerk.

When I mentioned [the rival novelist], whose novels were at that time selling as fast as we could unpack them, he dismissed him. It was done in a word or two, without heat. It was as though I had mentioned the weather in another town.

## Draft C

There is a particular vantage point from which a famous man may be seen as he really is, and it is not the lecture platform or the dinner table. It is the far side of a shop counter. A clerk in a bookstore can do nothing for a celebrity. He cannot praise him in print, cannot introduce him to anyone who matters, cannot add a line to his reputation. Nobody performs for a shop assistant, because there is nothing to be won by it, and so the shop assistant sees what is left when the performance stops.

I saw a good many well-known people in those years, and most of them confirmed my low expectations. They were brisk, or grand, or simply absent while their bodies bought things. The novelist I want to describe was different, and the difference has stayed with me longer than anything he wrote.

He talked to me as though I were a colleague. There was no condescension in it and no false heartiness either. He was earnest, even a little solemn, and he asked questions as if the answers might actually be of use to him. What share of the fiction we sold, he wanted to know, was British? Did our customers go looking for English novels, or did they come across them by accident? Was the proportion rising or falling? He put these questions politely, waited for my replies, and considered them. I had the odd sensation of being consulted.

What struck me most was what he left out. His own books were on the shelves within reach of his hand, and he did not glance at them. He did not ask how they were selling, did not steer the talk toward them, did not wait for me to mention them. It was as though they had been written by someone else and were no concern of his.

He was less indifferent to one of his rivals, a writer whose books were then selling far better than his own [example needed: the rival's name and titles]. When the name came up he dismissed it in a sentence, without heat and without further interest, and went back to his questions about British fiction.

I have met politer men since, and cleverer ones. I have not met many who treated a clerk as an equal when no one was watching.

## Draft D

I have long held that if you would know the true character of a celebrated man you should ask the fellow who sold him his collars. The shop assistant sees him when there is nothing whatever to be gained by performing. The great man does not play to the gallery before a clerk. The clerk is not the gallery. He is scarcely in the house. So what the clerk gets is the man himself, with the paint off.

I had this opportunity once, behind the counter of a bookstore, with a novelist whose name was then on every lip. I had braced myself for the manner, the lordly condescension, the weary patience of genius with the tradesman. I did not get it. He talked to me as one man talks to another who happens to know something about the matter in hand. He talked earnestly. He leaned upon the counter (it was not a very clean counter) and put his questions as though my answers might be worth hearing.

What did he want to know? He wanted to know, very politely, what share of our sales went to British fiction. How did it compare with the native article? Was it rising or falling? I told him what I knew, which was [detail needed: what the clerk told him], and he received it with the grave attention of a man taking down evidence.

Now, here is the curious part. Not once, in all that talk, did he mention his own books. They stood in a handsome row within reach of his elbow. He did not glance at them. He did not ask how they were selling. A lesser man will contrive to find his own volumes in a strange shop within the first two minutes, and will turn them face outward before he leaves.

He did mention one other novelist, a popular rival, whose books were then going out of our door by the armful. He disposed of him in a sentence. [quote needed: the novelist's dismissal of the rival]

Then he thanked me, and went. It is not often that the people who write books treat the people who sell them as equals. He did.
