# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-05

## The passage (by the author)

As to apartment houses, which are a very different matter: the newspapers publish at various seasons of the year copious Apartment-House Directories, with innumerable half-tone illustrations of these more or less sumptious places. And these directories are competent commentaries on their subject. George Moore remarked, "With business I have nothing to do—my concern is with art." Except that I live in one, with apartment houses I have nothing to do—my concern is with lodgings.

There is only one philosophical observation to be made upon apartment houses. And that is this: How can all these people afford to live in them? When you go to look at apartments you are shown a place that you don't like particularly. You don't think, Oh, how I'd just love to live here if I could only afford it! But you ask the rental as a matter of form. And you learn that this apartment rents for a sum greater (in all likelihood) than your entire salary. And yet, there are miles and miles of apartment houses even better than that. And goodness knows how many thousand people live in them! People whose names you never see in the newspapers as ones important in business, in society, art, literature, or anything else. Obscure people! Very ordinary people! Now where do they get all that money? But about lodgings:

I one time went to look at lodgings in Patchin Place. I had heard that Patchin Place was America's Latin Quarter. I thought it would be well to examine it. Patchin Place is a cul-de-sac behind Jefferson Market. A bizarre female person admitted me to the house there. It was not unreasonable to suppose that she had a certain failing. She slip-slod before me along a remarkably dark, rough-floored and dusty hall, and up a rickety stair. The lodging which she had to let was interesting but not attractive. The tenant, it seemed, who had just moved away had many faults trying to his landlady. He was very delinquent, for one thing, in the payment of his rent. And he was somewhat addicted to drink. This unfortunate propensity led him to keep very late hours, and caused him habitually to fall upstairs.

## Draft A

I have never been able to make out who it is that lives in the expensive flats. You pass them every day, great tall buildings with a man in a uniform at the door and a canopy out to the curb, and you are given to understand that the rents inside are something tremendous. Somebody pays them. But who? Not the famous, surely; there are not enough famous people to go round. No, the people who go in and out of those doors are, so far as I can see, perfectly anonymous, perfectly ordinary people. They look like you and me. And every month, without any apparent strain, they hand over a sum which would keep a man like me in comfort for a season. It is one of the mysteries of the city, and I have given it up.

The truth is, I do not care for flats of that kind at all. My interest is entirely in rooms, modest rooms, the kind of room one takes by the week and pays for (when one pays) in cash. A room with a bed, a table, a chair, and a window giving upon somebody else's wall. That is the sort of place I understand.

Not long ago I went to look at one in an alley in New York which is celebrated, I was told, as a haunt of the bohemian. It did not look celebrated. It looked, if anything, rather tired. The house I had come to see was a shabby affair; paint gone from the door, a stair that complained at every step, a smell in the hall of a great many dinners of long ago. The woman who showed me the room was not much interested in the room. She was interested in the last tenant.

He had drunk, it appeared. He had drunk a good deal, and at all hours, and with company. He had been late with the rent, and late again, and then later still. She told me what he had said and what she had said, and what the people across the hall had said, and I stood there in the middle of his former residence and listened to the whole of it.

It did not occur to her, I think, that she was telling me what would be said of me.

## Draft B

Who, precisely, are the people who inhabit the new luxury apartments? I have frequently wondered. They are not, so far as one can ascertain, persons of any particular distinction. You do not encounter their names in the newspapers, you do not meet them at dinners, and nobody points them out to a visitor as celebrities of the neighbourhood. They are, to all appearances, perfectly ordinary and entirely anonymous individuals, of the variety you sit beside in a crowded street car without troubling to glance at them twice. And yet, with the unfailing regularity of the tides, they surrender every month for their accommodation a sum which would maintain a respectable family in comfortable circumstances for a considerable season. How they manage it, and why they should want to, is a mystery I have never been able to penetrate.

Well, it is none of my business. My own interest in the question of lodging does not ascend above the modest rented room: the room with a solitary window, a bedstead somewhat too short, a washstand, a chair which doubles as a wardrobe, and a landlady who is, as it were, included with the furniture. Such a room possesses a history. The luxury apartment possesses only a rent.

Not long ago I went to inspect a room of this description in a bohemian alley in New York, a crooked, cobbled, forgotten sort of thoroughfare, where the houses lean together like elderly gossips exchanging confidences across a fence. The house in question was shabby; there is no gentler word for it. The paint had long since abandoned the struggle, the front steps sagged in the middle, and the knocker dangled from its door by a single, rather discouraged screw. I was received in the hallway by a woman who wished me to understand, before anything else whatever, that she had been sorely and continually tried.

Her previous tenant, it appeared, had been a confirmed drunkard, who returned at unreasonable hours, sang in the corridors, and paid his rent late, when he condescended to pay it at all. She related all this with considerable relish, standing upon the staircase while I stood beneath her looking as respectable as I could. Was I, she inquired, a drinking man? I was not. Did I pay punctually? I did. She seemed faintly disappointed.

## Draft C

It is one of the standing mysteries of the great city, who it is that lives in the expensive flats. You pass them every day. You see the awnings, the brass, the doorman in his uniform of a minor admiral, the marble hall beyond with its palms and its bench upon which no one has ever sat. Now and then you see a tenant go in. He is not a great financier. He is not anybody at all, so far as can be told by looking at him. He is a man of middle height, in an overcoat of the usual kind, carrying a parcel. She is a lady of the sort you would pass in any street car without a second glance. And these anonymous, entirely ordinary persons, it appears, pay for the privilege of their walls and their doorman a rent which it gives one a slight chill to hear named. How do they do it? Where does it come from? I do not know. I give it up.

The truth is, I am not really interested in them. My heart does not go out to the luxury flat. It is the modest room, the cheap room, the room at the top of the stairs with one window and a view of the chimneys, which has always had a hold on my imagination. There is a story in every such room; there is, so far as I have been able to discover, none whatever in a suite.

Not long ago I went to look at a certain alley in New York, [place], famed as a bohemian quarter. I had heard a great deal about it. I found a narrow way, and in it a house—a house very much down at the heel, its paint gone, its windows none too clean. A woman came out upon the steps while I stood looking. Did I want a room? Well, there had been a room. The gentleman who had it, she gave me to understand, was a gentleman who drank. He drank a good deal. And the rent—the rent came when it came, which was late, and sometimes it did not come at all. She shook her head. She was not sorry he was gone.

That, you see, is a room.

## Draft D

I have never understood who lives in the expensive flats. You pass the buildings every day, with their polished doors and their porters and their windows that give nothing away, and you know that somebody inside is paying a rent that would keep an ordinary household for a long time. Yet the people who come out of those doors are not famous, not visibly brilliant, not anyone you would look at twice in the street. They are anonymous. They wear sensible coats and carry sensible umbrellas and walk to the corner like everybody else. Where the money comes from, and why they choose to spend so much of it on a set of rooms, is a mystery I have given up trying to solve.

The truth is that those flats do not interest me. I cannot picture a life inside them, and I do not try very hard. What I care about are the modest rooms: the single room at the top of a narrow stair, the back bedroom let out by a widow, the place with a gas ring and a window onto somebody else's washing. These are the rooms where people actually make do, and making do is where the interesting part of living happens. A luxury flat tells you what someone can afford. A cheap room tells you who they are.

I learned this properly on a visit to a bohemian alley in New York, one of those short, crooked streets that seem to have been left behind when the rest of the city was straightened out. I had heard it was the kind of place where painters and writers lodged, and I went expecting something picturesque. What I found was a shabby house with a sagging step and a door that did not quite shut. The woman who showed me round was more interested in talking than in letting the room. She told me, without being asked, about the tenant who had lived there before: a man who drank, who came home at strange hours, and who was always late with the rent. She lowered her voice for the last part, as if the unpaid weeks were the real scandal.

I did not take the room. But I have remembered that conversation far longer than I have remembered any flat I have ever walked past, and I suspect that says everything about where my curiosity lives.
