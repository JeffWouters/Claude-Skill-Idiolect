## The passage (by the author)

There is a considerable literature on techniques that promise to improve the situation—relative sizing, probabilistic forecasting, the breaking down of work into ever smaller pieces—and I do not wish to dismiss any of them, since each captures something true about the problem. Yet I am struck by how often these techniques are adopted as a kind of procedural reassurance rather than as a genuine change in how uncertainty is discussed. A team may replace its hours with points, or its points with ranges, while the conversation surrounding the estimate remains exactly as it was: somebody asks when the work will be finished, somebody else provides an answer they suspect is optimistic, and everyone proceeds as though the answer were reliable. The technique changes; the institutional relationship to uncertainty does not. It may be that no method of estimation can compensate for an organisation that is unwilling to hear the word "unknown".

What would it look like to take uncertainty seriously? You might begin by treating every estimate as a statement about the present state of our knowledge rather than about the future, and by expecting it to change as that knowledge changes—which is to say, by treating revision as a sign of learning rather than as a failure of discipline. You might also distinguish, more carefully than we usually do, between the parts of a project that resemble work we have done before and the parts that are genuinely novel, since the former can often be estimated with reasonable confidence while the latter resist prediction almost entirely. None of this is especially original; it is, arguably, what thoughtful engineers have been saying for a very long time. The obstacle has never been a shortage of ideas but a reluctance, on all sides, to relinquish the comfort that a precise-sounding figure provides.

## Draft A

How often has a team adopted a new way of estimating and found, some months later, that nothing much has changed? It may be that the trouble with estimation lies less in our techniques than in how our organisations feel about uncertainty.

New sizing methods tend to arrive with considerable promise. Yet the pressures underneath remain as they were. If an organisation treats every estimate as a promise, then any method will eventually be bent towards producing numbers that feel safe to promise, and the new technique will inherit the distortions of the old one.

There is also the question of what an estimate is for. An estimate is, arguably, a statement of what we currently know, and what we currently know changes as the work proceeds. An estimate that is never revised is therefore not a sign of discipline but of a reluctance to learn. Who among us has not watched a figure produced at the very start of a project be defended long after everyone involved had stopped believing it?

It seems to me that the most useful step is to separate familiar work from novel work before forecasting either. Work a team has done many times can be estimated with reasonable confidence, because the team has, in some sense, already measured it. Work that is genuinely new cannot, and pretending otherwise only hides the uncertainty where nobody can see it. Treating the two differently, and saying openly which is which, does more than any new method to make a forecast honest.

Perhaps the question is not how to estimate better but how to become comfortable saying what we do not yet know.

## Draft B

Why does each new method of estimation arrive with such promise and leave so little changed? It may be that the method was never really the problem, and that what shapes our forecasts is less the technique we use than the attitude of the organisation towards uncertainty itself. A team can move from hours to points to some other unit of sizing, and for a while the novelty suggests that something has improved; underneath, however, the same expectations persist, and the same pressure to treat a guess as a promise quietly reasserts itself.

There is also the question of what an estimate is meant to be. If it is understood as a commitment, then revising it looks like failure, and people learn to pad their numbers or to defend them long after the evidence has moved on. If it is understood instead as a statement of what we currently know, then revising it is simply what happens as we learn more, and a changed estimate becomes a sign of attention rather than of error. The second view is, I suspect, the more honest one, though it asks a good deal of those who have to plan around the result.

It seems to me that the most useful step is to distinguish more carefully between work that is familiar and work that is genuinely new. Familiar work can arguably be forecast with some confidence, since the team has done something like it before and can draw on that experience; novel work cannot, and pretending otherwise merely hides the uncertainty inside a confident-looking figure. Treating the two separately allows the organisation to plan firmly where firmness is justified and loosely where it is not. Perhaps the aim of estimation is not to eliminate uncertainty but to be truthful about where it lies.

## Draft C

Every few years, it seems, a team discovers a new way of sizing its work. Hours give way to points, points give way to shirt sizes, shirt sizes give way to some further scheme that promises at last to make the future legible. The new method is introduced with some enthusiasm, the ceremonies are adjusted, and after a period of adaptation the forecasts turn out to be about as reliable as they were before. It would be easy to conclude that the techniques themselves are at fault, but I suspect the more honest explanation lies elsewhere. What rarely changes when a new sizing method arrives is the organisation's attitude to uncertainty, and it is that attitude, far more than any unit of measurement, that determines whether an estimate is useful.

An estimate is, or ought to be, a statement about what we currently know. It is a snapshot of understanding at a particular moment, and it should become more accurate as that understanding improves. The difficulty is that many organisations treat it instead as a promise, fixed at the point when knowledge was at its thinnest and defended thereafter as though revising it were an admission of failure. Under those conditions no technique can help, because the problem is not how the number was produced but what happens to it afterwards. A team that is permitted, and indeed expected, to revise its estimates as it learns will produce better forecasts with the crudest of methods than a team that must stand by its first guess with the most sophisticated.

It may also help to distinguish more carefully between kinds of work. Familiar tasks, the sort a team has done many times before, can often be estimated with reasonable confidence; novel work, where nobody quite knows what will be encountered, cannot, and pretending otherwise merely hides the risk. Forecasting the two separately is a modest step. It asks nothing more than that we be candid about where our knowledge ends.

## Draft D

Teams change estimation methods with some regularity. Hours give way to story points, story points to t-shirt sizes, t-shirt sizes to counting tickets or dropping estimates altogether. Each switch comes with enthusiasm and a workshop. And quite often, a few months later, nothing has really changed.

The reason is that the technique was rarely the problem. What sits underneath is the organisation's attitude to uncertainty. If a number, in whatever unit, is treated as a promise the moment it is spoken, people will pad it, defend it, and feel punished when reality disagrees. Swapping hours for points does not alter that. It only changes the vocabulary of the negotiation.

A more honest view is that an estimate is a statement about what the team knows right now. At the start of a piece of work that knowledge is thin, so the estimate is wide and tentative. As the work proceeds, the team learns things: the integration is harder than it looked, the data is cleaner than feared, a dependency turns out not to matter. The estimate should move with that learning. Revising it is not a failure of planning; it is planning working as intended. An organisation that expects revisions, and asks for them, gets better information than one that treats every change as slippage.

It also helps to stop treating all work as the same kind of thing. Some work is familiar. The team has done it many times, knows its shape, and can forecast it with reasonable confidence. Other work is novel, involving a new technology, an unclear problem, or a domain nobody on the team has touched before. Forecasting those two together, with the same method and the same expected precision, hides the real risk inside an average. Separating them lets people give firm answers where they can and honest ranges where they cannot. [example needed]

None of this requires a new technique. It requires deciding how the organisation wants to treat not knowing.
