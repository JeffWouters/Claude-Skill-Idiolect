# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-04

## The passage (by the author)

All this about Honor is not so much a digression as an approach. For if few people will quarrel with the lies of implication and of convention, and most people pray to be delivered from the lie of self-defence, the lie "of obligation" cannot be juggled away; and it is the lie of obligation which Honor commands. Honor has never permitted, still less commanded, a lie for personal gain or satisfaction of any kind; but there are cases when the gentleman must lie if he is to be a gentleman. The gentleman does not betray the friend who has trusted him, even though he may bitterly object to having that friend's secrets on his hands. From that supreme obligation lies sometimes of necessity result. I said just now that Honor and John Calvin must often have fought for the young soul; and it does not take an over-vivid imagination to conceive cases. Religion (in spite of the Decalogue) has tended to lump all lies together as the offspring of the Devil, while the code of the gentleman has always set aside a few lies as consecrated and de rigueur. But the gentleman, I venture to say, has always told those lies in the spirit in which a man lays down his life for his friend. For no gentleman lies, on any occasion, with unmixed pleasure. He feels, rather, as if he had put on rags.

It is easier—as some sociologists do—to plot the curves of a desire than to fix the boundaries of truth. The domain of truth is not world-wide: that, we know. They must be home-keepers indeed—perpetually cradled—who need never lie. Literal truth is imprisoned in a palace, like the Pope in the Vatican, affecting to be the ruler of the world. Even the faithful know that the claim is vain. The lies of obligation and convention are not, in the deepest sense, unveracious; for they are not pre-eminently intended to deceive. We expect them of other civilized beings and expect other civilized beings to expect them of us. Speaking such falsehoods, and such falsehoods only, we are still on truth's own ground. The lie told for the liar's own sake marks the moment when a man has passed from beneath her standard, across her shadowy sphere of influence, and is already hot-foot into the jungle.

## Draft A

The code of honour has never been quite the same thing as the moral law, and it is idle to pretend that it has. A gentleman, by that code, does not lie. Yet there are occasions—rare, and not of his choosing—when the same code obliges him to lie, and to lie without hesitation. A friend has trusted him with a confidence; some one who has no right to it demands to know the truth; and the gentleman, if he is worth the name, says whatever will keep the secret safe, and says it for his friend's sake alone. He never does it for himself, and there lies the whole distinction. A lie told for gain is, by the code, simply a lie, and the man who tells it has forfeited his standing.

Religion, to be sure, will not accept any such distinction. It condemns every lie without exception, the kindly as severely as the profitable, and I do not see how, on its own premises, it could do otherwise. The gentleman's code is more lenient; it excuses a few, and it has never been ashamed of excusing them. You may say that this is merely convenience dressed up as principle. Granted: yet the excuses it allows are remarkably few, and remarkably specific. It does not excuse the lie that spares oneself embarrassment, nor the lie that smooths a bargain, nor the lie that flatters a patron. It excuses, on the whole, only the lie that some one else has a claim upon—the lie told as a duty rather than a privilege, as it were ex officio.

And that, I fancy, is why such lies never wander very far from the truth. The customary falsehoods of polite society [example needed: a customary lie] deceive nobody, or very nearly nobody, since they are forms understood on both sides and are hardly meant to be believed (they would be a good deal less useful if they were!). The dutiful lie deceives, but only so far as the duty requires, and not a syllable further. It stands, one might say, at the very edge of truth, and keeps its eyes on it. The selfish lie is another creature altogether. It is not a small departure but a desertion; it abandons the truth entirely, because the truth was never its concern. The point is not that the gentleman lies less than other men. It is that he knows why he lies. That knowledge is his honour.

## Draft B

I remember being a good deal puzzled, as a child, by the discovery that gentlemen sometimes told lies. It had not occurred to me that honour and falsehood could live under the same roof. Yet the code of the gentleman—that curious, unwritten, and extraordinarily durable code—has always made room for a few lies; and it is worth asking, I think, precisely which ones.

The answer is simpler than one might suppose. A gentleman may have to lie to keep a friend's confidence. If a secret has been entrusted to him, and some one asks the question that would lay it bare, he is not expected to hand the secret over merely because he has been asked politely. He will lie, if he must; and he will not think the worse of himself for it. But he may never lie for gain. The moment the lie profits him—in money, in position, in the good opinion of a woman he wishes to marry—it ceases to be a gentleman's lie and becomes a common one. That is the whole of the distinction, and it is a very sharp one.

Now religion, it must be admitted, will have none of this. Religion condemns every lie, the kindly with the cruel, the dutiful with the selfish; and it is quite logical in doing so. The gentleman's code is not logical. It excuses a few lies, and it does so without apology. I do not pretend that the two can be reconciled. (It is always the theologian who wishes to reconcile them!) I observe only that most honourable men have lived by the second while professing the first, and that the world has not been much the worse for it.

For there is a real difference in kind, and not merely in motive. The customary lie—the "not at home," the "delighted to see you"—and the dutiful lie, told to shield a friend, both stay very near the truth. They deceive no one about anything that matters; they merely draw a curtain across a door. [example needed] The selfish lie is another creature altogether. It does not stay near the truth; it abandons it, and sets up a false world in its place for the liar's own convenience. The first kind of lie is a courtesy, or at worst a sacrifice. The second is a theft. And the gentleman, whatever else may be said of him, has always known the difference.

## Draft C

The code of honour that governs a gentleman is often taken to be simply a code of truthfulness, and in the main it is. His word is meant to be good, and a man known to lie for his own convenience loses the standing that the code exists to protect. Yet the code has never been quite so absolute as that description suggests, and the places where it bends are revealing.

The clearest case is the keeping of a confidence. A friend entrusts him with something private, and later he is asked about it by someone with no right to know. If a direct denial is the only way to protect what he was told, the code expects him to give it. Here the lie is not a breach of honour but a part of it, because loyalty to the friend is itself one of the things honour demands. What the code will never permit is a lie told for gain. The moment a gentleman lies to benefit himself, to win money, advantage or escape from blame, he has left the code entirely.

In this the code of honour parts company with religion. Religious teaching, in its strict form, condemns every lie without distinction. A falsehood is a falsehood, whatever the motive and whoever it helps, and good intentions do not change its nature. The gentleman's code is more lenient. It excuses a small number of lies, provided they are told out of duty or long custom and not out of self-interest.

It is worth asking why the code feels able to make these exceptions without collapsing. The answer seems to lie in how far each kind of lie strays from the truth. Customary lies, the conventional phrases that everyone understands and no one is deceived by, keep close to the truth because both sides know what is really meant. Dutiful lies, told to guard a friend or protect someone in danger, keep close to it in another way: they serve the same loyalty and good faith that truthfulness itself is meant to serve. The speaker's character remains honest even when a particular sentence is not.

Selfish lies are different in kind. They do not stay near the truth at all; they abandon it, because their whole aim is to make someone believe what is false for the liar's benefit. That is the line the code draws, and it is a sensible one.

## Draft D

There is a code—it is growing old-fashioned, I fancy, but it still exists—by which the gentleman orders his conduct. It is not written anywhere. It does not need to be. Every one who lives by it knows its articles, and every one who does not can recognise a breach of them at a glance. And one of its oddest articles concerns the lie.

The gentleman does not lie. That is the first rule, and the plainest. Yet he may, on occasion, have to. If a friend has trusted him with a confidence, he is its keeper ex officio; and if some one tries to pry it out of him, and keeping it requires a falsehood, the code does not merely permit the falsehood—it demands it. What it will never permit is the lie told for gain. A man who lies to get something is no gentleman; a man who lies to keep a trust may be the best of them.

Here the code parts company with religion. Religion, strictly read, condemns every lie without exception. It makes no allowance for the friend's secret, or for the question that should never have been asked. The gentleman's code excuses a few. Some one will say that this proves the code a lax and worldly thing. Granted; but it is worldly because it has to be lived in the world, and a rule that cannot be lived is apt to be quietly dropped altogether.

Let us look into the matter a little more closely. There are, it seems to me, two families of pardonable lies. There is the customary lie [example needed: a customary lie], the civil evasion that deceives no one because every one understands the convention. And there is the dutiful lie, told to shield another. Both stay near the truth. They do not alter the substance of things; they merely draw a curtain across it. The selfish lie is a different creature. It does not stay near the truth; it abandons it. It invents, it builds, it grows (as lies told for profit always grow!) until the liar himself can no longer find his way back.

The point is that the code cares not for the letter but for the direction. A lie that points toward loyalty is pardoned; a lie that points toward oneself is not. It is a rough distinction, and a clever casuist could find holes enough in it. But it has kept a good many men honest in the ways that matter. That is more than most codes can say.
