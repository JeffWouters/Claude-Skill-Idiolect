# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-07

## The passage (by the author)

Both from above and below, then, our colleges and schools have felt the hostile pressure. Colleges are, on the one hand, jeered at for doing their business badly, and, on the other, accused of being too difficult. We are always hearing that college is of no earthly use to a man except as he learns there to rub up against other men. We are always hearing, also, that the college curriculum is a cruel strain on the average boy or girl. On one score or another, the colleges are always being attacked; and the attack usually includes the hint that the real test of a "college education" is not the intrinsic value, but its success or failure in preparing the youth for something that has nothing to do with learning. Will it be of social or financial use to him? If not, why make sacrifices to get it? Far be it from me to assert that the intellectual flame never burns in the breast of collegiate youth! But I do believe it provable that there is far less tendency to regard learning as a good in itself, and far more tendency to cheat scholarship, if possible, in the interest of some other thing held good, than there was two generations ago. Ignorance of what real learning is, and a consequent suspicion of it; materialism, and a consequent intellectual laxity—both of these have done destructive work in the colleges.

The education of younger children is in like case. We put them into kindergartens where their reasoning powers are ruined; or, if we can afford it, we buy Montessori outfits that were invented for semi-imbeciles in Italian slums; or we send them to outdoor schools and give them prizes for sleeping. Every one knows what a fight the old universities have had to put up to keep their entrance standards at all. With the great new army of state universities admitting students from the public schools without examination, because they themselves are part of the big public-school system, how can it be otherwise?

## Draft A

The colleges are being attacked, at the moment, from two directions at once, and the curious thing is that the attackers do not appear to notice that they contradict each other. One party declares the college useless; the other complains that it is too difficult. Both, when you look into the matter, mean precisely the same thing. They mean that a degree does not pay quickly enough—in position, in money, in the respect of the neighbours—to be worth the trouble it costs. I do not know that any one has put it quite so plainly. But that is what the complaint amounts to, and the college is being judged by a standard it was never constructed to meet.

Let us consider the students themselves. Fewer of them, I fancy, care for scholarship for its own sake than cared for it two generations ago. I do not say none do; there are always a few. Yet the few were once a respectable company, and they are now something very like a remnant. The rest have come for whatever the degree will fetch in the market; and if it fetches less than they expected, they are not disappointed but indignant, as though they had been defrauded. You may say they have a right to be. Granted: yet a man who buys a violin in order to sell it again has no business to complain that it is difficult to play.

Nor does the trouble begin at the college gate. It begins in the nursery. The methods now fashionable for very young children [example needed: a named method or practice] require almost nothing of the child except that he be entertained; and a child who has never been asked for anything else will not easily learn to give it. Then, when he is older, he is admitted to the next school without an examination, and to the next after that. Nobody has tested him. Nobody, therefore, has failed him. The point is that a standard which is never applied is not a standard at all; it is merely an aspiration. We have aspired generously. We have not, I think, insisted on much. And the colleges, accepting whatever the schools send up to them, can hardly be blamed for beginning to resemble the schools.

## Draft B

I do not know how other people feel about the American college, but I am exceedingly sorry for it. It is being pulled in two directions at once, and by people who do not seem to notice that they contradict each other. On one side we are told that the college is useless: that it teaches nothing a man can sell, and that four years spent in it are four years lost. On the other side we are told that it is too hard: that it asks too much of the young, and drives away the boy who would do very well in business. Both critics, you will observe, judge it by the same measure. They ask what it pays—socially, or in hard cash—and when it does not pay enough, they condemn it. That the college might exist for some other end does not, apparently, occur to them.

This is not snobbishness; it is a matter of what our children are to become. For the students themselves have changed. Two generations ago, a fair number of the young men who went to college went because they cared for scholarship, for its own sake and for no other. There are fewer of them now—far fewer. I cannot think who will deny it. The boy of to-day wants his degree as he wants his motor-car, and for much the same reasons [example needed].

But the trouble does not begin at college; it begins long before. Let us look, by all means, at the schools where very young children are taught. The methods in fashion there are designed, it would seem, to spare the child any effort he does not enjoy; and a child who has never been asked to make an effort will not know how to make one later. Then, when he is grown, he finds that he may enter college without examination—that nobody means to ask him, at the door, whether he has learned anything at all. Is it any wonder that standards fall? They have been undermined at the foundation and knocked away at the top. I do not see how they could do anything else.

## Draft C

Our colleges are under attack from two directions at once, and the curious thing is that the attacks contradict each other. One set of critics complains that college is useless: that it teaches nothing a young man or woman will need in the world of business. Another set complains that college is too hard: that it demands more study than a reasonable student should be asked to give. Both judgments rest on the same assumption, which is that a college is to be measured by its social or financial payoff. If it does not lead to a better position or a larger income, it has failed; if it makes the road to those things steep, it has failed again.

What has quietly disappeared from this debate is the idea that learning might be worth having for its own sake. Fewer students today value scholarship for itself than did two generations earlier. The student who reads because the subject has seized him, who works at a difficult problem for the pleasure of mastering it, has become rarer. In his place stands the student who asks, of every course, what it will be worth to him afterwards. A college full of such students will soon be pressed to give them what they ask for, and what they ask for is rarely what is best.

The weakness does not begin at college. It begins much earlier, in the schooling of young children. The methods now favoured in the early years are designed, above all, to make learning painless, and in removing the pain they have too often removed the learning. [example needed] Children who have never been asked to struggle with anything do not arrive at higher schools ready to struggle.

Nor are they tested when they arrive. Admission without examination, adopted in the name of fairness, means that a school cannot know what its new students have learned, and so cannot hold them to anything. Standards that are never checked soon cease to be standards at all.

If we want colleges worth defending, we must first want students who value what colleges are for.

## Draft D

The colleges are in a curious position. They are attacked, and attacked from two sides at once, by critics who do not seem to notice that their charges cancel each other. One set of critics finds the colleges useless: the young man comes out of them no better fitted to make his way in the world than he went in. Another set finds them too hard: the young man is asked to learn things he will never need, and fails, and is made unhappy. Both complaints rest on the same assumption, which is that a college is to be judged by what it pays—in social advantage, or in hard money. I venture to say that no college worth the name was ever founded on that assumption.

Well: the assumption has won a great deal of ground. There are fewer students to-day who care for scholarship for its own sake than there were two generations ago. I do not say there are none. I say only that the boy who reads because he wishes to know has become something of an oddity among his fellows, whereas his grandfather's classmates would have thought him merely a good student. The college has not changed so much as the people who come to it.

And the trouble begins long before the college gate. It begins in the nursery school and the primary grade, where the methods now in favour ask a small child to do very little that is difficult and nothing that is dull. One can understand the kindly motive. Yet a child who has never been asked to work will not know how when the time comes. The same softness appears at the other end, in the practice of admitting students without examination [example needed: an institution or a figure showing how common this has become]. An entrance that tests nothing promises nothing. The standard is not raised by removing the bar.

Let us be plain about it. A school that will not ask for effort cannot give an education. It can only give a certificate.
