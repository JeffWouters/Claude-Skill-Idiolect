# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-08

## The passage (by the author)

I know only one person whom I could count on not to indulge herself in these conventional falsehoods, and she has never been able, so far as I know, to keep a friend. The habit of literal truth-telling, frankly, is self-indulgence of the worst. Nothing could be more delightful, in an evil sense, than telling certain people that their Christmas presents, their babies, and their hospitalities are all horrors which defy description; especially if one could count it a virtue to one's self to say those things starkly. But one cannot keep that weapon only for one's foes: the only excuse for saying inexcusable things is that one always says them. Roughly speaking, one's friends are the people of whom one thinks, habitually, pleasant things. But even friends can be annoying, or unbeautiful, or dull. And it is of the essence of those manners which are morals not to tell them so if one can help it. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend"—and must sometimes be dealt. But no stabbing over non-essentials! And above all, no stabbing when it is a pleasure to stab. Sometimes these truth-tellers congratulate themselves that their praise is immensely enhanced by its rarity. There, I fancy, they are mistaken: for in the first place, praise that is too long on the way loses its savor; and in the second, they acquire, I have noticed, a censorious habit of mind that prevents them from praising at all.

No: in the course of mere conventional living, a certain amount of lying must be done. "How do you do?" "I am very well, thank you." You may have indigestion, and in that case you have lied. Yet is it your business to make your acquaintance uncomfortable by telling him the facts in the case? Certain things are true of any man personally which have nothing to do with his social existence: personally, if he has a toothache, he has it; socially, he has not a toothache unless he mentions it. Then, there are lies which are not verbal at all—lies of implication. The early Puritans who objected to paint and powder, objected to them, I fancy, on perfectly Catholic grounds—it was immoral to make yourself attractive, and paint and powder were literally meretricious. On the same principle, to this day, a nun cuts off her hair. The modern feeling against paint and powder—for it does in some quarters survive—is rather, I imagine, on the score of dishonesty. You are not supposed to disguise a beautiful complexion if you really have it. But if you have not a good complexion, you are deceiving people—you are acting a lie—by making yourself look as if you had. The ground of the objection has shifted.

## Draft A

One is ready to grant, I say, that a lie is a bad thing. Let us condemn it, by all means, when it cheats a man of his money or a woman of her good name. But there our respect for veracity bids us stop. There is a kind of truth-telling which is not a virtue at all, but a fault; and it is the more dangerous because those who practise it are so very sure of their own righteousness.

I do not know how other people feel about the blunt person—the person who "always says what he thinks"—but I have never been able to like him. He tells you that your new book is dull, that your hat is unbecoming, that your children are spoiled; and he tells you so with a curious glow of satisfaction. That glow is the point. His honesty is, frankly, a pleasure to him, and a selfish one. He is not trying to help you; he is enjoying himself at your expense. And he pays for his enjoyment, in the end, as such people always do: one by one, his friends drift away from him, and he cannot for the life of him think why. He has told the truth, has he not? He has; and he has lost, in the telling, a good deal more than he has gained.

Consider, on the other hand, the thousand small untruths that make daily life endurable. Some one asks you how you are, and you say that you are very well, thank you. Perhaps you are not very well. Perhaps you have slept badly and your head aches. But the question was not a request for information, and your answer was not meant to deceive. It is a courtesy, like taking off one's hat; and to answer it with a full account of one's symptoms would be, not honesty, but a kind of bad manners. Nobody is harmed by the formula. Everybody would be harmed—a little—by its abolition.

It is interesting, I think, to watch how the argument against cosmetics has shifted. Our grandmothers (I am speaking, you see, of the respectable ones) objected to paint because it was wicked: it belonged to a certain sort of woman, and no decent woman would be mistaken for her. That objection has very nearly disappeared. What one hears now is something quite different. Paint, we are told, is wrong because it deceives—because the cheek pretends to a colour that is not its own [example needed]. The moral scruple has become a scruple about truth. And I cannot help thinking that it is a weaker scruple than the old one; for if we are to forbid every harmless deceit, we shall have to forbid half the civilities of life along with the rouge-pot.

## Draft B

There is a kind of person who prides himself on always telling the truth, and who means by this that he never softens anything. He tells a friend that her new dress is unbecoming, a colleague that his speech was tedious, an acquaintance that he looks ill. He calls this honesty. I think it is more often a fault, and not a small one.

Look closely at the blunt truth-teller and you will usually find that he enjoys himself. The pleasure of saying exactly what one thinks is a real pleasure, and it is a selfish one. The speaker gets the satisfaction; the listener gets the wound. We rarely hear the plain-spoken man deliver an unwelcome truth with any sign of reluctance. He delivers it with relish, and then wonders why his friends grow fewer each year. Friendships are not built on a steady diet of candour. They are built on a thousand small acts of consideration, many of which involve leaving something true unsaid, or saying something kinder than the whole truth.

Consider the most routine courtesy of all. Someone asks how you are, and you say you are fine. Perhaps you have a headache, perhaps you slept badly, perhaps you are worried about something you would rather not discuss. The answer is, strictly speaking, untrue. But no one is deceived in any way that matters. The question was a greeting, not an inquiry, and the answer is a greeting in return. To reply with a full account of one's condition would not be more honest in any useful sense. It would merely be tiresome, and a little inconsiderate of the person who asked.

These harmless untruths are the ordinary grease of social life. They let people pass one another without friction. A society in which everyone insisted on literal accuracy at every moment would not be a more virtuous society. It would be a more quarrelsome one, and a lonelier one.

It is instructive to see how the argument over cosmetics has shifted. There was a time when objections to paint and powder were moral in character: the painted face was taken as a sign of vanity or of worse. That objection has largely faded. What remains is a different complaint, that cosmetics are a form of deceit, because they present a face that is not quite the real one. [example needed] But if that is the charge, it proves too much. The same could be said of a well-cut coat, a pleasant tone of voice, or a smile offered to a stranger. All of them present us a little better than we are.

The truth deserves respect, and there are moments when nothing but the plain truth will do. But everyday social life is not one of those moments, and the person who treats it as if it were is not more honest than the rest of us. He is only less kind.

## Draft C

There is a kind of person who prides himself on telling the truth at all times and to every one, and who expects to be admired for it. I have known a few of him. He will tell you that your new hat is unbecoming, that your friend's book is dull, that you look tired; and when you wince, he will remind you that he is only being honest. One is meant to be grateful. I confess I have never managed it.

For the plain fact is that strict truth-telling, in the small affairs of everyday social life, is not a virtue but a fault. It is very often a selfish pleasure. The blunt man enjoys his bluntness; it gives him a comfortable sense of his own integrity, and it costs him nothing. It costs other people a good deal. He says what he thinks because he likes saying it, not because any one is the better for hearing it—and he generally ends by saying it to an empty room. Friendships do not survive many such candours. Nor should they, perhaps.

Some one will object that a lie is a lie, whatever its size. Granted; but let us look at the lies in question. A neighbour asks how you are, and you say you are quite well, though you slept badly and your head aches. Is that a sin? You have not deceived him about anything he wished to know. He did not want a report on your health; he wanted a greeting, and you gave him one. The formula is a courtesy, not a statement, and both of you understand it so. To answer it with the literal truth would be as odd as answering "Good morning" with a forecast of the weather. These routine untruths (and there are dozens of them in a single day!) do no harm to any one. They keep the machinery of life turning without a grinding of gears.

It is instructive, I think, to watch what has happened to the old quarrel about cosmetics. The older objection to paint and powder was a moral one: the painted woman was a vain woman, if not a worse one. That objection has very nearly disappeared. What one hears now is something rather different—that rouge is a kind of deceit, that the woman who uses it is pretending to a complexion she has not got. The charge has moved from morality to honesty. I fancy the moralists of to-day are hardly more reasonable than those of yesterday. Nobody is deceived by rouge who has any wish to know the truth; and nobody who has no such wish is harmed by being spared it.

My point is only this: there is a truthfulness that serves people and a truthfulness that serves oneself. The second deserves no praise.

## Draft D

I have known a good many people who prided themselves on invariably telling the truth, and I have liked remarkably few of them. That is not, I hope, because I am particularly fond of falsehood; it is because their truth was so seldom told for anybody's benefit but their own. The person who informs you exactly what he thinks of your new house, or your new book, or your new hat, is not serving truth in any disinterested fashion; he is enjoying himself, and enjoying himself at your expense. There is a genuine pleasure in bluntness—it is almost a sensual pleasure—and like most pleasures it has eventually to be paid for. He pays for it, as a rule, in friendships. One by one his acquaintances discover engagements elsewhere, and he calls it the price of honesty. I call it the price of self-indulgence.

Well: let us be fair to him. Some one will object that a lie is a lie, and that the small deception opens the door to the considerable one. Granted; but the objection proves altogether too much. When an acquaintance inquires how you are, and you answer that you are very well, thank you, you have quite possibly told an untruth, since you may have a headache or may have passed a wretched night. Does anybody seriously suppose that he wished to hear about it? He was not requesting information; he was offering a courtesy, and you returned it in the same currency. Nobody was deceived, because nobody expected to be told anything whatever. The point is that a statement which nobody takes as a statement cannot reasonably be called a lie in any sense that matters. It is a form of greeting, like a bow.

It is instructive, I fancy, to observe what has happened to the ancient quarrel over cosmetics. Formerly the objection to rouge and powder was a moral one: the painted face was the mark of a questionable woman, and there was an end of it. That objection has worn exceedingly thin, and hardly any one troubles to make it now. But the quarrel did not die; it merely shifted its ground. We are told now, not that paint is wicked, but that it is deceitful—that the woman who uses it is pretending to a complexion she does not possess. Heaven forbid that I should defend every pot of rouge in the country. Yet I notice that the accusation has travelled from sin to fraud, and that the very people who bring it will assure their hostess, without a blush of any description, that they have had a perfectly charming evening. They're entirely right to tell her so. They are only mistaken in supposing the rouge is worse.
