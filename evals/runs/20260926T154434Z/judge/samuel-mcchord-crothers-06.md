# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-06

## The passage (by the author)

Many attempts have been made to depict the character of an accomplished scamp. But Gil Blas and Roderick Random and Jonathan Wild the Great are after all seen from the outside. The author may attempt to do them justice, but there is a vein of irony that reveals a judgment of his own. They lack the essential element of incorrigibility, which is that the scamp does not suspect himself, has not found himself out.

No novelist has ever been able to give such a portraiture of a complacent criminal as was given a century ago in the autobiography of Stephen Burroughs.

Burroughs was the son of a worthy clergyman of Hanover, New Hampshire, and from the outset was looked upon as a black sheep. As a mere boy he ran away from home and joined the army, and then with equal irresponsibility deserted. He became a ship's surgeon, a privateersman, then a self-ordained minister, a counterfeiter, a teacher of youth, a founder of libraries, and a miscellaneous philanthropist. He was a patriot and an optimist and an enthusiastic worker in the cause of general education. He was chock-full of fine sentiment and had a gift for its expression. He enjoyed doing good, though in his own way, and never neglected any opportunity to rebuke those who he felt were in the wrong. He had a desire to reform the world, and had no doubt of the plans which he elaborated. He was capable on occasions of acts of magnanimity, which, while not appreciated by the public, gave him great pleasure in the retrospect. The intervals between his various enterprises were spent in New England jails. These experiences only deepened his love of liberty, which was one of the passions of his life.

Burroughs had a happy disposition that enabled him to get a measure of satisfaction out of all the vicissitudes of his life. He had learned neither to worry nor to repine. He was not troubled by the harsh judgments of his fellow men, for he had learned to find his happiness in the approbation of his own conscience.

## Draft A

The rogues of fiction are, after all, under a certain restraint. However freely they may run through the chapters, they are never quite out of sight of their author, who is waiting at the end of the book with his moral. He may be very indulgent. He may let his hero pick pockets with the grace of a gentleman and cheat at cards with the wit of a philosopher. But in the last chapter the reader is given to understand that these things were not to be imitated, and the rogue is judged, if not by the magistrate, then by the man who made him.

It is otherwise with the real rogue who writes his own memoirs. There is no one behind him to point the moral, and he does not feel the need of one. I have in mind an early American swindler, [name needed], whose account of himself is one of the most cheerful books I know. It is not the confession of a sinner. It is the record of a man who never for a moment doubted that he was in the right.

His career had a variety that a more scrupulous man might have envied. He was a soldier, and then a preacher. When preaching did not answer, he took to forging money. When that became inconvenient, he taught school. At intervals he was in prison. He passed from one of these callings to another without any sense of incongruity, as a traveller passes from one inn to the next. Each was a stage on the road, and he was always the same traveller.

What is remarkable is not that he did these things, but that he did them with so good a conscience. He had a conscience, and he consulted it constantly. It was his private tribunal, and it always found in his favor. Public opinion might condemn him; the courts might sentence him; but these were outside authorities whose jurisdiction he did not recognize. He trusted his own conscience as he trusted nothing else, and it never betrayed his confidence.

So he remained cheerful through everything. The prison did not depress him, for he did not feel that he belonged there. He was the one character in his story who was quite satisfied with its hero.

## Draft B

Novelists have always been fond of rogues. The swindler, the forger and the charming liar fill the pages of fiction, and readers follow them gladly. But a fictional rogue never escapes his author. Somewhere in the telling, the writer passes judgment, whether by a fall at the end, a moment of remorse, or simply a wry tone that lets the reader know the author sees through his creation. The rogue is enjoyed, but he is also weighed.

The early American swindler who left behind a memoir of his own life had no such author standing over him. He wrote his story himself, and the most striking thing about it is that he never once judged himself. From the first page to the last, the book is a record of complete self-approval.

His career was remarkably varied. He served as a soldier. He took up preaching. He forged money. He taught school. He spent time in prison. Any one of these turns might have given another man cause to pause and reconsider the direction of his life, and the combination of them might have produced a confession, or at least an apology. Instead, the memoir moves from one occupation to the next with the same easy confidence, as if each was a reasonable choice made by a reasonable man. [example needed]

What carried him through was his cheerfulness. He does not seem to have been troubled for long by anything, including the walls of a cell. Setbacks were described as the work of other people's misunderstanding, and successes as proof of his own ingenuity. He was never bitter, and he was never sorry.

The source of that steadiness was his conscience, or at least what he called by that name. He trusted it entirely. When the public disapproved of him, he concluded that the public was mistaken. When the law punished him, he regarded the law as a matter of opinion. His own inner verdict was the only one he recognized, and it always came back in his favour.

That is what makes the memoir so strange to read. It offers a rogue without an author to judge him, and a man who saw no need to do the judging himself.

## Draft C

The rogues of fiction are seldom left to themselves. However gaily they go about their business, there is always the author at their elbow, and the author has his own opinion of them. He may admire their wit and enjoy their escapes, but in the end he sees to it that they are found out, or repent, or at the very least are made to look a little foolish in the last chapter. The reader is let off with a moral, and the rogue with a sentence.

But the real rogue writes his own book, and there is no one to stand at his elbow. I have lately been reading the memoir of an early American swindler, [name needed], and what strikes me most is not his wickedness but his entire satisfaction with it. He does not judge himself, because it never occurs to him that there is anything to judge.

His career was of a pleasing variety. He was a soldier, and then a preacher. He forged money, and then he taught school. At intervals he was in prison. To the ordinary mind these callings do not follow naturally one upon another. To him they were all simply openings, and he entered each of them with the same cheerful readiness and left it with the same absence of regret. When he was preaching he preached, and when he was forging he forged, and in neither case does he seem to have done the work badly.

What sustained him through it all was a good conscience. It was not the kind of conscience that troubles its owner in the night. It was, rather, a confidential adviser who could always be relied upon to see his side of the case. Public Opinion was against him, and said so in the courts. But Public Opinion was a stranger, and his Conscience was an old friend. It is natural that he should have preferred the friend.

There is something almost enviable in such a state of mind. Most of us go through life with a divided allegiance, half wishing to do as we please and half afraid of what the neighbors will say. He had no such difficulty. He approved of himself from the beginning, and he never found reason to change his mind.

## Draft D

The rogues of fiction are never free. However gaily they cheat and steal through their adventures, there is always an author standing behind them, and the author, being a respectable person with a reputation to consider, sees to it that judgment overtakes them in the last chapter. We are allowed to enjoy their wickedness only on condition that we see it punished. The novelist is judge as well as biographer, and he cannot help it.

But the swindler who writes his own memoir is under no such constraint. In the recollections of one of these early American adventurers, [name needed], what astonishes the reader is not the length of the offences but the completeness of the self-approval. He was a soldier, and then a preacher. He forged money, and then he taught school. He spent a term in prison, which he treats as an enforced interval between engagements. Through all these changes of occupation he never once sat in judgment upon himself, for it did not occur to him that there was any case to try.

What is most remarkable is his cheerfulness. The ordinary sinner, when he is found out, becomes gloomy and apologetic and anxious to explain. This man was never gloomy. He had a conscience, and he consulted it regularly, and it always gave him an excellent character. Public Opinion might say what she liked; she was not, in his view, a competent authority. He had a higher court of appeal, and that court was always in session and always sat in his favour.

I would not hold him up as a model. Yet there is something wholesome in the spectacle of a man so entirely at peace with himself, when so many honest people spend their lives in anxious self-examination without ever being able to arrive at a verdict. He arrived at his verdict early. It was "Not guilty," and he never saw any reason to reopen the case.
