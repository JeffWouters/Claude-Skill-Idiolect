## The passage (by the author)

For, if such holy song Enwrap our fancy long, Time will run back and fetch the age of gold; And speckled vanity Will sicken soon and die, And leprous sin will melt from earthly mould;

Yea, Truth and Justice then Will down return to men, Orbed in a rainbow; and, like glories wearing, Mercy will sit between, Throned in celestial sheen, With radiant feet the tissued clouds down steering: And Heaven, as at some festival, Will open wide the gates of her high palace hall.

But all the imagery of the gala day of peace fades away before the immediate reality.

But wisest Fate says no, This must not yet be so.

This veto of "wisest Fate" is not absolute. It only calls a halt upon our imagination until the rest of our nature catches up with it. Mankind is not to have peace till it has suffered for it and worked for it. The workmen must do their work over and over again till they have learned the right way.

That the "Christian nations" are not hypocrites, but novices who have been making some progress toward the Christian ideal, becomes evident when we look back over their history. They are not the descendants of the simple shepherds of the plains of Bethlehem. Far from it! When they first began to "profess and call themselves Christians," they were not thinking of the beatitudes. They had not got that far.

Turn to the Heimskringla and read how King Olaf converted the pagan bonders.

"So King Olaf went into the God-house and a certain few of his men with him, and a certain few of the bonders. But when the king came whereas the gods were, there sat Thor the most honored of all the gods, adorned with gold and silver. Then King Olaf hove up the gold-wrought rod that he had in his hand and smote Thor that he fell down from the stall; and therewith ran forth all the king's men and tumbled down all the gods from their stalls. But whiles the king was in the God-house was Iron-Skeggi slain without, even at the very door, and that deed did the king's men. So when the king was come back to his folk he bade the bonders take one of two things, either all be christened, or else abide the brunt of battle with him. But after the death of Skeggi there was no leader among the folk of the bonders to raise up a banner against King Olaf. So the choice was taken of them to go to the king and obey his bidding. Then King Olaf christened all folk that were there and took hostages of the bonders that they would hold to their christening. Thereafter King Olaf caused men of his wend over all parts of Thrandheim; and now spoke no man against the faith of Christ. And so were all folk christened in the country-side."

## Draft A

Every generation seems to discover, as if for the first time, that the world ought to be at peace. The idea arrives with great force. It is plainly right, it is plainly within reach, and it is plainly overdue. And then, somehow, it is postponed again. The treaties are signed, the speeches are made, the leagues and congresses are formed, and a few years later the guns are out once more. It is tempting to conclude that the dream was foolish, or that the people who held it were hypocrites. I think both conclusions are mistaken.

The trouble is not that the dream is wrong. The trouble is that the dream runs ahead of us. We can imagine a peaceful world far more easily than we can become the kind of people who would keep one. Peace is not a gift that can be handed down to a population that has not learned how to hold it. It has to be earned, and it has to be earned repeatedly, by effort that looks small and unremarkable at any given moment and only shows its worth over a long stretch of time. Each attempt that fails is not wasted. It is part of the schooling.

This is also, I think, the right way to look at the nations that call themselves Christian. It is an easy charge to make that they are frauds: that they profess a gospel of peace and then go to war, that they preach mercy and practise conquest. The charge has some sting, but it misreads the situation. These nations are not finished products pretending to a virtue they lack. They are learners. They have taken up a standard far above their present conduct, and they are, slowly and badly, trying to grow into it. A pupil who has not yet mastered a lesson is not a liar for sitting in the classroom.

If that seems too generous, it helps to remember how rough the beginning was. Christendom did not start as a gentle gathering of the converted. In many places it started with compulsion. One of the old Norse sagas tells of a king who brought the new faith to his people in a way that would shock any modern missionary. He did not persuade them. He went among them, smashed their idols in front of them, and told them plainly that they could accept baptism or face war. That was how the cross arrived: not with an argument, but with a hammer and a threat.

It would be easy to hold up such a story as proof that the whole enterprise was corrupt from the root. I read it the other way. If that was the starting point, then the distance travelled since is remarkable. A faith that was once imposed by breaking statues is now, in the same lands, a source of the very conscience that condemns such methods. The standard has worked on the people who carried it, even when they carried it badly.

That is the hope for peace as well. We begin crudely, we fall short, and we try again. The dream stays ahead of us, and that is where it belongs.

## Draft B

Every generation has its vision of a world at peace, and every generation finds the vision postponed. The prophets announce it, the poets sing it, and the committees draw up its constitution. Then something happens, and the millennium is put off to a more convenient season. We are disappointed, and we are inclined to think that somebody has been deceiving us.

But the fault is not in the dream. It is in the dreamer's reckoning of time. We have a way of supposing that when we have seen a good thing clearly we have gone most of the way toward possessing it. The mind moves quickly; human nature moves at its own pace. A peace that can be had by wishing is not worth much, and a peace that is worth much cannot be had by wishing. It must be earned, and earned not once but over and over again, by people who are not yet quite fit to keep it.

This is a hard saying for the impatient reformer. He looks at the nations which call themselves Christian and asks why, if they believe what they profess, they do not practise it. He concludes that they are hypocrites, and that the whole profession is a pious fraud.

I should not put it so. A fraud is one who pretends to have what he knows he has not. A learner is one who has undertaken what he has not yet mastered. The Christian nations are learners. They have enrolled in a school whose lessons are too hard for them, and they make many blunders at the blackboard. That is a reason for keeping them after hours, not for turning them out of the school.

Nor should we forget how low the school began. We are apt to think of the conversion of the northern peoples as a gentle affair, in which the good tidings were received with glad hearts. The old records tell another story. In one of the Norse sagas a king undertakes to bring his people into the fold. He does not argue with them. He goes into the temple, smashes the idols, and tells the assembled farmers that they may choose between baptism and war. Most of them, being practical men, choose baptism.

It was not a promising beginning. The converts had changed their allegiance without changing their minds, and the new faith had been spread by the very methods it was meant to abolish. Meekness had been introduced at the point of the sword. One might have predicted that nothing would come of it.

Yet something did come of it. The words that had been imposed from without began, in the slow course of centuries, to work from within. Mercy, which had been a stranger in the land, found here and there a lodging. It is still a lodger rather than the master of the house, and it is frequently reminded of the fact.

When we are tempted to despair of the peace that keeps receding, it is well to remember where we started. We have come a long way from the smashing of idols. We have a long way yet to go. The distance behind us is the best argument for the distance ahead.

## Draft C

There is a pleasant dream that visits every generation, the dream of a world at peace, not for a season, but for good. The prophets have announced it and the poets have celebrated it, and every treaty has been signed in the comfortable belief that this time the arrangement would hold. Every time the date has been postponed. The dream is not at fault; it is simply a good deal further along the road than the dreamers are.

We are apt to think of peace as a gift that will one day be handed down to us, like an inheritance from a prosperous uncle who has no other relatives to consider. It is nothing of the kind. It is a wage, and it must be earned by the day. A people that has learned to keep its temper for a year has not thereby learned to keep it for a century, and it must try again, and fail, and try once more. The lesson is not learned once; each generation begins it where the last left off, and sometimes a little behind.

But there is a harsher judgment abroad. When the nations that call themselves Christian go to war, the critic is quick to denounce them as hypocrites who profess the Prince of Peace and practise the arts of Mars. What is this but fraud? I should not put it so. A fraud is one who pretends to possess what he has not, and these nations have never seriously claimed to be finished Christians. They are learners, and rather slow ones. The pupil who writes a crooked line in his copybook is not a forger. He is only a pupil.

Nor were the lessons easy at the beginning, and we forget how rough the schooling was. There is a Norse saga that tells of a king who undertook the conversion of his people with considerably more zeal than patience. He did not reason with them. He went into the temples and smashed the idols, and when the chieftains demurred he let them understand that the alternative to baptism was war. It is easy to smile at such a conversion, yet out of that rude beginning something gradually grew. The men who were driven to the water did not become saints overnight, but their children were a little less violent, and their grandchildren a little less violent still. The new faith was worn at first like a borrowed coat, too large in the shoulders and awkward in the sleeves, and only in time did it begin to fit.

That is the way of all great changes in the human temper. The ideal runs on ahead, and we come stumbling after it, complaining of the distance. It would be pleasanter if it waited for us. It never does, and perhaps that is precisely why we keep following.

## Draft D

The world has never lacked for dreamers of perpetual peace. In every age some one has drawn up the plan, and it has usually been a very good plan. The swords were to be beaten into ploughshares on a fixed day, and the nations were to sit down together like reasonable men who had at last found out their true interest. The trouble has not been with the plan. The trouble has been that the people for whom it was drawn were not ready to live in it. They admired the architecture, but they did not care to move in.

It is a hard saying, but I think it is a true one, that peace is not a gift to be handed down to men, but a habit which they must acquire. And habits are not acquired by a single resolution. They come by trying and failing and trying again. The dream always outruns the dreamer. When we are disappointed that the millennium has been postponed once more, we might remember that it has never been postponed by the prophets, but only by the rest of us, who have not yet learned our lessons.

There are those who grow bitter over this. They point to the nations that call themselves Christian and ask what right they have to the name. A Christian nation, they say, that goes to war is a contradiction in terms; it is a fraud, and the sooner it confesses the fraud the better. Ah, but that is to mistake the nature of the claim. A nation that calls itself Christian is not boasting of an attainment; it is enrolling itself in a school. It is not a graduate, but a learner, and a learner who makes mistakes in his exercises is not thereby proved to be an impostor. He is only proved to be a learner.

If we would be charitable in our judgment, we have only to look back at the beginnings. The schoolroom was a rough place when the first scholars were admitted. There is an old Norse saga which tells how a king, [name needed], set about the conversion of his people. He did not trouble himself with arguments. He went into the temple and smashed the idols, and then he told the assembled farmers that they might choose between baptism and war. It must be confessed that most of them chose baptism. It was the more comfortable alternative.

I do not suppose that the farmers went home with any very clear notion of what had happened to them. They had exchanged one set of observances for another, under circumstances that did not encourage theological inquiry. Yet from such beginnings the long education went on. The king who broke the idols was not a saint, but he was a beginning, and it is from beginnings of that sort that we have come. When we measure the distance we have travelled since, we need not despair because the road ahead is still a long one.
