## The passage (by the author)

What, then, might we do differently? One possibility is to treat each recurring meeting as something that must periodically earn its renewal, rather than as a permanent fixture whose continuation requires no argument. A team might pause every so often and ask, with genuine openness, whether the original purpose still holds, whether a different purpose has replaced it, and whether that new purpose is worth the collective attention it consumes. The conversation is often uncomfortable, since it touches on questions of status and inclusion that are rarely discussed openly; the person who organised the meeting may feel that their judgement is being questioned, and the people who value it most may be the least inclined to say so. It is nevertheless the kind of discomfort that tends to be productive, because it forces the team to articulate what it actually wants from its time together. I suspect that many meetings would survive such scrutiny in a modified form—shorter, less frequent, or reframed around a clearer question—and that a few would be quietly and gratefully retired.

There is a broader lesson here about the way institutions accumulate habits. Very little of what we do at work is designed from scratch; most of it is inherited, layered upon earlier decisions whose rationale has long since been forgotten, and the recurring meeting is simply the most visible example of a pattern that runs through our processes, our tools and our documentation alike. We tend to treat these inheritances as either sacred or absurd, when it may be that the more useful attitude is a kind of patient curiosity—asking what need the habit once served, whether that need still exists, and what we would lose if we let it go. Is it too much to hope that we might bring that curiosity to our calendars? Perhaps not; perhaps the humble weekly meeting is exactly where such a practice ought to begin, because it is small enough to change and familiar enough that changing it teaches us something about everything else.

## Draft A

Recurring meetings have a peculiar advantage over almost everything else in working life: once booked, they never have to argue for their existence again. A project needs a budget renewal, a role needs a job description, a tool needs a licence that someone signs off each year. A standing meeting just repeats. The simplest fix is to take that advantage away and make each meeting justify its continuation at regular intervals.

That can be as light as a note in the invite saying the series ends in three months unless someone renews it. Or it can be a few minutes, every so often, where the group asks plainly whether this meeting is still worth the time it takes. What is it for now? Would anyone miss it? Could it be shorter, less frequent, or folded into something else?

These conversations feel awkward. Nobody wants to be the person who suggests that a colleague's meeting is pointless, and the organiser may hear the question as a verdict on them. People tend to hedge, or say everything is fine, or wait for someone more senior to speak first. That discomfort is real, and it is worth naming so the group can get past it.

It is also worth pushing through, because the answers are often more interesting than expected. A review that begins as a question about one meeting frequently turns into a conversation about what the team actually wants from its time together. Some people value the meeting for reasons the organiser never intended. Others have been quietly resenting it for months. Both are useful to know. [example needed]

The same approach works for inherited routines more broadly: the weekly report nobody reads, the approval step nobody remembers adding, the template everyone fills in the same way. Rather than asking whether the habit is useful today, start by asking what need it originally met. Sometimes that need has gone, and the routine can go with it. Sometimes the need is still there and the routine is a poor way of meeting it. And sometimes the habit turns out to be doing exactly what it should, and the review simply confirms it.

## Draft C

What would happen if every standing meeting had to earn its place in the calendar again from time to time? It may be that very few would survive the question unchanged, and that the exercise would reveal something more useful than a list of cancellations. Most recurring meetings are never asked to justify themselves after the first few weeks; they acquire a kind of permanence simply by recurring, and the longer they continue the harder it becomes to imagine the week without them. A periodic review, in which each meeting must explain what it is for and whether it still does that, reverses this presumption and places the burden of proof where it arguably belongs.

Such reviews are, I suspect, rather awkward to conduct. Nobody enjoys suggesting that a meeting someone else organised has outlived its usefulness, and the conversation can easily feel like a judgement on the people involved rather than on the arrangement itself. The awkwardness, however, is often where the value lies, since it forces a team to say aloud what it actually wants from the time it spends together. You might find that a meeting everyone privately resented is in fact the only place where certain decisions get made, or that a meeting everyone defended is kept alive largely out of courtesy. Is it any wonder that teams which never ask the question drift so easily into habits they would not choose?

It seems to me that the same approach applies well beyond meetings, to the whole inheritance of routines that any team accumulates over time. The report that nobody reads, the approval step that nobody remembers introducing, the ritual that outlasted its champion—each of these was presumably created to meet some need, and the sensible first move is to ask what that need was before deciding whether to keep, change or abandon the practice. Sometimes the need has gone, and the routine can be retired without regret; sometimes the need remains but would be better met another way. Perhaps the point of reviewing an inherited habit is not to be rid of it but to understand, at last, what it was for.

## Draft D

Suppose that every standing meeting were required, at regular intervals, to make the case for its own continuation. Not in any elaborate way: simply that once in a while the people who attend it would stop and ask whether it still deserves the hour it occupies, and would be free to conclude that it does not. The idea sounds almost trivially sensible, and yet very few teams practise it. Meetings are easy to create and curiously difficult to end, since ending one seems to imply a judgement about whoever set it up, or about those who have kept attending without complaint. The periodic review removes some of that difficulty by making the question routine rather than accusatory. The meeting is not on trial; it is merely due for its check.

I should admit that such reviews tend to be awkward, at least at first. People are reluctant to say that a gathering bores them, or that they have long since stopped preparing for it, or that they attend chiefly because they fear what might be decided in their absence. But the awkwardness is, I think, precisely where the value lies. In having to say what the meeting is for, a team is obliged to say what it actually wants from its time together, and the answers are frequently more revealing than anyone expected. Some will want decisions; some will want information; some, perhaps, will want nothing more than a regular chance to see one another's faces. These are all legitimate needs, but they are different needs, and a single meeting rarely serves all of them well.

The same approach might usefully extend beyond meetings to the whole collection of habits a team inherits: the reports nobody reads, the approvals nobody questions, the rituals whose origins have been forgotten. The temptation with such practices is to treat them either as sacred or as absurd. A more generous and more productive question is to ask what need each one originally met. Sometimes that need has vanished, and the habit can be retired with a clear conscience; sometimes it persists in a different form, and the habit merely needs reshaping. Either way, we can hardly decide what to keep until we understand why it was there.
