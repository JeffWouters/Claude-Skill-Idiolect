## The passage (by the author)

There is also a subtler distortion at work. Status updates tend to describe activity rather than understanding; they report what has been done, which is easy to enumerate, rather than what has been learned, which is often more important but harder to articulate. A team may complete a great many tasks while its grasp of the underlying problem remains as uncertain as it was at the outset, and the update will faithfully record the tasks while saying almost nothing about the uncertainty. Conversely, a team may spend a period apparently producing little, while in fact resolving a fundamental question that will make everything subsequent far easier; the update, which has no category for such progress, will make that period look like a failure. You might say that the format rewards visible motion over invisible insight, and in doing so it shapes, perhaps more than we realise, what teams choose to work on.

What might a more truthful kind of update look like? It seems to me that it would make room for confidence as well as progress—for the reporter to say, in effect, how sure they are about what they are reporting, and what would change their mind. It would distinguish between the parts of the work that are well understood and the parts that remain obscure; it would treat an emerging concern as legitimate information rather than as an alarm that must be justified before it can be raised. Such updates are harder to write and harder to read, since they resist the comforting simplicity of a single colour. They also, I suspect, give the people who receive them a far more accurate sense of where things stand, which is presumably what the update was meant to provide in the first place.

Perhaps the difficulty of reporting status honestly is a symptom of something larger: the discomfort that institutions feel with uncertainty, and the corresponding pressure on individuals to present their work as more settled than it is. The update becomes a small performance of control, repeated at regular intervals, in which everyone understands that the performance is partly fictional but nobody wishes to be the first to abandon it. To change this is not a matter of redesigning a template but of cultivating a culture in which saying "I am not yet sure" is regarded as a sign of honesty rather than of weakness. We might discover, if we tried, that the most useful thing a status update can communicate is not how far we have come, but how clearly we can see the road ahead.

## Draft A

What is a progress report actually for? In principle it tells those who are not close to the work how the work is going; in practice it tends to tell them something narrower, which is what has been finished. The weekly update lists the tasks closed, the milestones reached, the features delivered, and each item is true. It may be that the trouble with progress reports lies not in any individual line but in what the format leaves unsaid, so that a report can be accurate in every detail and still misrepresent the state of a project.

A team learns a great deal in the course of a week that never appears as a finished task: the discovery that an assumption was wrong, the realisation that a dependency is more fragile than anyone thought, the slow accumulation of understanding about what the problem really is. None of this fits comfortably in a list of things done. A week in which a team completed little but learned that its approach would not work may well have been the most valuable week of the project, and yet the report will make it look like the least productive.

There is also the question of certainty. Reports are written in the language of accomplishment, and that language carries an implied confidence that the people writing them rarely feel. You may have written such a report yourself, choosing words that sound settled because unsettled words would invite questions you could not yet answer. The pressure here is arguably organisational rather than personal: organisations reward the appearance of control, and people quite reasonably learn to supply it, presenting themselves as more certain than they are because certainty is what the format seems to ask for.

It seems to me that a better report would say less about what has been done and more about what is known. It would state plainly how confident the team is in its current plan and why; it would name the open risks, including the ones that are uncomfortable to mention; it would record what the team has learned, especially where that learning changed the plan. None of this is complicated. It requires only that those who read the reports treat uncertainty as information rather than as a failing, which is considerably harder than it sounds.

The progress report is, in the end, one of the few regular occasions on which a team describes its own work to the wider organisation, and what it chooses to leave out says a good deal about what it believes is safe to say. Perhaps the most honest report is not the one with the longest list of completed tasks but the one that admits how much remains unknown.

## Draft B

What does a progress report actually tell the people who read it? On the surface, it describes where a project stands, setting out what has been done, what remains and whether the work is still on schedule. It may be that this description is accurate in every detail and still misleading as a whole, because the format records activity far more faithfully than it records understanding. Anyone who has written one of these reports will know how easily a week of genuine confusion can be summarised as a list of tasks marked complete.

The difficulty lies partly in what the report chooses to count. Completed tasks are visible and easily enumerated; insight is neither. A team may spend several days discovering that its original approach was mistaken, which is arguably the most valuable thing that could have happened to the project, and yet the report for that period will show very little movement, or will show movement on work that is about to be abandoned. You might reasonably conclude from such a document that the project is going well when it is in fact changing direction, or that it is struggling when it has just learned the thing it most needed to know.

There is also the matter of the pressure under which these reports are written. Most organisations reward the appearance of certainty, and people learn quickly that a report which admits doubt invites questions that a confident one does not. The result is a kind of quiet inflation, in which each layer of reporting removes a little more of the uncertainty that the people doing the work still feel, until what reaches the top of the organisation bears only a loose resemblance to the situation on the ground. Is it any wonder that projects so often seem to go wrong suddenly, when the signs were visible to the team for weeks?

It seems to me that a better report would say less about what has been finished and considerably more about what the team now believes, and how confidently it believes it. It would name the open risks plainly; it would distinguish the parts of the plan that have been tested from those that are still assumptions; it would record what has been learned, even when the learning has made the project look slower. None of this is difficult to write, though it may be difficult to write honestly in an organisation that punishes doubt. Perhaps the most accurate progress report, in the end, is not the one that sounds most certain but the one that is most candid about what it does not yet know.

## Draft C

Anyone who has written a progress report will know the particular discomfort of the exercise. The template asks what has been done, and so we list it: the tickets closed, the milestones reached, the meetings held and the documents circulated. What the template rarely asks is what we have learned, and this omission matters more than it might first appear. A project is not simply a sequence of tasks moving from one column to another; it is a gradual process of discovering what the work actually involves, and much of that discovery is invisible in a list of completed items. We may have closed a dozen tasks in a week and learned nothing of consequence, or closed none and learned that the whole approach needs to be reconsidered. The report, as usually constructed, treats these two weeks as though the first were the better one.

What might a more honest report look like? One possibility is that it would state, alongside whatever has been completed, how confident the team actually is in the remaining plan, and which risks remain open. Not the risks that have been neatly logged and assigned an owner, but the ones that still keep people uneasy: the dependency nobody quite understands, the assumption that has not yet been tested, the estimate that everyone privately suspects is optimistic. A report of this kind would be less tidy and, in some respects, less reassuring. It would also give its readers something closer to the information they need in order to make decisions, which is presumably why reports exist at all.

The difficulty is that the obstacle here is not really the template. Most organisations exert a steady, often unspoken pressure on people to appear more certain than they are. Confidence is rewarded; hesitation is read as weakness, or as a sign that someone has not done their homework. Under these conditions it is entirely rational to report activity rather than insight, since activity can be demonstrated and insight can only be asserted, and to present risks as managed rather than open, since an open risk invites questions that nobody wants to answer in a status meeting. The report becomes a kind of performance, and those who read it learn, over time, to discount it accordingly.

Changing this would require something more than a new format. It would require those who receive reports to welcome uncertainty when it is honestly stated, rather than treating it as a failure to be corrected. That is a harder shift than it sounds, because it asks leaders to tolerate the discomfort of not knowing. I suspect, though, that it is the only change likely to make progress reports describe the project rather than the mood around it.

## Draft D

Most progress reports tell you what got done. Tickets closed, features shipped, milestones ticked off. They look reassuring, and they are usually accurate as far as they go. The problem is that they don't go very far. A list of completed tasks describes activity, not understanding, and on most projects the thing that actually matters is what the team has learned since the last report.

Think about what really changes over a few weeks of work. The team discovers that an integration is harder than expected, or that a requirement nobody questioned turns out to be ambiguous, or that a risk everyone worried about has quietly gone away. These are the moments when the project's real shape becomes clearer. Yet none of them fit neatly into a "done" column. A report can show ten completed tasks while the most important news, that the hardest part is still ahead and less understood than anyone assumed, goes unmentioned.

Better reports would say something different. Instead of only listing output, they would state how confident the team is in the plan and why. They would name the open risks plainly: what we don't know yet, what could still go wrong, and what we are doing to find out. A report like that might read as less tidy, but it would give the people reading it a far more honest picture of where the project actually stands. It would also make surprises less likely, because the uncertainty would be visible before it turned into a missed date.

So why don't we write them that way? Part of the answer lies in the organisation around the report. Many organisations, often without meaning to, pressure people to appear more certain than they are. Confidence is read as competence, and doubt is read as a problem to be escalated. If admitting uncertainty invites extra scrutiny, people learn to leave it out. The report fills up with completed tasks because those are safe to share. The risks stay in people's heads, or in hallway conversations, until they can no longer be hidden.

Changing this is not mainly a matter of better templates. It depends on how leaders respond when someone writes "we are not sure." If that sentence is met with curiosity and support, people will keep writing it. If it is met with alarm, they will stop. [example needed]

A progress report should help people make decisions. To do that, it has to tell the truth about what is still uncertain, not just about what is already finished.
