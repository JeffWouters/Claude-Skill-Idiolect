# Judge packet synthetic-idris-04

## The passage (by the author)

Where do deadlines actually come from? The question may seem odd, since most deadlines arrive with an air of inevitability, as though they had been imposed by the nature of things rather than decided by particular people for particular reasons. Some are genuinely external—a regulatory requirement, a contractual obligation, an event that will happen whether or not the software is ready—but a considerable proportion are, in some sense, invented; they emerge from planning conversations, from the desire to have something to announce, from a general feeling that work without a date attached is work that will never be finished. I do not say this to dismiss them, since invention is not the same thing as arbitrariness. It may be that invented deadlines serve a real purpose, concentrating attention and forcing decisions that would otherwise be postponed indefinitely; the difficulty is that we so rarely distinguish between the invented and the unavoidable, and we therefore tend to treat all of them with the same anxious reverence.

Consider what happens to a team as a deadline approaches. There is often a gradual narrowing of attention, in which everything not directly connected to the immediate goal begins to seem like a distraction; conversations become shorter, decisions become quicker, and the careful deliberation that characterised the early stages of the project gives way to a more pragmatic, sometimes more ruthless, style of working. Some of this is healthy—the pressure clarifies priorities and dissolves the kind of endless refinement that can paralyse a project. Some of it, arguably, is not; corners are cut that will have to be repaired later, people work hours that they would never accept in calmer periods, and the quality of judgement declines precisely when the stakes are highest. Who decides whether the trade-off is worth making, and on what basis? In my experience the decision is almost never made explicitly; it simply happens, as the logic of the deadline asserts itself.

## Draft A

Ask a team where their deadline came from and the answer is often vaguer than you would expect. Some dates are genuinely imposed from outside: a regulatory change, a contract clause, a trade show, a season. Many are not. They were chosen internally, by someone, at some point, for reasons that made sense in a planning meeting and were never written down. Over time the origin fades and the date takes on the weight of a fixed fact.

That matters, because a date has real effects on the people working towards it. The early effect is often good. An approaching cutoff concentrates attention. Side projects get parked, decisions that had been circling for weeks get made, and the team finds a focus that is hard to create any other way. A date gives a group something shared to aim at, and that can be energising.

The later effects are less comfortable. As the cutoff gets closer, the pressure shifts from focus to compromise. Tests get skipped, reviews get lighter, known problems get logged for later. People work late and at weekends to close the gap. [example needed] Some of those shortcuts are harmless and some become the next quarter's incidents. The overwork has costs too, in tiredness, in mistakes, and in how people feel about the next deadline.

What is striking is that nobody explicitly weighs these two sides against each other. The person who picked the date was usually thinking about the benefit, about getting something out, creating momentum, or lining up with another plan. They were rarely thinking about the shortcuts and the late nights that the date would produce further down the line. And the team, by the time it feels those costs, tends to treat the date as immovable, because it no longer remembers that it was a choice.

A useful habit is simply to ask, when a date is set, where it comes from and what happens if it moves. If the answer is that nothing external depends on it, the date is a tool, and the team can decide how hard to lean on it.

Where do our deadlines come from? You might assume, if you have spent any time working towards one, that it was handed down from somewhere beyond the team's control. It may be that far more of them are chosen internally than we tend to admit.

This is not in itself a criticism. A date chosen by a team or its managers can serve a real purpose, and many are set with good intentions. The difficulty is that once a date has been announced, its origin is quickly forgotten, and it begins to carry the weight of an outside constraint whether or not it ever was one.

There is also the effect such dates have on the people working towards them. An approaching cutoff concentrates the mind; I suspect most of us have done some of our clearest work in the days before one. But the same pressure invites shortcuts, and decisions taken quickly because there is no longer time to take them well. It invites overwork too, and the cost of that is borne quietly, in evenings and weekends, rather than recorded anywhere a project plan would notice.

It seems to me that the striking thing is not that these effects exist but that nobody weighs them. Focus is gained and quality is put at risk, yet the exchange is rarely made explicitly by anyone with the standing to make it. The date simply arrives, and the trade-off happens underneath it, unexamined. Is it any wonder that teams feel the consequences of a choice they never knew was being made?

A due date is, in the end, one of the few decisions in working life whose costs are almost entirely deferred to the people who did not make it. Perhaps that is why it is so easy to set one, and so hard to set one well.

## Draft C

Where does a project deadline actually originate, if you trace it back through the plans that produced it? It may be that the answer is more often "from us" than we would care to admit. We tend to speak of due dates as though they were imposed from outside, by a customer or a regulator or some immovable event in the calendar, and some of them certainly are; a great many, however, turn out on inspection to have been chosen internally, by someone who required a date for a schedule and selected one that seemed reasonable. Once written down, such a date acquires an authority that its origins hardly justify, and the team begins to organise its work around it as though it were a fact about the world.

There is also the matter of what an approaching cutoff does to the people working towards it. You may have noticed that a deadline a few days away concentrates attention in a way that no amount of careful planning manages, and that decisions which had lingered indefinitely are suddenly settled in an afternoon. That sharpening of concentration is arguably the principal reason deadlines exist at all. The same pressure, though, invites shortcuts that would never otherwise be accepted, and it encourages a kind of overwork that is tolerated because it is supposed to be temporary. Who among us has not watched a temporary push quietly become the normal pace?

What strikes me most, in the end, is that nobody explicitly weighs these effects against one another. The benefits of concentration and the costs of haste are both genuine, and I suspect that in many cases the balance would favour the date if anyone paused to consider it; the difficulty is that the date is usually set before the question is asked, and by someone who will not bear the cost of the answer. Perhaps the most useful thing a team can do with a deadline is not to meet it or to miss it but to ask, before accepting it, who chose it and why.

## Draft D

Where do deadlines come from? The question sounds naive, but it is surprisingly rarely asked. We tend to speak of due dates as though they arrived from outside, handed down by customers or regulators or some external event that cannot be moved, and occasionally this is true. Far more often, I suspect, the date was chosen by someone inside the organisation: a manager who wanted a target, a planning exercise that needed an end point, a reasonable guess made months ago that has since hardened into a commitment. There is nothing wrong with this in itself. Teams need some sense of when things are meant to be finished, and an internal date can serve that purpose perfectly well. What is worth noticing is how quickly such a date loses any trace of its origins, so that a choice somebody made comes to be treated as a fact about the world.

The effect of an approaching cutoff on a team is genuinely mixed. On the one hand, there is the familiar sharpening of attention as the date draws near: priorities clarify, peripheral work is set aside, and decisions that had been deferred for weeks are suddenly made in an afternoon. On the other hand, the same pressure invites shortcuts that would never have been accepted earlier, and it draws people into longer hours and later evenings, with costs that tend to surface only after the date has passed. Both effects are real, and both follow from the same cause.

What seems to me most curious is that nobody ever weighs one against the other. The focus is welcomed and the overwork is regretted, but the two are seldom considered together, as the price and the benefit of a single decision. Were we to set a date with that trade-off explicitly in view, asking how much urgency we actually want and how much strain we are prepared to accept for it, we might choose differently, or at least choose knowingly. As things stand, the trade-off is made all the same; it is simply made by nobody in particular.
