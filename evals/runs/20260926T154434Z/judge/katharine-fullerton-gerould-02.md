# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-02

## The passage (by the author)

In this short distance, we have come near to the heart of the matter. Remember that the only lie forbidden in the Decalogue is false witness against one's neighbor. I may feel real respect for the lady on the porch—when I think that it may be hailing, I feel positive awe—but I should not like to make her the recipient of an intimate confidence. Such a person is wholly at the mercy of the unscrupulous. To be, for one's self, at the mercy of the unscrupulous, suggests, I admit, the saint; to be, for one's friends, at the mercy of the unscrupulous, suggests the cad. It is not, for the normal person, a pleasant thing to lie: it is much easier to record the truth quite automatically. There is in each of us who have been decently brought up a natural antipathy to saying "the thing which is not." The basis of truth is so much the finest basis on which to meet one's fellow-men! I have much sympathy with the unpopular people who cannot bring themselves, even in a ball-room, to "play the game." Of all ugly things to be, perhaps a liar is the ugliest. And yet, and yet—We may not go into Victor Hugo's rapture over the nun in Les Misérables who gave the mendacious answer to Javert; but which of us wishes she had told the inspector that Jean Valjean was actually in the room? Fortunately, such crucial instances are rare; and usually we can benefit our friends most by telling the truth about them—if it were not so, they would not be beloved. It is a poor cause which has to be lied for regularly. But in the rare case like that of Sœur Simplice, let us hope that we, too, should lie, and be as sure as she of making our peace with Heaven.

## Draft A

Truthfulness is, for most of us, the easiest of the virtues. One has only to say what is so; it asks no memory, no invention, and very little courage. And it makes the soundest footing for any relationship. A friend whose word one can take is worth a dozen who merely amuse. I have no quarrel with the plain truth-teller. I only wonder whether he is quite so safe a friend as he supposes.

For consider his position when he is trusted with a secret. Some one asks him a direct question. He cannot lie; he has made a principle of it. He may refuse to answer—but a refusal, as every one knows, is very often an answer in itself. The man who can never lie cannot safely guard a friend's confidence. He is honest at his friend's expense. That is not integrity. It is vanity.

You may say that this opens the door to every convenient falsehood. Granted: yet the door is a narrow one, and I do not propose to widen it. The lie told for one's own ease or profit remains what it always was. My point is only this: there are rare cases in which the truth does harm and the lie does none.

The classic instance—the locus classicus, if you like—is the hunted man. He has fled past your gate; his pursuers come up and ask which way he went. Is one bound to tell them? The strict moralist says yes (God save the mark!), and would send them down the right road with a clear conscience. Most of us, I suspect, would point the other way, and sleep none the worse for it. The instinct is sound. Truth is owed to those who have a right to it, and the pursuer has forfeited his.

Such cases are rare. Most of our lives need no lie at all. But a rule that cannot bend for the hunted man is not a rule. It is a trap.

## Draft B

Let us begin with the ordinary case, which is also, thank Heaven, the common one. Truthfulness is normally easy; it demands nothing of one but a little courage now and then, and it gives every friendship a foundation that will bear considerable weight. One knows where one stands with a truthful man. One need not keep a ledger of what one has told him, nor wonder what he has repeated elsewhere, and that ease is worth a good deal more than the small conveniences a lie can purchase. I have no quarrel with the principle. My point is only this: a principle that admits no exception whatever may end by betraying the very friendships it was meant to protect.

For consider the person who cannot lie—not will not, but cannot. He is an admirable creature in most weathers, and a thoroughly dangerous one to confide in. Put a friend's secret in his keeping, and then let some one ask him the direct question; he has no answer but the true one, or a silence that announces the same thing rather more loudly. Such a man may preserve his own integrity very well. He cannot, I think, preserve yours. You may say that he can decline to answer, and so he can, occasionally; but there are questions whose refusal is itself a confession. Granted that most of us are never put to it. Yet the difficulty is not imaginary. Suppose a hunted man has taken shelter under your roof, and his pursuers come to the door and inquire whether you have seen him [example needed: the circumstances of the case]. Is one really obliged to say yes? I cannot believe it. The lie in that doorway seems to me not a departure from virtue but an exercise of it. It is exceptional, to be sure, and it should remain exceptional. But it is right.

## Draft C

Let us grant, to begin with, that truthfulness is not a very heroic virtue. For most of us, most of the time, it is simply the easiest course. The truth has the great merit of requiring no memory; one need not keep a ledger of what one has said, and to whom. And it makes, I suppose, the only sound footing on which two people can build anything at all. A friendship founded on pleasant fictions is a house on the sand—charming, perhaps, until the first high tide.

But there our praise of candour bids us pause. Consider the man who has made it his boast that he can never, under any circumstances, tell a lie. He is an admirable person; I should not care to tell him a secret. For a secret is not kept by silence alone. Sooner or later some one asks the direct question—it is always some one who has a right to ask it!—and the man who cannot lie must either answer or so conspicuously refuse to answer that the refusal tells everything. His friend's confidence is safe with him only so long as nobody is curious. That is a very precarious safety. I do not know how other people feel, but I would rather trust a man who could lie for me, and would not do it lightly, than one who could not do it at all.

And there are cases, rare though they be, in which the lie seems not merely excusable but right. The old example is the hunted man who has fled into your house, and the pursuer at the door who asks where he has gone. [example needed] Frankly, I cannot think who would send the pursuer upstairs. The purist may tell us that the truth must be spoken though the heavens fall; but it is not the purist's heavens that are falling. In such an hour, the lie is the only honest thing left to say.

## Draft D

Most of the time, telling the truth costs us very little. We say what we think happened, what we believe, what we intend, and we move on. It is the easy course, and it is also the sound one, because every relationship worth having rests on the confidence that the other person means what they say. Friendship, trade and family life would all come apart quickly if we could not rely on each other's word. So the ordinary rule is plain enough: speak truly.

The difficulty comes when the truth is not ours alone. A friend tells us something in confidence, and later someone asks us directly about it. If we are the sort of person who simply cannot bring ourselves to say anything false, we are in a poor position to keep that confidence. Silence can help for a while, and so can turning the question aside, but a determined questioner will often read a refusal to answer as an answer. The person who can never lie turns out to be an unsafe keeper of other people's secrets, however honest they are about their own.

This does not mean that lying on a friend's behalf is generally allowed. Most of the time a careful silence, or a plain statement that the matter is not ours to discuss, does the work well enough, and we should reach for those first. But there are rare cases where nothing else will serve. The familiar example is the hunted man: someone fleeing for his life hides in our house, and his pursuers come to the door and ask whether we have seen him. To tell them the truth would be to hand him over. Here almost everyone feels that the lie is not merely permitted but right.

What marks such cases is that the lie is told for another person's protection, not our own advantage, and that the harm the truth would do is grave and immediate. Outside those narrow limits, the easy rule still holds.
