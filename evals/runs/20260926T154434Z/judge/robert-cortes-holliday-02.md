# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-02

## The passage (by the author)

Now, I myself hunt lodgings as some men hunt wild game.

Nothing is better when one is out of sorts, somewhat run down, and peevish with the world generally than to go out one fine afternoon and hunt lodgings In some remote part of town.

When in a foreign city, especially, the first thing I myself do, as soon as I am comfortably settled somewhere—and after, of course, having looked up the celebrated sights of the place, the Abbey, the Louvre, Grant's Tomb—-is to put in a day or so hunting lodgings.

Even to read in the papers of lodgings to let is refreshing and educational. All lodgings are "sunny"—in the papers. They are let mainly by "refined" persons, and are wonderfully "quiet." I remember last summer in London there was "a small sitting to let to a young lady." Lodgings, by the way, are usually "apartments" in England, as you know. Though, indeed, it is true that when a gentleman rents over there what we call a "furnished room" he is commonly said to "go into lodgings." A fine phrase, that; it is like to that fine old expression "commencing author." And that reminds me: the most fascinating lodgings to hunt, perhaps, anywhere, are called "chambers." These which I mean are in the old Inns of Court in London. And the most charming of these remaining is Staple Inn, off Holborn. I used frequently to hunt chambers in "the fayrest Inne of Chancerie." There are no "modern conveniences" there. You draw your own water at a pump in the venerable quadrangle, and you "find" your own light. But to return:

There was also last summer an apartment to let to a "respectable man" or, the announcement said, it "might do for friends." One of the reasons why many people are bored by hunting lodgings is that they are not humble in spirit. They seek proud lodgings.

## Draft A

When I am out of sorts I go and look at rooms. I do not want a room. I have a room. But there is no pastime known to me so certain to sweeten a sour mood as the looking at rooms to let. At home it is good; abroad it is better. In a strange city, where everything is a little absurd to begin with, it is the best thing I know.

You begin with the advertisements. An advertisement of a room to let is a kind of literature, and, like other kinds of literature, it is not to be taken too literally. A room which is "spacious" will be found to admit of one chair. A room which is "sunny" has been sunny, it is to be supposed, at some time. "Every convenience" means that there is a bath somewhere in the house, on some floor or other. And in England one must learn, besides, a new language. What we at home call by one name, they call by another; and what they call by the name we use for something else is something else again. [example needed] I have climbed a good many stairs to find that I did not know what I had come to see.

The most charming rooms of all, to my mind, are in the old legal inns of London. You go in through a gate, out of the roar, into a kind of quiet that does not seem to belong to a great city. There are old walls, and old stairs, and old doors with names painted beside them. The rooms are panelled, and low, and look out on a bit of grass. They are altogether delightful. They have, so far as I could discover, no comforts whatever.

And that is the lesson of the thing. If you would get pleasure from looking at rooms, go modestly. Do not expect a great deal. Expect, indeed, very little. Then you will not be disappointed—and you may very likely come away in a better humour than you went.

## Draft B

Some people cure a bad temper with a long walk and some with a hot bath. My own remedy is to go and look at rooms I have no intention of renting. I recommend it without reservation. There is something in climbing a strange staircase behind a landlady, hearing the key turn and stepping into a stranger's idea of comfort that drives out every sour thought. The pleasure is keenest abroad, where every door opens onto a way of living slightly different from one's own.

The first lesson for the beginner is that advertisements are works of imagination. A room described as spacious will accommodate a bed if the bed is not too ambitious. A view means that a window exists. Sunny is a statement of hope rather than of fact. None of this should discourage anyone; half the fun lies in measuring the prose against the premises.

The second lesson, for an American in England, is that the words themselves will not behave. The British have their own names for rooms and for arrangements of rooms, and they do not always mean what an American expects [example needed: specific British and American terms]. It is wise to ask, and wiser still to go and look, because the answer will rarely match the picture in one's head.

For the connoisseur, nothing in London compares with the old legal inns, those quiet courts and staircases that once housed lawyers and now let their chambers to anyone willing to climb. The rooms have a charm that no modern building can buy: old panelling, deep window seats, the sense that generations of clerks and barristers have worn the floor smooth before you. What they do not have is comfort. Heating is a matter of opinion, and hot water may be a rumour.

That is why the seeker must arrive modest. Go expecting the romance of the place and nothing more, and you will come away delighted. Go expecting a modern flat, and you will come away in a worse mood than the one you set out to cure.

## Draft C

Of all the pastimes open to a man of small means and a sour temper, I know of none so reliable as looking at rooms to let. You need not want a room. You need only go and look at one. Before you have climbed the second flight of stairs your grievance against the world has somehow mislaid itself, and by the time the landlady has shown you the cupboard you are in excellent spirits. Abroad the cure is surer still. In a strange city every door opens on a small play, and you are admitted free.

The advertisements, of course, are works of the imagination. A room described as "sunny" has at one time or another been visited by the sun. A room described as "commodious" will hold a bed and a man, provided he stands. "Every convenience" means that there is a tap somewhere on the premises. And the English, it should be said, do not speak our language in this department at all. [example needed: a British term for a room and what an American would call it] The seeker must learn a new vocabulary before he can learn to disbelieve it.

Well, the most charming rooms of all, to my mind, are in the old inns of the law in London. Here are staircases worn hollow by generations of lawyers, doors with names painted over names, windows that look upon a quiet court and a tree, a silence that the roar of the town outside does not quite get into. Here, too, are no comforts whatever. The bath is a matter for negotiation. The heating is a matter for faith. The kitchen, when there is one, is a matter for regret.

The wise seeker, then, goes modestly. He does not expect the advertisement. He does not expect America. He expects four walls, a view of something old, and a landlady with a story. He usually gets them. And if he does not take the room, why, he has had his afternoon.

## Draft D

There is no more reliable remedy for a sour temper than looking at rooms. I do not mean taking them; that is a different matter, and a serious one. I mean looking at them. You wake with a grievance against the universe, you read the advertisements over breakfast, and by noon you are climbing somebody's staircase with the agreeable, irresponsible feeling of a man who has committed himself to absolutely nothing. Abroad the remedy is even more effective, for unfamiliar staircases are better medicine than familiar ones.

The advertisements, naturally, are not to be believed. Every room is "bright," every neighbourhood "select," every landlady "refined"; a "sunny front room" faces a brick wall, and a "quiet house" stands next door to a livery stable. One learns to read these notices as one reads the testimonials of a patent medicine, with interest and without faith. In London there is the additional complication of the vocabulary, since the British terms for rooms are not the American ones, and a man who imagines he knows what he is asking for will discover, on the doorstep, that he has asked for something else. What, precisely, is a "set"? What are "chambers"? Well, you find out by climbing the stairs.

The old legal inns are the place for this sort of excursion. Their rooms are charming—panelled, low, crooked, overlooking a small courtyard and a solitary tree—and they are entirely without comforts. The water is somewhere below; the heating is a coal fire of your own construction; the light, in winter, is largely a matter of opinion. Nobody who wants a bath in his own quarters should apply there. But nobody who has seen them is perfectly content afterwards with an apartment.

The seeker, in short, should be modest in his expectations. If he wants luxury, he had better remain at an hotel. If he wants a room that has been inhabited for a long time, by people who were not in any particular hurry, he may perhaps find one, and he will pay for it in inconvenience. That is the fair price.
