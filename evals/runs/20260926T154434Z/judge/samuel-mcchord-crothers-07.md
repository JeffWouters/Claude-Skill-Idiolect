## The passage (by the author)

It happens, however, that every once in a while the hot passions of youth carry all before them. The account of what happened at the beginning of the civil wars in Israel is typical. King Rehoboam called a meeting of the elder statesmen of his kingdom. They outlined a policy that was eminently conciliatory. But we are told, "He forsook the counsel of the old men which they had given him, and consulted with the young men that were grown up with him and which stood by him."

That's the difficulty! The hardest thing about a good policy is to get it accepted by the people who have the power. What avails the wisdom of the old men when all the young men are "spoiling for a fight?" Something more is needed than statesman-like plans for strengthening the framework of civilization. You may have a fireproof structure, but you are not safe so long as it is crammed with highly inflammable material.

There is a periodicity in the passion for war. It marks the coming into power of a new generation. A quarter of a century from now "the good gray poet" Rudyard Kipling may be singing sweet lyrics of peace. All things come in time. The Kipling we know simply utters the sentiments of "the young men brought up with him." What he has been to his contemporaries Tennyson was to the generation before. Kipling never wrote a more scornful arraignment of peace or a more passionate glorification of war than Tennyson's "Maud."

We are listening to the invective of a youth whose aspirations have been crushed and ideals shattered by a civilization that seems to him to be soulless. He has seen something which to him is infinitely more cruel than the battle between contending hosts

Why do they prate of the blessings of peace? we have made them a curse, Pickpockets, each hand lusting for all that is not its own; And lust of gain, in the spirit of Cain, is it better or worse Than the heart of the citizen hissing in war on his own hearthstone?

## Draft A

There is an old story in Scripture of a king who came to the throne and was asked by his people to lighten their burdens. He took counsel first with the old men who had served his father, and they advised him to speak gently. He took counsel next with the young men who had grown up with him, and they advised him to speak roughly. He followed the young men. The kingdom was divided, and the elders have used the story as a text ever since.

But the elders perhaps seldom read the story to the end of its meaning. It is not only that the young are rash. It is that every generation, at intervals that recur with a certain regularity, grows weary of the wisdom it has inherited and longs to prove itself in some more strenuous fashion than its fathers did. The nation that has enjoyed a long peace is precisely the nation in which this restlessness appears.

This is why the most reasonable plans for peace so often come to nothing. They are drawn up by sober persons who assume that everybody wants what is sensible. The plans are sound enough. Yet if those who hold the power of the nation are in a mood for conflict, the soundness of a plan is no recommendation to them; it is an argument against it. You cannot persuade a man out of an appetite by showing him that it is unreasonable.

This temper is not confined to barbarous ages. Kipling to-day speaks for young men who have come to despise a mercenary peace, a peace of shopkeepers who count their profits and call the total civilisation. Tennyson, a generation earlier, voiced the same youthful scorn for such a peace in [quotation needed], and set the impatience of his own young contemporaries to music. The poet sings what the young feel. The old men listen, and shake their heads, and are not consulted.

## Draft B

There is an old story in the Bible of a king who, in a time of difficulty, had two sets of counsellors. The elders gave him the advice of experience. The young men who had grown up with him gave him the advice of their own temper. He turned his back upon the elders and followed his peers, and the result was division among his people.

We are accustomed to think of that king as a foolish young man who made a single blunder. But I suspect that the story is not so much about a king as about a generation. There come times in the history of every nation when the young men are consulted, or when, whether consulted or not, they make their voice heard. And the young men have always a certain impatience with the counsels of prudence.

It is on this point that many excellent peace plans come to grief. They are drawn up by sensible persons who have taken account of everything except the temper of those who hold power. The plan may be sound in every particular. It may show conclusively that war does not pay, that it ruins the victor as well as the vanquished. But what if those who are to carry it out do not wish to be paid? What if they crave the conflict for its own sake, as a relief from the dullness of a world that has grown too reasonable?

For peace, to the young, has a way of looking mercenary. It seems to be the policy of the counting-house, the timid preference of men who have something to lose. Kipling in our own day has given voice to this scorn, and Tennyson before him voiced it no less plainly. They were not the enemies of peace; they were the spokesmen of youth, which will not believe that the best thing a nation can do is to keep its shop open.

The elders may be right. But it is not enough to be right. One must also persuade the young men.

## Draft C

Every so often a nation that has lived at peace for a generation begins to drift toward war, and when one looks for the source of the drift, one finds it again and again among the young. It is not that the young are wicked or that the old are wise. It is that each generation must discover for itself what the one before it learned at a price, and some discoveries can only be made the hard way.

The pattern is very old. The Bible tells of a king who came to the throne and was offered two kinds of counsel. The elders who had served his father advised him to be moderate and to lighten the burden on his people. The young men who had grown up with him advised the opposite: to show strength and to answer complaint with a harder hand. He ignored the elders and followed his peers, and the kingdom divided. The story has been read for centuries as a warning, and for centuries it has been ignored in much the same way. [example needed]

The lesson for our own time is uncomfortable. It is possible to design a sound plan for peace, carefully reasoned and fair to all parties, and still see it fail. A plan for peace depends on the will of those who hold power, and if they crave conflict, no arrangement on paper will restrain them. The difficulty lies not in the plan but in the temper of the people who must carry it out.

Poets tend to feel that temper before politicians admit to it. Kipling gives voice to it today, and Tennyson did so earlier. In both, one hears the same note: a youthful scorn for peace when that peace seems to rest on trade and profit rather than on honour. To the young, a peace kept for the sake of commerce looks mercenary, even shameful, and war can appear by contrast as something clean and noble.

That scorn is not new, and it is not easily argued away. Anyone who hopes to keep the peace must first understand why so many of the young find it so hard to love.

## Draft D

There is an old story in the Bible of a king who came to his throne at a difficult time. The elders who had served his father gave him sober advice. They told him to deal gently with the people, and the people would serve him. It was very good advice, and he did not take it. He turned instead to the young men who had grown up with him, and they told him to be harsh. He followed his friends rather than his counsellors, and the kingdom was divided.

The story is worth remembering, for it has a way of repeating itself. Every so often a generation comes of age that is weary of the wisdom of its elders. It has heard a great deal about the blessings of peace, and it suspects that the blessings have been rather unevenly distributed. It looks about and sees a world that seems to it tame and calculating. What it wants is not safety but something to do.

This is very disconcerting to the makers of peace plans. They have worked everything out with great care. They have provided courts and treaties and methods of arbitration, and on paper there is nothing left to fight about. But a plan for peace is only as good as the temper of those who hold power. If they are eager for conflict, they will find it; and a reason can always be discovered afterward.

The poets are usually the first to feel the change. We hear to-day in Kipling the note of scorn for a peace that is merely mercenary, a peace of shopkeepers who count the cost of everything. Tennyson, in an earlier time, voiced the same impatience. [quotation needed: a line from Tennyson] It is the young man's complaint against a world that has grown comfortable, and it is none the less sincere for being a little theatrical.

I do not think we should dismiss it. The young are not wholly wrong in their suspicion that Peace has sometimes been a respectable name for Greed. But neither were the elders wrong in the story of the king. It is one thing to despise a mercenary peace. It is another thing to find a war that is any better.
