# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-01

## The passage (by the author)

The pure-bred American once cared for culture, and no longer—to the same extent, at least—does. If any one asks why America (I use the word loosely, as meaning our United States), having always, since the Revolution, been a democracy, can have cared for so undemocratic a thing, the answer is simple. The democracy of our forefathers was a purely pragmatic affair. The Declaration of Independence was framed by men living in a world where it was almost true enough to be workable. Roughly speaking, in pioneer and colonial days—wherever and whoever the pioneers and colonists may be—the community is a democracy because it is an aristocracy. In those grimmer worlds, the fittest do survive because there is no incubator process to keep the feeble going. A pioneer and colonial group, moreover, is apt to be like-minded; people do not exile themselves in each other's company unless they want the same things. Minor differences of opinion are swallowed up in like major needs: you form coalition governments against savages and famine or a specially detested tyranny. In the modern "I am as good as you are" sense, our ancestors were not democratic at all. They were democratic for their own special group, and a pragmatic truth misled them—as, because we admire them, we are permitting it to mislead us. They were Brahminical in their attitude to learning; they thought it supremely valuable, and they did not believe in—no Brahmin wants to believe in—a royal road to it, any more than they believed in a royal road to the salvation of the soul. They believed in intellectual, as much as they did in spiritual, election; and they certainly did not think that politics could influence either. Up to the last generation or two, they looked upon the cultured man as a peculiarly favored person; and because culture (unlike beauty, let us say) depended to some extent on the effort of the individual, they thought it fit to mention.

## Draft A

Now it is often asked, with a certain air of triumph, how a people so bent on equality could ever have set such store by learning. The question seems to me to answer itself, if one will only look at the people who asked it first. The early American communities were small; they were made up, for the most part, of like-minded men and women who had come a long way to be among their own kind. In such a society the strong rose naturally, as cream rises—no one had to vote them up, and no one would have thought of voting them down. Their superiority was not a grievance; it was a convenience.

We are apt to forget, too, that the founders were egalitarian only within their own circle. They believed—and said, with admirable eloquence—that all men were created equal; but they did not, I think, lose much sleep over the men who stood outside the door. Equality was a family affair. Inside the family, it was a very real thing; and it was quite compatible with a frank respect for the brother who had read more books than you had. (It is always the brother who has read more books!) Nobody suspected him of being an aristocrat. He was merely a better-furnished democrat.

For learning, in their view, was not a thing handed out. It was earned, as a farm was earned, by personal effort; by candle-grease and cold mornings and a stubborn refusal to be beaten by a Latin verb. A man who had got it had got it honestly, and his neighbours respected it much as they respected a well-cleared field. There was nothing undemocratic in that. The field was open to any one who would swing the axe. What they would not have understood—what would have struck them, I suspect, as faintly indecent—is the modern notion that learning may be distributed, like a public dole, to every one who holds out his hand. They prized it precisely because it could not be given away; and I am not at all sure that they were wrong.

## Draft B

It can seem strange that the early Americans, who built a democracy and spoke so often of equality, should have held learning in such high regard. A people that distrusted rank and title might be expected to distrust the rank that learning confers as well. Yet they did not, and the reason lies in the kind of society they actually lived in.

The frontier communities were small. They were made up of people who had come for much the same reasons, who shared much the same habits and beliefs, and who faced the same hard conditions. In such a group there was little need for a formal ladder of rank, because everyone could see who was capable. The strong, the able and the diligent rose to the top without anyone arranging it, and the others accepted their leadership as a plain fact of life rather than as an insult to their own standing.

The founders carried this outlook with them. Their egalitarianism was real, but it had edges. It applied to those within their own circle, to men of their own kind and condition, and it did not stretch much further than that. Equality, as they understood it, meant that no one inside the circle was born above another. It did not mean that every person's judgment was worth the same, or that distinction of any sort was suspect.

Learning fitted neatly into this picture. It was not an inheritance, like a title or an estate, passed down whether deserved or not. It had to be won, hour by hour, through a person's own effort and discipline. A man who had made himself learned had done something, and what he had done could be seen and respected by his neighbours. In that sense learning was the most democratic kind of distinction there was: open in principle to anyone willing to work for it, and closed to anyone who was not.

So the early Americans saw no contradiction. They honoured learning because they honoured effort, and a democracy of the able had every reason to prize what the able had earned.

## Draft C

It is a commonplace, to-day, that democracy and learning are natural enemies; that a people which believes every man as good as his neighbour can have no use for the scholar. Yet the early Americans prized learning, and prized it a good deal. The contradiction is only apparent. Let us look at the society they actually lived in.

The frontier community was small, and it was like-minded. Men did not go into the wilderness in random crowds; they went with their own kind, and the strong among them rose as naturally as cream rises in a pan. No one appointed them. No one needed to. Leadership, in such a group, was not conferred but discovered—and every one knew where to find it. That is not the democracy of the ballot-box. It is something older.

The founders, too, were egalitarian (I fancy they would have been startled to hear it questioned) within their own circle. Among themselves they were equals, and they said so handsomely. Beyond that circle their equality did not, on the whole, extend. Some one will object that this is hypocrisy. Granted; but it is hypocrisy of a very ordinary human sort, and it explains a great deal. A man who believes himself the equal of the best men he knows does not, therefore, believe that the best are no better than the worst.

The point is that learning, for them, was not a gift. It was earned. A man got it by personal effort—by candle-light, often, and against odds [example needed: an early American who taught himself]—and having got it, he respected it in others precisely because he knew its price. Learning handed out gratis would have seemed to them as suspect as a title bought for cash. What was earned could be honoured; what was merely given could not.

So there was no real quarrel between their democracy and their books. They believed in an open road, not in a level field. Every one might travel; not every one would arrive. That was the whole of their creed.

## Draft D

It is a common reproach, and on its face a fair one, that a democracy ought to have small use for learning. Why should a people who held every man as good as his neighbour have set such store by books? The answer, I fancy, lies in what those early communities actually were. A frontier settlement was not a nation; it was a small group of families, much of a mind, thrown together against the wilderness and against their own mistakes, and in such a place the strong rose as naturally as cream rises in a pan—no one appointed them, and no one needed to. The leader was simply the man who could lead. Nobody called that aristocracy. Everybody saw it, and nobody minded.

Some one will object that the founders talked of equality in terms that leave no room for rank. Granted; but they talked of it among themselves, and their equality was rather the equality of a club, generous inside the door and not particularly concerned with the street. They were egalitarian within their own circle, and they didn't, I think, pretend otherwise. It is we who have stretched their words to cover every one, and it is perhaps hardly fair to blame them for our reading. The point is that respect for learning never quarrelled with their democracy, because learning was not a privilege in their eyes. It was a wage. A man acquired it by his own effort, by giving to his mind the labour he gave to his fields [example needed: a real instance of such effort]; and whatever he earned in that way belonged to him as surely as his clearing did. Nobody could hand it to him. Nobody could take it away. That, to be sure, is not the modern notion. We have come to think of education as something distributed, like the post; they thought of it as something won—not a gift but a conquest. And a people who believe knowledge must be won will honour the winners.
