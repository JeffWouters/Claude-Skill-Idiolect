# Judge packet synthetic-noor-01

## The passage (by the author)

When the page is full, I take it down. I put it in a box file in the hall cupboard. Then I start a new one. I carry over the jobs that are not done.

The box file has about 200 old lists in it now. I looked through them last winter. It was strange. Like a diary, but only the dull parts.

And the dull parts told a story.

The same jobs came back again and again. Clean the gutter: twice a year, every year. Bleed the radiators: every October. The back gate: oiled 11 times in 15 years. It still squeaks.

Some jobs sat on the list for a long time. Paint the hall was carried over 34 times. That is more than two years. I did it in the end, in one weekend. It took less time than it took to keep writing it down.

Some jobs just vanished. Fix the doorbell. It sat there for months. Then one list it was gone. I have no memory of fixing it. I checked. The doorbell works. So I must have done it. Or someone did.

And the food part was the saddest and the best. You can see the years in it. Baby food, then fish fingers, then crisps and cola in large amounts. Then, for a few years, oat milk. Then less of everything, as the house got quieter.

I keep the lists for no good reason. I will not read them often. But I do not throw them out.

This morning the new list went up. Top part: milk, onions, bin bags. Middle part: fix the drip in the shower, sort the screw jars. Bottom part: paint the hall. Again.

The pencil hangs on a string from the lemon magnet. It is down to four centimetres. I sharpened it with the kitchen knife and hung it back.

So the box is not a record of jobs. It is a record of us. I did not write it as one. That is why I trust it.

This morning I put the lid back on the box. The newest sheet is still on the fridge. It is short. It has room for more.

## Draft D

We never threw the old to-do sheets away. Each one came off the fridge when it was full or too stained to read, and went into a box instead of the bin. Years of them are in there now. I started rereading them expecting a pile of chores. What I found was closer to a diary, written by a family that thought it was only making lists.

The first thing that jumps out is the rhythm. The same chores come back at the same point every year, as reliably as the seasons that cause them: [example needed] in spring, [example needed] before winter. Laid side by side, the sheets show a household that runs on a calendar nobody ever wrote down. We simply did the same things when the same time came around, and the lists remembered it for us.

Then there are the tasks that would not die. Some items were copied from one sheet to the next, month after month, sometimes year after year. [example needed] is the worst offender. You can see the handwriting change as different people carried it forward, each one sure they would get to it soon. Others did the opposite. They appear once or twice, and then they are simply gone. No line through them, no note, no sign they were ever done. Whatever happened to them, the sheets are not telling.

The grocery entries are the part I did not expect to find moving. Read in order, they trace the children growing up. Early sheets ask for [example needed]. Later ones change as tastes and appetites change, and the quantities creep upward. And then, sheet by sheet, the items that were only ever for them start to drop off. Nobody decided to stop buying them. The children left, and the list quietly noticed before we did.

It is a strange record to keep by accident. I am glad we kept it.
