# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-06

## The passage (by the author)

Some author—is it Mr. Kipling?—says of one of his heroines that she was as honest as her own front teeth. I know a great many people who are as honest as their own front teeth are false; and certainly no one expects them to go about calling attention to the skill of their dentist. Perhaps some sophist will say that between wearing false hair and declaring one's false hair to be one's own, there is all the difference in the world. I protest that it is tacit falsehood to wear it at all—unless one does it after the fashionless fashion of an ancient lady I knew in my childhood who, quite bald at the age of ninety-five, hung two wads of chestnut hair across her head, like saddle-bags, on a black velvet ribbon. And such tacit falsehoods are all in the spirit of the conventional politeness we use daily. To rouge a pale face may be vanity; but to thank a stupid hostess for the pleasure she has not given, is loving one's neighbor as one's self. I am inclined to think that even rouge is more often than not altruistic in intention. One does not wish, for the sake of society, to be either a fright or a brute. Certain things are demanded of every man who meets the world on its own ground. From the moment he has "accepted with pleasure," he has agreed to play the game; and it is as unfair of him to give or take the wrong cues as it would be for the castle to insist on making the knight's move. No: we need not go out of our way to lie; but we must not, even to be clever, tell the truth when an innocent lie is innocently demanded of us.

## Draft A

We are all, in small ways, liars, and most of us would be poorer company if we were not. Consider the ordinary furniture of social life: false hair, a touch of paint on the cheek, the warm thanks we offer a host whose evening nearly put us to sleep. None of these is strictly true, and none of them, I think, deserves to be called a vice.

Take adornment first. Whoever wears false hair or makeup is telling a silent untruth. The message is plain enough: this colour, this fullness, this bloom are mine. They are not, and everyone half knows they are not. Yet we do not denounce the wearer as a deceiver. We understand that the untruth is part of a convention, a kind of costume the world agrees to admire without examining too closely. The lie deceives no one who matters and harms no one at all.

Now the dull host. We leave a gathering where the talk was thin and the hours were long, and at the door we say that we have had a delightful time. A moralist might object that we have spoken falsely. But what would honesty have gained? A wounded host, an awkward farewell, and a small cruelty dressed up as virtue. Thanking such a host is kindness, not vice. It recognises the effort made on our behalf, even when the effort fell short.

The deeper point is that joining society means accepting its rules. No one is compelled to dine out, to pay calls, or to appear in company. But whoever chooses to do so enters into an understanding with everyone else present, and part of that understanding is a set of harmless, expected untruths. They are the small coin of social exchange. To refuse them while still claiming the pleasures of company is not integrity. It is simply bad manners, wearing a more flattering name.

## Draft B

There are lies that no one ever speaks aloud. The woman who wears false hair tells one every time she walks into a room; so does the woman whose colour comes out of a little box rather than out of the weather. She has said nothing, to be sure. But she has let us believe something that is not so, and she meant us to believe it. An artificial adornment is, I fancy, simply a silent untruth—a statement made in the language of appearances instead of the language of words.

Now I do not propose to be severe about it. Let us take the case a step further. You have dined with a dull host; you have been bored from the soup to the coffee; and at the door you thank him for a delightful evening. You may say that this is falsehood, and so, in the strict sense, it is. Granted; yet I cannot bring myself to call it a vice. It is a kindness, and a very small kindness at that. The man has done his best by you. He has not earned a lecture on the tedium of his conversation (Heaven forbid that any one should give it!), and he would be none the wiser for receiving one.

The point is that society is a game with rules, and one does not sit down to a game in order to quarrel with them. Whoever joins it agrees, tacitly, to a certain number of harmless and expected untruths: the false curl, the borrowed bloom, the grateful word to the tiresome host. Nobody is deceived by them in any way that matters. Every one knows the convention and every one keeps it. To refuse is not honesty but bad manners.

## Draft C

There is a kind of person who will not tell a lie, and who is very proud of it, and who nevertheless goes out to dinner in false hair. I do not say this to be unkind; I say it because the false hair is itself a lie, and a lie told without a word spoken. So is the discreet touch of colour on the cheek. So, if it comes to that, is every artificial adornment that invites the beholder to believe in a natural endowment which the wearer does not possess. One need not condemn it. One need only observe that it exists, and that nobody—least of all the wearer—regards it as a transgression against truth.

Well: if the silent untruth is permitted, I cannot understand why the spoken one should be executed for it. You have dined with a tedious host (every one has!), and on departing you thank him for a delightful evening. Is that a vice? Some one will say it is. Granted; but it is also a considerable kindness, and I fancy it is the kindness, not the untruth, that he takes home with him. He knows perfectly well what his evening amounted to. He does not want to be told. The point is that society is not a court of law, where every syllable is recorded and scrutinized; it is a game with rules, and one of the rules is that certain trivial falsehoods are expected of us. We did not make the rule, to be sure. But we joined the game, and in joining it we agreed to play it as it is played. The person who declines to say the customary thing ought, in common honesty, to remain at home. That's a hard saying, perhaps; yet I think it a fair one. To sit at another's table and refuse him the expected falsehood is not candour but ill-breeding. It is taking the dinner without paying for it.

## Draft D

One is ready to grant, I say, that truth is a very fine thing. Let us praise it, by all means; let us teach it to our children, and punish them when they forget it. But I cannot think that any one of us lives by it quite so strictly as he pretends. Look about you at the next dinner-party. The lady on your left has hair that did not grow on her head, and a colour in her cheeks that did not come from her blood. She has not said a word, yet she is telling you something that is not so. False hair and paint are, frankly, a silent untruth; they are made for nothing else. And I do not find that the lady is the worse for them, or that you are.

Then there is the host. He has given you a dull evening—a very dull evening—and at the door you take his hand and thank him for it. Is that a vice? I do not believe it. It is a kindness, and a small one, and he would be a churl who withheld it. The man who looks his host in the eye and says, "I have been bored," has told the truth, I suppose; but he has told it for his own satisfaction, and not for anybody's good.

The matter, it seems to me, is simple enough. If you wish to live in society, you must take society's rules along with its dinners. You cannot have the one without the other. And the rules (I am speaking, you see, of the harmless ones) include a certain number of lies that everybody expects and nobody is deceived by. To refuse them is not to be honest; it is merely to be rude. If one does not care to pay the price, one is quite free to stay at home—and nobody, I fancy, will greatly miss one.
