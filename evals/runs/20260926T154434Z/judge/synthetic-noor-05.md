## The passage (by the author)

There is a hiss. Air coming out. It smells of metal and old water. The hiss can go on for a while. On the back bedroom one it went on for almost a minute.

And then the sound changes. The hiss goes wet. A spit. Then a bead of water. That is the moment. Turn the key back, clockwise, snug. Not tight. You can strip the little valve if you force it.

Wipe the water. Check the cloth. Move on.

Eight of the nine had some air. The one in the hall had none. It never does. I do not know why. It is the lowest one in the house and the closest to the boiler. Something to do with that.

When they are all done, I go to the boiler. There is a pressure gauge on the front. A round dial with a needle. It should sit between 1 and 1.5 bar when cold. After bleeding it drops, because you let water out. This year it was down to 0.6.

So I top it up. Under the boiler there is a short silver hose with two taps. The filling loop. I open both taps, slowly, and watch the needle climb. At 1.2 I close them. The pipes groan while the new water finds its way round.

Then the heating goes back on.

I walk round the house and feel each radiator again. Top to bottom. Hot all over. The back bedroom one too. It is the best part of the job. You put your hand flat on the metal and the heat is even from edge to edge.

That night the house was quiet. No knocks. No tick. Just the low hum of the pump in the cupboard and the click as the thermostat turned it off.

I will do the same next October. The air comes back. It always does. Small bubbles, a little at a time, over the year.

## Draft A

I bleed the radiators once a year. It never stays done. The air comes back, every winter. It always has.

## Draft B

Then the closing. This is the part people rush. I do not. I hold a rag under the valve and I wait. First comes the air. A thin hiss, steady and dry. Then it starts to spit. Then water comes. A clean run of it, no bubbles, no sputter. That is when I close it. Not before. Not after the floor is wet.

Close it gently. A small turn is enough. Do not lean on the key. The valve is small and it is soft. If you force it shut, you damage the seat. Then it weeps all winter and you are back with the rag. I found that out the hard way. [example needed]

So I go round the house like that. Every radiator. Key, rag, wait, water, close. It is slow. It is meant to be slow.

Then the boiler. Letting out air lets out pressure too. Always. So when the last valve is shut, I go and look at the gauge. It has dropped. It always drops. The needle should sit at [pressure figure needed]. Mine sat well below it.

The filling hose is the short loop under the boiler. Two small taps, one at each end. I open the first. Then the second, slowly. You hear the water go in. I watch the needle, not the taps. It climbs faster than you think. When it reaches the mark, I close both taps. Then I check the needle again a minute later. If it has crept past the mark, I let a little out at a radiator. If it has fallen, there is still air somewhere.

Last, the walk round. I turn the heating up and give it time. Then I put a hand on each radiator. Top and bottom. Left end and right end. They should all be warm all over. A cold top means air. A cold patch low down is something else.

This year they were all warm. Every one. That will not last. The air comes back. It comes back every year. So next autumn I will get the key and the rag out again.

## Draft C

Bleeding the radiators is one of those jobs that comes round every year, and every year I half forget how it ends. Starting is easy. You turn the little valve, you hear the hiss, and you feel as though you are doing something useful. Finishing it properly is where the care goes.

The first thing to get right is knowing when to close each valve. The hiss of air is the signal to wait. When it stops and water starts to come through, even a thin bead of it, that is the moment. Close it then, and close it gently. The valve is small and it does not need force. Turning it hard to be sure only risks damaging the seat or the thread, and a valve that no longer shuts cleanly is a much bigger problem than a bit of air. A cloth underneath catches the drips; a steady hand does the rest.

Once every radiator has been done, the boiler pressure will have dropped, because the air you let out was taking up space the water now has to fill. So the next step is the filling hose. Open it slowly, watch the gauge, and stop when the needle reaches the right level for your system [pressure figure needed]. Then close it again, fully. It is easy to overshoot if you look away, so I do not look away.

The last part is the one people skip. After the heating has run for a while, go round the house and put a hand on every heater. Each one should feel warm across its whole surface, top to bottom, end to end. A cold band along the top usually means there is still air in it, and that radiator gets another turn.

Even when it all goes well, I know I will be back next year. Trapped air returns, slowly and quietly, whatever you do. That is not a failure of the job. It is simply the rhythm of it, and finishing well each time keeps the rhythm easy.

I bleed the radiators every autumn. Not because I remember. Because the pipes start to tick.

Tonight the house is quiet. No ticking. The key is on the shelf above the boiler, where it lives. The cloth is on the last radiator, drying.
