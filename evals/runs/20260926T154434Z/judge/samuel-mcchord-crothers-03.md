## The passage (by the author)

In this case they suddenly became very inimical, and Burroughs was again compelled to depart under cover of darkness. His night thoughts were always among his very best.

"Journeying on, I had time for reflection. At the dead of night—all alone—reflection would have its operation. A very singular scene have I now passed through, said I, and to what does it amount? Have I acted with propriety as a man, or have I deviated from the path of rectitude? I have had an unheard-of, disagreeable part to act; I do not feel entirely satisfied with myself in this business, and yet I do not know how I could have done otherwise, and have made the matter better. My situation has been such that I have violated the principle of veracity which we implicitly pledge ourselves to maintain towards each other, as a general thing, in society. Whether my peculiar circumstances would warrant such a line of procedure is the question. I know many things will be said in favor of it as well as against it."

From this difficult question of casuistry he found relief in reverting to the one instance in which he had been clearly wrong, viz., joining the young men in Hanover in their raid on the farmer's beehive. "My giving countenance to an open breach of the laws of the land in the case of the bees was a matter in which I was justly reprehensible; but that matter is now past. I must take things as they are, and under these circumstances do the best I can. I know the world will blame me, but I wish to justify my conduct to myself, let the world think what it may."

In this endeavor he was highly successful; and as he walked on, his spirits rose. He contrasted his own clear views with the muddled ideas of his late parishioners. "They understand the matter in the gross, that I have preached under a fictitious name and character, and consequently have roused many ideas in the minds of the people not founded on fact. Therefore they concluded from this general view the whole to be founded on wrong. The name impostor is therefore easily fixed on my character. An impostor, we generally conceive, puts on feigned appearances in order to enrich or aggrandize himself to the damage of others. That this is not the case with me in this transaction, I think is clear. That I have aimed at nothing but the bare necessaries of life, is a fact."

## Draft A

The preacher left town by night. It was not the hour he would have chosen for a departure, but the choice had been taken out of his hands. The congregation had begun to ask questions, and a man whose ordination rests on his own say-so does not stay to answer questions. He took the back road, and he walked fast.

A man who walks fast in the dark has time to think, and the preacher thought. He was not a hardened person. He had a conscience, and it was a conscience of the reasonable sort, which is willing to hear both sides. He put the case to it fairly. Had he done wrong?

He had, it must be admitted, told the people that he was a minister of the gospel, which he was not. He had preached to them, taken up their collections, and eaten at their tables. On the face of it this looked bad. But the preacher was not one to be governed by the face of things. He went into the matter more deeply, and the deeper he went the better it looked.

For what, after all, is a fraud? A fraud is one who deceives in order to enrich himself. He had not been enriched. He had been fed. He had asked of the world nothing but his subsistence, and he had asked for that in the only terms the world seemed willing to hear. The merchant takes his profit, and nobody calls him a fraud. The preacher had taken no profit at all. He had taken his supper. There is a difference between a speculator and a man who is merely trying to stay alive, and he was not going to let his conscience confuse the two.

Thus reassured, he was able to review the rest of his life with a good deal of composure. He went over it item by item, as an honest bookkeeper goes over his accounts, and he found it, on the whole, in order.

There was one entry that troubled him. Years before, he had stolen honey from a farmer. He could offer no defence for it. He had not been starving; he had wanted the honey. It was a plain theft, committed for the pleasure of it, and it lay upon his soul.

He walked on through the dark, a man at peace with himself except in the matter of the honey. It is a comfortable thing to have a conscience that can be precise.

## Draft B

He left the town a little after midnight, by the back road, with his conscience under examination. The night was dark and the road was rough, which suited him, for he had no wish to be recognized, and a man who is walking fast in the dark has an excellent opportunity for reflection.

The question he put to himself was a plain one. Had he done wrong? He had preached without ordination, prayed without conviction, and accepted the hospitality of people who believed him to be something he was not. On the face of it the case looked bad. But he was not a man to be satisfied with the face of anything, and he determined to go into the particulars.

He began by taking an inventory of his sins, as an honest merchant takes an inventory of his stock. He went over the shelves carefully and found them surprisingly bare. The sermons, when he examined them, were sound enough; he had borrowed them from better men, and they had done no harm in the borrowing. The prayers had been received with every appearance of satisfaction. Nobody, so far as he could discover, had been made worse by his ministry, and several had been made quite comfortable.

There remained, it is true, one item that would not be explained away. Years before, he had stolen a quantity of honey from a farmer. [example needed: the circumstances of the theft] He had never paid for it, and the recollection troubled him. Here, at last, was genuine guilt, and he acknowledged it frankly, with something like relief. It is a great comfort to a man in his position to find one sin he can repent of without reservation, since it lends a certain credibility to his acquittal on all the other counts.

As for the word fraud, he rejected it altogether. A fraud, he reasoned, is a man who deceives others for profit, and he had never made a profit in his life. He had asked for nothing beyond his board and a bed. He had sought subsistence, not riches. Was it a crime to wish to eat? He thought not.

By the time the sky began to lighten he had reached a verdict, and it was a merciful one. He was guilty of the honey. On everything else the court found for the defendant.

## Draft C

The night was dark, and the preacher, if we may still call him so, was making the best time he could along the road that led away from the town. He did not look back. He had reasons for not looking back, and they were good reasons. But as the distance grew between him and his late congregation, his mind, relieved of immediate anxiety, turned to a more leisurely inquiry. Had he done wrong?

It was a question he was well fitted to answer. He had spent a good many evenings of late expounding the moral law to others, and he saw no reason why he should not apply it to himself. He was not a man to shirk an examination of conscience, provided he might conduct it himself.

He went over the ground carefully. He had represented himself as something he was not. That was undeniable. But what, after all, had he taken? He had taken his meals and his lodging, and such small contributions as the people saw fit to give. He had not grown rich. He was leaving with very little more than he had brought. When he put the matter to himself in this way, it did not look so very black.

There was, it must be confessed, one thing that troubled him. It was not anything that had happened in the town. It was an old affair, and it had to do with a farmer and some honey. He had taken the honey. He had not asked for it, and he had not paid for it. He had been young then, and the honey had been very good, but he did not try to excuse himself on either ground. That had been theft, plain theft, and he owned it. It was a relief to have found something that he could honestly repent of. It gave a certain solidity to the rest of his reasoning.

But a fraud? No, he would not accept the word. A fraud, as he understood it, was a man who deceived others in order to enrich himself. He had never looked for profit. He had sought only subsistence, the bare means of keeping soul and body together. Was a man to be condemned because he wished to eat? The birds were fed, and nobody called them impostors.

By the time the sky began to lighten, the argument was complete. He had weighed himself in the balance and had not been found wanting, except in the matter of the honey. And the honey was a long way behind him.

## Draft D

He left in the dark, which seemed the wisest hour for it. The road was quiet, and he walked quickly, putting as much distance as he could between himself and the people who had so lately listened to him preach. It was only once he was well clear that he allowed himself to slow down and think.

The question that came to him was a natural one. Had he done wrong? He turned it over carefully, the way a man turns over a coin he suspects may be bad. He had stood before those people as a preacher, which he was not. He had spoken to them with a confidence he had not earned. They had trusted him. Put that way, it did not sound well.

But he was not inclined to put it that way for long. He began, instead, to go through his conduct piece by piece, looking honestly for anything he would be willing to call a sin. And, to his own mild surprise, he found one. Some time ago, well before any of this, he had taken honey from a farmer. He had not asked for it and he had not paid for it. That, he admitted to himself, was theft, plain and simple, and he was sorry for it. It sat on his conscience in a way nothing else did. He could picture the farmer, and the honey, and the fact of having taken what was not his.

Having confessed that much, he felt a good deal lighter, and he went on to consider the rest.

The rest, he decided, did not amount to guilt. People might call him a fraud, but the word did not fit. A fraud, as he understood it, was a man who set out to enrich himself at other people's expense. He had done nothing of the kind. He had not grown rich. He had not tried to grow rich. What he had wanted was simply to live: a meal, a roof, enough to keep going from one day to the next. That was subsistence, not profit, and he could not see how a man could be blamed for wanting to stay alive.

He examined this argument from several sides and found it sound. The people had received their sermons; he had received his supper. Nobody had been ruined. If there was a balance to be struck, it seemed to him fairly even.

So he walked on through the night with his conscience in reasonable order. There was the matter of the honey, which he regretted. Beyond that, he could find nothing he needed to repent of, and he did not look very hard.
