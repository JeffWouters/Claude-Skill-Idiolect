## The passage (by the author)

There is a curious psychology to all this. A deadline that is widely understood to be arbitrary can nevertheless exert enormous force, because once it has been announced it acquires a reputational dimension; missing it would mean admitting that the original plan was wrong, and the people who made the plan may be more invested in its correctness than in its outcome. You have perhaps seen this dynamic play out, in which a date that everyone privately believes is unrealistic is publicly defended until the last possible moment, at which point it is quietly moved and the whole exercise begins again. The consequence is a kind of institutional theatre, in which the deadline functions less as a planning tool than as a performance of confidence. I suspect that most participants in this theatre understand its nature perfectly well; they simply feel unable to be the one who stops the performance.

Is there a better way to relate to deadlines? It seems to me that the most important step is simply to be honest about their origins—to ask, whenever a date is proposed, what would actually happen if it were missed, and to allow the answer to shape how seriously the date is treated. A deadline tied to an unmovable external event deserves one kind of response; a deadline chosen because it seemed like a reasonable target deserves another, and conflating the two does a disservice to everyone involved. Perhaps we might also learn to renegotiate dates earlier and more openly, treating a revised deadline not as a failure of planning but as the natural result of learning more about the work. None of this is technically difficult, and none of it requires new tools or elaborate methods. What makes it difficult is the reluctance to disappoint, which is, in some sense, a reluctance to be honest.

## Draft A

What happens to a date once it has been announced? Before anyone says it aloud, an arbitrary due date is little more than a convenient point on a calendar, often chosen for reasons that have nothing to do with the work itself. It may be that the moment of announcement changes its nature entirely, because a date that others have heard becomes attached to the reputation of whoever named it, and people will defend their reputations far more stubbornly than they defend any plan.

This is why unrealistic dates are so rarely abandoned at the point when they ought to be, even by people who privately doubt them. The person who announced the date, and arguably everyone who repeated it, now has a stake in its survival; to let it slip is to admit, in some sense, that a public judgement was mistaken, and that the confidence with which it was delivered was never quite deserved. You might have watched a team work evenings and weekends to protect a date that, on reflection, mattered to almost nobody.

It seems to me that the most useful question to ask of any such date is what missing it would really cost. Sometimes the answer is considerable—a contractual obligation, a dependent team, a launch that cannot move—and the date deserves the effort spent defending it. More often, I suspect, the honest answer is that very little would happen at all, beyond a few awkward conversations and a revised line in a plan. Once that is clear, the case for renegotiating early becomes difficult to resist, since a date moved weeks in advance disappoints far fewer people than one missed on the day. The obstacle is rarely practical; it is the fear of letting others down, which is an entirely understandable reason for holding on, though not a particularly good one. Perhaps the date worth keeping is not the one we announced but the one we can still honestly stand behind.

## Draft B

Due dates often begin life as something close to a guess. Someone needs a date for a plan, a date is offered, and the conversation moves on. What happens next is curious. Once the date has been announced, and particularly once it has been repeated in a meeting or written into a slide, it ceases to be a guess and becomes something more like a reputation. The person who named it is now associated with it, and to miss it would be to be seen as unreliable. So people find themselves defending dates they never quite believed in, long after the evidence has turned against them, because the date has become a question of standing rather than of scheduling.

A useful discipline, when a date begins to look unrealistic, is to ask plainly what missing it would really cost. Sometimes the answer is significant: a contractual obligation, a dependency in another team, [example needed]. Surprisingly often, though, the honest answer is that very little would happen at all, beyond a certain awkwardness and the need to explain. The date was arbitrary when it was set, and it remains arbitrary now; only the social weight around it has grown. Naming the actual consequence, rather than the imagined one, tends to shrink the problem to its proper size.

Where a date does need to move, there is a strong case for renegotiating it early. A week's notice gives others room to adjust their own plans; a day's notice merely transfers the crisis to someone else. Most people understand this perfectly well, and yet the conversation is deferred again and again. The obstacle, I suspect, is rarely a lack of information. It is the fear of disappointing others—of watching a manager's face fall, or of admitting that one's earlier confidence was misplaced. That fear is entirely human, and no process will abolish it. But it is worth recognising that the disappointment of an early renegotiation is almost always smaller than the disappointment of a late one.

## Draft C

A date gets mentioned in a meeting. Maybe someone picked it because it was the end of a quarter, or because it sounded reasonable at the time. Nobody calculated it very carefully. But once it has been announced, something shifts. The date is no longer just a planning guess; it has become part of someone's reputation.

That is why people defend unrealistic dates long after they know better. Missing a date you announced feels like being caught out, as if you had made a promise and broken it. So instead of saying the date no longer holds, people work longer hours, cut corners they would rather not cut, or simply stay quiet and hope. The date was arbitrary to begin with, but the social stakes around it are very real.

A useful question cuts through this: what would missing the date actually cost? Sometimes the answer is significant, a contractual obligation or an external event that won't move. Often, though, the honest answer is that very little depends on that exact day. [example needed] When a team asks the question directly, it frequently discovers that it has been protecting a number rather than an outcome.

If the date really does need to change, the best time to say so is early. Renegotiating while there is still room to adjust plans is far easier for everyone than announcing a slip at the last minute. Most people know this. The obstacle isn't a lack of information; it is the fear of disappointing others, of being the one who delivers bad news.

Recognising that fear for what it is helps. Raising a problem early is not a failure of commitment. It is the most useful thing you can do for the people depending on you, because it gives them time to respond while they still can.

## Draft D

There is a particular kind of meeting in which everyone knows the date is not going to be met and nobody says so. The date was announced some weeks earlier, perhaps casually, perhaps to reassure someone senior, and since then it has acquired a weight quite out of proportion to the thinking that produced it. It may be that the difficulty with arbitrary due dates is less about planning than about reputation.

Once a date has been said aloud, it stops being an estimate and becomes something closer to a public statement about the person who made it. Missing it feels like being found out. People will therefore defend dates they no longer believe in, working longer hours, trimming quality where nobody will immediately notice, and hoping the gap closes of its own accord. It rarely does.

It seems to me that the most useful question in such a situation is a very plain one: what would it actually cost to miss this date? Sometimes the answer is considerable, because something outside the team genuinely depends on it. More commonly, I suspect, very little would happen beyond some mild disappointment and a revised plan. Is it any wonder that the question is so seldom asked, when asking it means admitting that the date was never as fixed as everyone behaved as though it was?

The sensible course is to renegotiate early, while there is still time to change scope or expectations without drama. The obstacle is rarely practical. It is, in the end, the fear of disappointing others, and of being the first to say what everyone else already knows.

Perhaps the date was never the real commitment; the real commitment was to tell each other the truth about it.
