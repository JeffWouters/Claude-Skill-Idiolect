# Judge packet synthetic-noor-07

## The passage (by the author)

It came loose on the third try.

And there was the washer. Black rubber, once. Now it was grey and hard and split on one edge like a dry lip. No wonder. It could not seal against anything.

I took it to the shop on the corner. The man there has a drawer of washers sorted by size. He held mine up to the light. Then he picked one out without looking. Half inch. 30 cents. I bought three.

At home the new washer went on with a small nut. I tightened it by hand, then a quarter turn with pliers. Not more. Rubber needs room to press.

Then back in with the headgear. Snug, then a little more. I wiped the threads first. Grit in the threads is how you strip them.

Handle on. Screw in. Blue cap pressed home with my thumb.

So then the valve under the sink. I turned it slowly. You want the pipes to fill without a bang. There was a hiss. Then a knock somewhere in the wall. Then quiet.

I opened the cold tap. It spat twice and ran clean. I closed it.

And I waited.

I stood at the sink and watched the spout for a full minute. Nothing. I went and made a coffee and came back. Nothing.

The whole job took 40 minutes. Most of it was the stop valve and the walk to the shop. The actual work was ten minutes. That is how it goes with small repairs. The work is short. The getting ready is long.

I put the two spare washers in a jar marked TAP. The jar lives in the drawer by the back door. It holds the old one too. I keep the old one so I remember the size.

That night the kitchen was very quiet. I noticed it when I switched off the light. No tick. No small wet sound in the dark.

## Draft A

The dripping tap had been going for a while before I finally dealt with it, and the fix itself turned out to be a small one: a worn rubber seal that no longer closed the flow properly. Swapping it was the easy part. Everything around it took longer.

I bought the replacement seals from a local hardware shop. They were cheap, which is part of why a dripping tap is so annoying to put off. The part that solves the problem costs very little [price needed]. I bought more than one, on purpose, and I will come back to why.

Most of the job was preparation. Shutting off the supply, clearing the space under the sink, laying out the tools, putting a cloth in the basin so nothing small disappears down the drain, and working out how the tap actually comes apart before taking it apart. The repair itself, pulling out the old seal and fitting the new one, took a fraction of that time. I have come to accept that this is normal. With small repairs, preparation is the job and the fix is the last few minutes of it.

Reassembly deserves care. Each part goes back in the order it came out, seated properly, tightened firmly but not forced. It is easy to rush this bit because the problem feels solved, but a part put back crooked will leak just as surely as a worn seal.

Then the supply goes back on, and it goes back on slowly. Opening the valve all at once sends a rush of water and pressure through a tap you have just rebuilt, and if anything is not quite seated, that is when you find out. Turning it gradually gives you time to watch, listen and stop if needed. In my case the tap held, and the drip was gone.

The spare seals are now stored away for later. Next time a tap starts to drip, the part will already be in the drawer, and the preparation will be a little shorter.

## Draft B

The tap dripped for [number needed: how long]. I put it off. Then I could not sleep for it.

So in the end I fixed it. The fix itself is a worn rubber washer. Not the tap. Just the washer, a small rubber ring.

First the shop. The local hardware shop sells them loose, for very little. [number needed: price]. I bought a handful in different sizes, because I did not know which one I needed. That turned out to be right.

Then the preparation. This is the long part. I turned off the supply under the sink and opened the tap to drain what was left in the pipe. I put the plug in the sink so nothing small could go down it. I laid a tea towel on the draining board. Every part goes on the towel in the order it comes off. Here is the order. Cap. Screw. Handle. Headgear nut. Then the valve, with the old washer on the end of it.

The old washer was flat and hard and split at one edge. Not worn. Worn out. I pressed on a new one and it sat snug straight away.

Then everything back on, in the reverse order along the towel. Slowly. Hand tight on the nut, then a little more with the spanner. Not a lot. Too much and you crush the new washer before it has done a day's work.

Then the supply. I opened the valve under the sink a little at a time. I turned the tap on. Water. I turned it off. Nothing. I stood and watched the spout for a while anyway.

The whole repair took less time than getting ready for it. That is usually how it goes. The next one will be quicker. I kept the spare washers. They are in the drawer under the sink.

Tonight the sink is dry. The tea towel is back on its hook.

## Draft D

The washer was the whole problem. A small rubber ring. It had worn out. That is why the tap dripped. Water got past it every time I turned it off. It took me longer to find that out than to fix it.

I took the old one to the hardware shop down the road. I always take the old part. It saves guessing. The man at the counter found a match in a drawer. It cost [price needed]. Almost nothing. So I bought a bag of them. And a spare of the other ring, the thin one on the spindle. They cost [price needed] as well.

Most of the job was the getting ready. That is always true with taps. Turn off the water under the sink. Open the tap to drain what is left in the pipe. Put the plug in so nothing small goes down it. Lay a towel in the sink. Lay the parts out on it in order, as they come off. That took longer than the fix itself. I did not mind. It is the part that stops you making a mess.

The new washer went on in a moment. Then the reassembly. Slowly. Each part back in the order it came off. The towel helps here. I did not tighten anything hard. Snug is enough. Brass is soft. If you crank it down, you crush the new rubber and you are back where you started.

Then the water. Not all at once. I opened the valve under the sink a little at a time. You hear the pipe fill. There is a knock, then a hiss, then quiet. I left the tap open until the water ran clean and smooth. No spitting. Then I closed it. And I watched it.

Nothing. No drip. I watched it for a while longer to be sure. Still nothing.

The rest of the bag is in the drawer by the sink. With a note of which tap it fits. The next one will not take a trip to the shop.
