# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-08

## The passage (by the author)

Great was the joy in the community when one day Four-Eyed Nick announced that he had struck pay ore and that he was about to celebrate his good fortune by getting married. Every one was intensely interested. The newspapers made an especial feature of the approaching marriage in high life. Nick was dazed by the sudden glare of publicity. Who should be invited? His generous heart rebelled against any discrimination, and he solved his problem by saying, "Come one! Come all!" He engaged every vehicle in the town to be at the disposal of such of his fellow citizens as would honor him with their presence at his nuptials.

It would have delighted the heart of Chaucer to have seen the procession of wedding guests wending their way over the ten miles of abominable mountain road to Nick's cabin. Not on the road to Canterbury was there more variety or more hearty good fellowship. Nick had invited the town, and the town was bent on showing its appreciation of the compliment. The mayor and members of the city council, the lawyers, editors, doctors, clergymen, gamblers, mining experts, saloon-keepers, and honest miners all joined heartily in doing honor to one whom they, for the moment, agreed to consider their most distinguished fellow citizen.

No one could remain long in assured obscurity. It pleased the community to turn its search-light now upon one member and now upon another, and give him a brief experience of living in the public eye. Greatness of one sort or another was sure to be thrust upon one in the course of the year. The choicest spirits of the town were always collaborating in some work of high-grade fiction, and were on the lookout for interesting material. It would have been churlish for any one when his turn came to have refused to be a notability.

## Draft A

When a prospector strikes it rich, the whole camp feels that it has a share in his good fortune. This is not because he is expected to divide his ore. It is because, for a little while, he belongs to everybody.

There was once a lucky man who, having found his rich vein, decided to be married. He did not propose to be married quietly. He invited the entire town, and to make sure that no one should have an excuse for staying away, he hired every carriage that could be had.

The procession that set out for his cabin was a remarkable one. The cabin was a long way off, in a remote spot where carriages were not in the habit of going. In the first carriage, perhaps, rode the officials of the town, conscious of their dignity. Behind them came the gamblers, who were equally conscious of theirs. The clergy were there, and the miners, and they all rode together over the same rough road and were shaken by the same ruts. It was the most democratic assembly the town had ever seen. Nobody asked who was respectable. Everybody had been invited.

I do not know that the bride and groom were the chief interest of the day. The chief interest was that the whole town was going somewhere together.

But this, I think, was the peculiar genius of such a place. It had no settled aristocracy, and so it made one as it went along. It took turns. To-day it was the lucky prospector who was famous; to-morrow it would be some one else. Each resident, in the course of things, had his brief moment when the town was talking about him. Fame, in older communities, is a jealous possession, and those who have it are careful to keep it in the family. In the mining town it was passed around like a hat.

## Draft B

When a prospector strikes rich ore, the whole camp feels that it has been let into the secret of Providence. It was so with [name needed], who came upon a vein that made him a man of consequence overnight. He did what a man of consequence ought to do in a mining town. He got married, and he invited everybody.

I do not mean that he invited his friends. That would have been a narrow and exclusive proceeding, unworthy of the occasion. He invited the town, and to make sure that the town could come, he hired every carriage in it. The ceremony was to be held at his cabin, which lay far out from the settlement, and the road to it had not been built with processions in mind.

It was a remarkable procession. The officials of the county rode side by side with the gamblers, and the clergy found themselves sharing a seat with miners who were perhaps more at home in a saloon. Nobody minded. For one day Respectability and Disreputability agreed to travel together, and each found the other better company than rumour had led it to expect. The carriages jolted over the ruts, and the company, which had never before been assembled in one place, rather enjoyed the novelty of it.

But the true meaning of the day was not in the wedding. It was in the town's sense of justice. A mining settlement has no aristocracy, and it cannot afford one; it has instead a rotation of celebrity. Each resident in turn is lifted up and made famous, for a week or a day, and then set down again so that his neighbour may have his share. The prospector's turn had come. He enjoyed it thoroughly, and so did everyone else, knowing that their own would come in due course.

## Draft C

In a mining town, fame is not a thing to be hoarded by a few. It is common property, and the town has a generous way of passing it round, so that every resident, sooner or later, has his turn. It may be for a day or a week; it is seldom longer. But while it lasts it is complete.

Consider the turn that came to [name needed]. He was a prospector of no particular distinction until he struck rich ore. The next day he was the most important man in the camp. He had decided to be married, and he was determined that the whole town should share his happiness. So he invited everybody. Not content with that, he hired every carriage the place afforded, so that nobody should have the excuse of staying at home for want of a conveyance.

The wedding was to be at his cabin, which lay at a considerable distance from the town, in a spot better adapted to finding ore than to holding a wedding. Thither we set out in a long procession. I have seen many processions, but never one so representative. The officials of the town rode side by side with the gamblers, and the clergy were packed in with the miners. It was not a time for social distinctions. Everybody was a guest, and a guest of a man who had just struck it rich is on a level with every other guest.

On the long road there was abundant opportunity for conversation, and the conversation was of the most democratic kind. The official and the gambler found that they had a good deal in common, and the clergyman was not above learning something from the miner at his elbow.

As for the bridegroom, he bore his honors modestly. He knew, as we all knew, that his fame was a loan from the town, and that next week it would be somebody else's turn.

## Draft D

When the prospector struck rich ore, he did what a lucky man in a mining town was expected to do. He decided to get married, and he decided that the whole town should come.

He did not send out a few careful invitations. He invited everyone. Then, so that nobody would have an excuse to stay home, he hired every carriage in the place. Every buggy, wagon and hack that could be pulled by a horse was engaged for the day, and the town set out together for his remote cabin in the hills. [example needed]

It was a strange procession. The officials of the town rode in it, dressed for the occasion and conscious of their dignity. The gamblers rode in it too, in their best waistcoats, perhaps sharing a seat with the very men who had tried to close their tables. The clergy were there, along with the miners who made up most of the population and who had never before been invited anywhere in a hired carriage. For a few hours, the distinctions that mattered so much in town counted for very little on the road. Everyone was a guest, and everyone was going the same way.

What made the day possible was not only the prospector's generosity but the character of the town itself. A mining settlement had its own way of handing out fame. It took turns. One week the talk was all about a man who had found a good claim, the next about someone who had lost everything at cards, the next about a quarrel or a fire or a marriage. Each resident, sooner or later, had his moment at the centre of attention, and then the town moved on to someone else.

The prospector's wedding was his turn. He had the whole town at his door, in every carriage it owned, and for that day he was the most famous man in the district. By the next week, the town would have found someone new.
