# Judge packet synthetic-idris-01

## The passage (by the author)

Consider the way a meeting begins its life. Usually it is created in response to a genuine difficulty—a project that has lost its sense of direction, a dependency between teams that nobody quite owns, a decision that keeps being deferred—and in those first weeks it is arguably the most valuable hour anyone spends. The people in the room have a shared problem; they bring information that cannot easily be written down; they leave with a clearer sense of who is doing what. The difficulty is that the problem eventually resolves, or changes shape, while the invitation remains exactly as it was, reproducing itself week after week with the indifference of any automated process. Does anyone notice the moment at which the meeting stops serving its purpose? In my experience the transition is almost always invisible, since the attendees are still talking, the notes are still being taken, and the calendar still insists that this is where we ought to be.

It would be easy to conclude from this that recurring meetings should simply be abolished, and there are teams that have tried precisely that experiment with a certain evangelical enthusiasm. I am not convinced that the conclusion follows. What the meeting provides, even in its degraded form, is a guaranteed occasion for contact—a moment when you can raise something half-formed without having to justify interrupting anyone, when the quieter members of a team can be seen and heard, when small frictions surface before they harden into grievances. Perhaps the ritual persists precisely because it performs this function, however inefficiently, and because nobody has designed a better substitute. In some sense the meeting is less a tool for making decisions than a piece of social infrastructure; its value is not visible in any particular session but in the accumulated familiarity it produces over months. Remove it without replacing that function, and we may discover that the team has become efficient in a way that is also, subtly, lonelier.

## Draft A

Most standing weekly meetings begin with a perfectly good reason. There is a launch to coordinate, perhaps, or a problem that keeps slipping between two teams, or a new group of people who have not yet learned how to work together, and someone sensibly proposes that everyone gather at the same hour each week until the matter is settled. For a while the meeting earns its place. Then the launch ships, the problem is solved, the people learn one another's habits, and the meeting carries on regardless, because nothing in the calendar knows that its purpose has quietly departed. What is striking is how rarely anyone notices the moment of transition. The agenda thins, the updates grow shorter and more ritual, attendance becomes a little less complete, and yet the invitation recurs with the serene confidence of something that has always been there.

The obvious response, once the drift is noticed, is to cancel the lot. There is a certain satisfaction in clearing a calendar, and a certain righteousness in declaring that time is too valuable to be spent on meetings nobody can justify. I suspect, though, that this impulse deserves more caution than it usually receives. A meeting that has outlived its original reason has not necessarily outlived all reasons; it may simply have acquired new ones that nobody thought to name. The weekly gathering is, for many teams, one of the few fixed points at which people who otherwise work apart actually see and hear one another. It supplies a rhythm of regular contact, an occasion for the small exchanges that build familiarity, and a sense, however faint, of belonging to something shared. None of this appears on the agenda, and none of it would be missed in the first week after cancellation. It might be missed a great deal in the months that followed, when people discovered that they no longer quite knew their colleagues and could not say why. The question worth asking, then, is not whether the meeting still does what it was created to do, but what it has come to do instead, and whether that is something we would want to lose.

## Draft B

Most standing weekly meetings begin with a good reason. A project is at a delicate stage, two teams keep stepping on each other, a new process needs watching. Someone books an hour every Tuesday, and for a while that hour earns its place. Decisions get made, problems surface early, people leave knowing more than when they arrived.

Then the reason fades. The project ships, the teams settle into a working rhythm, the process becomes routine. The meeting does not notice. It stays in the calendar because it is already there, and because nobody owns the question of whether it should be. The agenda thins into status updates that could have been a message. Attendance slips. People bring laptops and half-listen. The meeting has outlived the problem it was built to solve, and the drift happened so gradually that no single week felt like the moment to stop.

The obvious response is to clear the calendar. Cancel every recurring meeting, start from zero, and let only the ones that people miss come back. It sounds bracing and efficient, and there is something satisfying about the idea. But it carries a risk that is easy to overlook, because a meeting that has lost its official purpose may still be doing something useful that nobody named.

A standing meeting is often the only guaranteed contact some team members have with each other. It is where the quiet colleague gets a regular chance to speak, where someone mentions in passing that they are stuck, where a new joiner learns who does what. It is a small, reliable ritual that holds a group together, the kind of thing that only becomes visible once it disappears. [example needed]

None of that means every weekly meeting deserves to survive. It means that the stated reason and the real value can come apart. Before scrapping a meeting that no longer does what it was designed for, it is worth asking what it has quietly started doing instead, and whether anything else would do that job.

## Draft C

Why does a weekly meeting exist? You may have sat through one recently and found yourself unable to answer, not because the meeting was bad, exactly, but because nobody present could remember what it was for. It may be that most standing meetings are born useful and simply outlive the circumstance that called them into being.

The pattern is familiar enough. A meeting begins in response to something: [example needed: the trigger that started a standing meeting]. For a while it earns its place, because the problem it addresses is live and everyone in the room can see it. Then the trigger fades. The problem is solved, or absorbed into ordinary work, or quietly forgotten, and the meeting carries on regardless, since nothing about its calendar entry records why it was created. The drift goes unnoticed, because it happens gradually and because attending has become a habit rather than a decision.

The obvious response is to scrap such meetings wholesale, and there is considerable satisfaction in imagining the empty calendar that would follow. I suspect this is riskier than it looks. Meetings that have lost their original purpose do not always lose their function; they may have acquired another one along the way, without anyone deciding that they should.

There is also the matter of contact. A standing meeting, whatever its agenda, guarantees that a group of people will see one another at a regular interval. It supplies, in some sense, a rhythm to working together, and it offers occasions for the small exchanges from which a team's sense of itself is built. None of this appears in the meeting's stated purpose, and so none of it is counted when the meeting is judged. Is it any wonder that a team which cancels its standing meetings may find, some months later, that its members know one another rather less well?

The weekly meeting is, in the end, one of the few fixtures of working life that persists without anyone having to argue for it. Perhaps that is its weakness and its value at once: it survives not because it is needed but because it is there, and sometimes being there was the need all along.

## Draft D

Why do so many teams still gather every week for a meeting that nobody can quite explain? It may be that the answer lies less in any decision than in the absence of one, since most standing meetings begin with a clear purpose and simply continue after that purpose has faded. A project needed coordinating, or a problem needed watching, and a weekly slot seemed the sensible way to handle it; the project ended or the problem settled, but the slot remained in the calendar, and the habit of attending outlived any memory of why it had been created. You may have sat in such a meeting yourself, dutifully working through an agenda that no longer corresponds to anything the team is actually doing.

There is also a temptation, once this drift has been noticed, to sweep such meetings away wholesale and reclaim the hours they consume. The impulse is understandable, and arguably it is sometimes justified, but I suspect it carries a risk that is easy to overlook. Standing meetings frequently do something that appears on no agenda: they supply a regular point of contact, a predictable occasion on which colleagues see one another and exchange the small observations that would otherwise go unsaid. A team that loses this may not notice the loss for some time; it may simply find, months later, that people know rather less about the work of their colleagues than they used to, and that the bonds which made collaboration easy have quietly weakened.

The meeting that has outlived its reason, in the end, may still be serving a purpose that nobody thought to name when it was first set up. That does not mean it should continue unexamined, since a meeting kept alive only by inertia is a poor way of meeting a need that could be met more deliberately. Perhaps the question worth asking is not whether a meeting still does what it was designed to do but whether it does something the team would miss.
