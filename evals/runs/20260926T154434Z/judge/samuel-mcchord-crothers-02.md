# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-02

## The passage (by the author)

So it was in a community which I fondly remember. It was not much to look at, this brand-new Nevada mining town. The main street swaggered up the gulch in a devil-may-care fashion, as if saying to the teamsters, "You may take me or leave me." To the north it pointed to an alkali flat, and to the south to a dusty old mountain, which was immensely richer than it seemed. On the mountain side were hoisting works and hundreds of prospect holes which menaced the lives of the unwary. In the gulch were smelters which belched forth divers kinds of fumes. To the stranger they seemed to threaten wholesale asphyxiation, but to the citizen they gave the place the character of a health resort. An analysis of the air showed that it contained more chemicals than were to be found in the most famous mineral springs. Certain it was that there were enough to kill off all germs of contagious diseases. The community felt the need of no further hygienic precautions, and put its trust in its daily fumigations. No green thing was in sight, not so much as a grass blade, for the fumes were not only germicides, but also herbicides. On the main street were saloons and gambling houses, in close proximity to two or three struggling churches. There were two daily newspapers, each of which kept us informed of the other's manifold iniquities. A narrow-gauge railroad had its terminus at the foot of the gulch. Once a day a mixed train would depart for the world that lay beyond the alkali flat. Some of the passengers would be "going below," which meant nothing worse than a trip to California; others were promoters going East on missions of mercy to benighted capitalists. The promoter was our nearest approach to a professional philanthropist. As for the rest, the chief impression was of dust. It would roll in great billows down the gulch; it seemed as if the mountains had been pulverized. Then the wind would change and the dust billows would roll back. No matter how long it blew, there was always more where it came from.

## Draft A

I remember with a certain affection a new silver camp in Nevada which in those days considered itself a city and was prepared to argue the point. It had one street, and that street had to accommodate everything the community required for its material and spiritual welfare.

The smelter stood at the edge of town and poured out its smoke with a liberality that nobody thought of resenting. On the contrary, it was generally regarded as a public benefactor. The citizens held, with the confidence of men who had never consulted a physician on the subject, that the fumes were a powerful disinfectant, and that no germ could live in such an atmosphere. It is likely they were right, for nothing else could live in it either. There was not a blade of grass, a shrub or a geranium within reach of that smoke. The inhabitants accepted the loss of vegetation as the price of health, and considered the bargain an excellent one.

Along the street the institutions of civilization were arranged in their customary proportions. There were bars, and there were gaming halls, and between them, somewhat apologetically, stood a few feeble churches that seemed to have wandered in by mistake. There were also two newspapers, which were not content merely to report the news but made a point of supplying it, chiefly by denouncing each other. Their feud was the one enterprise in town that never showed signs of exhaustion.

A small train connected the camp with the outer world. It came and went with a leisurely air, as though it had no great confidence that anybody would wish to leave, and some doubt whether anybody ought to arrive.

But the true sovereign of the place was the dust. It rose with every wagon and settled on every surface; it seasoned the food, it thickened the whiskey, and it gave the sermons in the feeble churches a gritty quality they might otherwise have lacked. One did not live in the camp so much as under it. Yet I think of it kindly. It had no illusions about itself, except the illusion that it was going to last.

## Draft B

I have a tender recollection of a new silver camp in Nevada, where I once lived for a while. [name of camp needed] was not a beautiful town, and it made no pretence of being one. It had the frankness of youth. It did not ask to be admired; it asked only to be allowed to grow up.

The smelter stood at one end of the camp and poured out its smoke day and night. A stranger might have thought this a misfortune, but the inhabitants knew better. The smoke, they told me, was the best disinfectant in the world. No germ could live in it. It was a matter of civic pride that the place was so healthy. It was true that nothing green could live in it either. There was not a blade of grass or a flower to be seen, for the smoke killed every plant that ventured within reach. But that was only the price of health, and nobody thought it too high.

There was one street, and it held everything. The saloons were there, and the gaming halls, and they did a prosperous business. The churches were there also, but they were feeble folk, and seemed to exist on sufferance. It was hard for them to make themselves heard above the rattle of the chips. There were two newspapers, and they kept up a feud with a vigor that would have done credit to rival dynasties. What one editor affirmed the other denied, and each gave the impression that the future of civilization hung upon the outcome. The subscribers, I think, read both and enjoyed the quarrel.

A little train connected the camp with the outside world. It was not a great railroad, but it was a comfort to know that it was there. It was the thread which bound us to the rest of mankind, and when it came in there was always a crowd to meet it.

But the thing I remember best is the dust. It was the chief fact of daily life. It was in the air and on the table and in one's clothes and in one's thoughts. One did not fight against it; one accepted it, as the people accepted the smoke, as a part of the order of things.

## Draft C

I remember that camp with more affection than it probably deserved. It was new then, raw and hopeful, one of those Nevada silver towns that seemed to spring up overnight wherever someone had found ore worth digging.

The first thing anyone noticed was the smoke. The smelter sent it up day and night, and it drifted over everything. You might suppose people would complain about it, but they did the opposite. The local opinion was that the smoke was healthy. It was a disinfectant, people said, and it kept sickness away. I never heard anyone seriously doubt it. The fact that it killed every plant in the place did not seem to trouble the theory. There was not a green leaf to be found within reach of it, and this was taken, if anything, as a sign of how thoroughly it cleaned the air.

The town was mostly one street, and that street held nearly everything that mattered. There were bars, a good many of them, and gaming halls beside them, and both did steady business. There were churches too, though feeble ones; they had a hard time competing with their neighbours for attention, and they knew it. And there were two newspapers, which spent a great deal of their energy attacking each other. Their quarrel was one of the regular entertainments of the place. [example needed]

A small train connected the camp with the rest of the world. It was not much of a railway, but it mattered a great deal, because it was the one line running outward. People and supplies came in on it, silver went out on it, and news of whatever was happening elsewhere arrived with it.

What I remember most, though, is the dust. It was the real ruler of daily life. It got into clothes, food, beds and conversation. You walked through it, breathed it, and brushed it off, and a few minutes later it was back. Nobody fought it for long; you simply lived with it, as you lived with the smoke.

It was not a beautiful place, and it was not a respectable one. But it was young and busy and full of expectation, and I think of it fondly still.

## Draft D

There is a kind of affection which attaches itself only to places that have no claim upon it. I have it for a new silver camp in Nevada. It was not beautiful, it was not healthy, and it was not, by any standard I had been taught, respectable. I think of it with more tenderness than of many towns that were all three.

The smelter stood over the camp and poured out its smoke day and night. A stranger might have supposed this a misfortune. The inhabitants knew better. The smoke, they told you, was the finest disinfectant in the world; no germ could live in it. This was undoubtedly true. Nothing else could live in it either. There was not a green thing within reach of the fumes, and the hills stood about as bare as the day they were made. But the people were well, or said they were, and in a new camp what a man says is a large part of the evidence.

All the life of the place was gathered along one street. There were the saloons, which were many, and the gaming halls, which were prosperous. There were the churches, which were feeble, and which carried on their work with the patient air of missionaries among a people who had not yet decided whether they wished to be saved. And there were two newspapers, which were at war. Each regarded the other as the chief obstacle to the advancement of the camp, and each said so in every issue. The feud gave the town a sense of public life. A community which can support two editors who despise each other is not without resources.

A small train connected us with the outer world. It came and went with a certain informality, and its arrival was an event. It brought letters, and supplies, and now and then a newcomer who looked about him with visible misgiving.

Over everything lay the dust. It was the one citizen who never left. It was in the food, in the bedding, in the ledgers and in the sermons. We did not fight it; we had come to terms with it, as one comes to terms with a relative.

I do not know that I should wish to live there again. But I should not wish to have missed it.
