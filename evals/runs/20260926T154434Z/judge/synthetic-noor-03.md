# Judge packet synthetic-noor-03

## The passage (by the author)

The kitchen tap started to drip in March. One drop every four seconds. I timed it with the clock on the oven.

At first I did nothing. A drip is easy to live with. You stop hearing it after a day or two.

But then I put a jug under it. By morning the jug held 600 millilitres. So that was the end of living with it.

I have fixed taps before. Not this one. This one is old, with a brass body and two cross-shaped handles. Hot on the left. Cold on the right. The drip came from the cold side.

So on Saturday I cleared the counter. I laid a tea towel in the sink. That is the first rule: cover the plughole. Small parts fall. They always fall.

Then I turned off the water. There is a stop valve under the sink, behind the bleach and a bag of old sponges. It had not been turned in years. It took both hands and a cloth for grip. Then it gave.

I opened the tap to drain it. A cough of air. A thin run of water. Then nothing.

The handle came off with one screw under a small blue cap. I lifted the cap with the tip of a knife. The screw was a flat head, soft with age. I used the right driver and pushed hard as I turned. It came out whole.

Under the handle sat the headgear. That is the brass part that holds the washer. It takes a spanner. Mine is an adjustable one, 20 centimetres long, from my father's box. I set it snug on the flats and held the tap body with my other hand. You have to hold the body. Or the whole tap turns in the counter and you make a new problem.

## Draft A

The kitchen tap had been dripping for months. It is an old brass one. I had stopped hearing it. So I measured it. I put a jug under the spout overnight. In the morning there was [amount] of water in it. That is a lot of water for a small drip. Once I had the number I could not ignore it. I had to fix the tap.

First, the drain. Put the plug in. Then lay a cloth over the plug. Small parts come out of a tap. Screws, washers, a tiny spring. If one drops, it goes straight for the plughole. The plug stops it. The cloth stops it bouncing. I learned that the hard way. [example needed]

Then the water. There are two small valves under the sink. One for hot, one for cold. Turn them both off. Clockwise. They are stiff if nobody has touched them for years. Mine were. Then open the tap. Both sides. Let it run until it stops. There is always more water in the line than you think. Leave the tap open. Now the line is empty and nothing will spray at you when you take it apart.

Now the handle. There is a small cap on top. Prise it off with a thin blade. Under it is a screw. Undo it and lift the handle off. It may be tight. Wiggle it. Do not lever it against the body. Under the handle is the inner assembly. It has a big nut at the base. That nut needs a spanner. And here is the important part. Hold the body of the tap with your other hand while you turn the nut. Or with a second spanner. Old brass is soft. If you do not brace it, the whole tap turns on the sink. Then you have a second leak. Brace it, then turn. The nut gives. The assembly lifts out.

## Draft B

The old brass faucet in our kitchen had been dripping for a while before I did anything about it. A slow drip is easy to ignore. You stop hearing it. What finally changed my mind was measuring it. I put a container under the spout and left it for [example needed: how long], and came back to [example needed: how much water]. Once I had a number, I could not pretend any more. That was real water, going straight down the drain, every day.

So I started the repair, and most of the work turned out to be preparation.

First, the drain. The parts inside a faucet like this are small, and the kitchen sink drain is exactly the right size to swallow one of them. I plugged it and laid a cloth in the bottom of the basin, so that anything I dropped would land softly and stay put.

Next, the water supply. I shut off the valves under the sink so no water could reach the faucet while it was open. Then I opened the tap to empty the line. Whatever water was still sitting in the pipe ran out into the sink rather than into my face once the faucet came apart.

Only then did I start taking things off. The handle came first. [example needed: how the handle was fastened and removed]. Under it sat the inner assembly, the part that actually controls the flow and the likely source of the leak.

This is where the old brass needed care. Loosening the assembly means turning something that has been sitting tight for years, and all that force goes into the faucet body as well. If the body twists, it can strain the connections underneath or damage the fitting. So I braced the body with one hand, or with a second tool, and turned the assembly with the other, keeping the body still while the inside came loose.

With the assembly out, I could finally see what was wrong.

The kitchen tap drips. It has done that for [number needed, how long]. I told myself it was nothing. Then I measured it.

Then I opened the tap to empty the line. It ran, then it spat, then it went quiet. I left it open.

Only then did I start on the tap itself. The handle came first. [example needed, how the handle is fixed on]. It came off in the end.

Under the handle is the inner assembly. That is the part that does the work. It screws into the body. This is where you can do harm. The body is fixed to the sink. If you turn the insides without holding the body, the whole tap turns with it. Then you have a second job.

So I braced the body with one hand. I turned the insides with a tool in the other. It was tighter than you think. Then it gave.

Tonight the insides lie by the sink, on a clean cloth. The other cloth is still in the drain. The jug is still under the spout. It is dry.
