# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-04

## The passage (by the author)

But hold! "William Edwards," says a newspaper notice, "who used to drive a post stage between New York and Albany, died on Saturday at his home. He was born in Albany," and so and so, "and many were the stories he had to tell of incidents connected with the famous men who were his passengers." Even so. We were ourselves a clerk. That is, for a number of years we waited on customers in a celebrated book shop. This is one of the stories we have to tell of the personages who were, so to say, our passengers. Or perhaps we are more in the nature of those unscrupulous English footmen to high society, of whom we have heard, who "sell out" their observation and information to the society press.

Anyhow, we are of a loquacious, gossipy turn; and we were booksellers, so to speak, to crowned heads. We have recently heard, too, of another precedent to our garrulous performance, the publication in Rome of the memoirs of an old waiter, who carefully set down the relative liberality of prominent persons whom he served. After having served Cardinals Rampolla and Merry del Val, this excellent memoirist entered opposite their names, "Both no good." With this we drop the defensive.

We noticed Mr. Wharton sitting down, legs crossed, smoking a cigar. Awaiting, we presumed, his wife. A not unpicturesque figure, tall, rather dashing in effect, ruddy visage, dragoon moustache, and habited in a light, smartly-cut sack suit of rather arresting checks, conspicuous grey spats; a gentleman manifesting no interest whatever in his surroundings.

Mr. Brownell, the critic, entered through the front door and moved to the elevator.

There stepped from the elevator car a somewhat portly little man who joined Mr. Wharton. He wore a rather queer looking, very big derby hat, oddly flat on top. His shoulders were hooped up somewhat like the figure of Joseph Choate. A rather funny, square, box-like body on little legs. An English look to his clothes. Under his arm an odd-looking club of a walking-stick. Mr. Brownell turned quickly to this rather amusing though not undistinguished figure, and said, "Mr. James—Brownell." The quaint gentleman took off his big hat, discovering to our intent curiosity a polished bald dome, and began instantly to talk, very earnestly, steadily, in a moderately pitched voice, gesticulating with an even rhythmic beat with his right hand, raised close to his face.

## Draft A

I ought perhaps to say a word in my own defence before I begin, since what follows is, frankly, gossip. It is gossip about a celebrated man; and there are people who believe that the great should be written about only by their equals, or by those who have been properly introduced. I do not agree. The coachman has always known more about his master than the master's biographer. The footman, standing behind the chair, overhears the conversation that the guests remember incorrectly afterwards. There was a waiter in Rome, so the story goes, who was perfectly candid about the emperors he had served, and nobody thinks the worse of history for it. A clerk in a bookshop belongs to that company. He stands behind the counter as the footman stands behind the chair; he is not supposed to be listening; he is always listening.

So much for the apology. Now for the visit.

It began, as far as I was concerned, with the husband. He was in the shop some time before anything happened, and he was very handsomely dressed: well enough, at any rate, to be noticed in an establishment where the customers are not, as a rule, remarkable for their clothes. He did not look at books. He looked at the door, with the resigned air of a man who has been instructed to wait, who has waited before, and who knows perfectly well that the waiting will be longer than he was promised. I took him, I remember, for a person of some importance in his own profession, whatever it was. I still do not know what it was.

Then the door opened and the novelist came in.

I had seen portraits, and they had not prepared me. He was stout—decidedly stout—and he wore a hat of a shape I have not encountered before or since; it sat on him, as it were, as an afterthought. When he removed it he was bald. And before the door had fairly closed behind him he was talking. He did not wait to be recognised, or to be greeted, or to discover whom he was addressing, but talked to the husband, and to me, and to the shop in general, as though we had all been in the middle of a conversation and he had merely stepped outside for a moment and come back.

## Draft B

It may be objected that it is not quite the thing for one who has been a clerk in a bookshop to go about telling what he saw there. I have thought of that. And I have concluded that I am in excellent company. The coach drivers of the great houses have always known a good deal about the great, and it has not, so far as I have heard, been thought wrong in them to mention it. The footman stands behind the chair; he has ears. Much of what we know of the manners of other days we know, when you come to think of it, from people who were paid to hold the door. And there was the waiter in Rome. He was the most candid man I ever met. He told me the private habits of half the notable visitors to the city, and he told it without malice and without the least embarrassment. It was, he seemed to feel, a part of his employment. I think it was.

So I shall go on.

It was a morning like any other. There was a gentleman waiting in the shop. He was very well dressed; better dressed, I recall thinking, than the occasion required, if there was an occasion. He was not looking at books. He stood near the door with his hands behind him and the air of a man who has been told to wait and has agreed to wait, but who would not, himself, have chosen to wait just there. He was, as it turned out, the husband. I did not know that then. I knew only that he was waiting, and that he did it well.

Then the novelist came in. I have seen a good many portraits of [the novelist] since, and not one of them is like what came in at the door. He was stout. He wore a hat which I shall not attempt to describe, except to say that it was not the hat one would have expected, and that I have never seen another like it. He took it off, and he was bald—entirely, shiningly bald. And before the hat was fairly off he was talking. He talked to the husband, and to the shop, and to me. There was no preliminary. He simply began, as though he had been talking all the way from wherever he had come, and had merely stepped through the door in the middle of a sentence.

## Draft C

I suppose I ought to apologise before I begin, since what follows is, frankly, gossip. But I have thought about it a good deal, and I find I am in respectable company.

Consider the coach driver. For generations he has carried the great and the not so great about the country, and it has never been expected that he should keep quiet about what he heard behind him. Consider the footman, who stands against the wall at dinner while the famous talk as though he were part of the furniture. Nobody is astonished when his memoirs appear; people are only astonished when they are dull. And consider the Roman waiter [example needed: the particular waiter and what he said], whose candour about the eminent diners he served is, as far as I can tell, the most honest thing written about them. These men saw their betters at close range and without ceremony, and they told what they saw. A bookstore clerk belongs to the same humble trade. He stands behind his counter as the footman stands behind his chair, and he is permitted, I think, the same privilege.

So, with that settled, to the day itself.

The first person I noticed was not the novelist at all but a man waiting near the front of the shop. He was very well dressed, in the careful, unhurried way of someone who has never needed to hurry, and he had the patient look of a husband who has been told he will only be a moment. He did not browse. He did not ask for anything. He simply waited, gloves in hand, with the air of a man who has done a great deal of waiting in his life and has made his peace with it. I remember wondering whom he belonged to.

A minute later I found out. The door opened and the novelist came in. I had formed some picture of him from his books, and it was wrong in every particular. He was stout, a good deal stouter than I had imagined. He wore a hat of a shape I could not account for, neither in fashion nor quite out of it, and when he took it off he was bald. None of this seemed to trouble him in the least.

Nor did he waste any time. Before I had fairly said good afternoon he was talking, easily and at length, as though we had been interrupted in the middle of a conversation and he was glad to pick it up again. What he talked about is the substance of the pages that follow.

## Draft D

We are aware that there is something not altogether dignified in the position of the former shop assistant who sets down what he saw of the great across the counter. It smacks of the servants' hall. But the servants' hall, when one comes to think of it, has given us a good deal of history. From time immemorial the coach driver has known more of his master's affairs than his master's biographer. The footman behind the chair heard what was said at the table, and a good part of what was meant. And there was that waiter in Rome, [detail needed: which waiter and the source], who spoke of his distinguished patrons with a candour that their own friends would never have dared. We ask only to be admitted, in a humble way, to that company. We, too, stood by and handed things.

It was a morning in [date needed: season or year]. The first figure we remember is a husband. He had taken up a position near the door, as husbands do, while his wife went among the shelves. A well-dressed man; very well dressed. Good coat, good gloves, a stick carried as though it had been to a school for sticks. An air of having waited in a great many places for a great many people, and of not minding. He did not look at the books. He looked at the door. He was, as it were, holding the shop for someone.

Then the novelist came in.

Stout; decidedly stout. A hat that we could not then, and cannot now, account for: [description needed: the hat]. Beneath it, when presently it was lifted, a head quite bald. We confess we had not expected that. One pictures the author of a celebrated novel otherwise; one pictures, perhaps, a great deal of hair, and some of it falling over the brow. Here was none.

And talk. He had not got fairly through the door before he was talking; to the husband, who had not asked for it, to us, to the shelves at large. There was no preliminary survey of the premises, no pause to take the measure of the place and the people in it. He simply began, as a tap begins when it is turned, and it did not appear that anyone was expected to turn it off.

We put down our pencil. It was evident that this was not going to be a short sale.
