## The passage (by the author)

We are made to see the inglorious peace in which men seek only their own ease.

Peace sitting under her olive, and slurring the days gone by, When the poor are hovell'd and hustled together, each sex, like swine, When only the ledger lives, and when only not all men lie.

From the evils of a soulless commercialism, and from the inanities of fashion, what is the way of escape? From the evils of peace he turns to the heroism of war.

I wish I could hear again The chivalrous battle song.

Ah God, for a man with heart, head, hand, Like some of the simple great ones gone For ever and ever by, One still strong man in a blatant land.

At last, breaking in upon the deadly stupidity and selfishness of the common life, is the noise of battle:—it lightened my despair When I thought that a war would arise in defence of the right, That an iron tyranny now should bend or cease, The glory of manhood stand on his ancient height, Nor Britain's one sole God be the millionaire.

Let it go or stay, so I wake to the higher aims Of a land that has lost for a little her lust of gold, And love of a peace that was full of wrongs and shames, Horrible, hateful, monstrous, not to be told; And hail once more to the banner of battle unroll'd!

That was an appeal to Young England, the England that was too young to remember the Napoleonic wars and was thirsting for an experience of its own.

## Draft A

There is a Victorian poem whose speaker looks at the peaceful society around him and finds it contemptible. [title and poet needed] It is not the kind of poem one expects from a comfortable age, and that is exactly what makes it worth reading.

The speaker's complaint is with what peace has produced. Everywhere he looks he sees commerce, and behind commerce a kind of worship of money. People have grown greedy and selfish. Trade has become the measure of everything, and the pursuit of gain has worked its way into every corner of life, corrupting what it touches. The calm that others praise, he sees as a slow rot.

His answer is war. He does not want it for its own sake, or at least he does not say so. What he hopes is that conflict will do what peace has failed to do: shake people out of their small, grasping habits and give them something larger to live for. War, in his mind, would bring back nobility and purpose. It would ask men to risk themselves for something other than profit, and in asking that, it would make them better.

It is easy to dismiss this as a young man's fever, or as the fancy of a writer who had never seen a battlefield. Perhaps it was. But the mood the poem describes is not a curiosity. It comes back, in one form or another, with every generation. Each new cohort seems to find something hollow in the prosperity it inherits and to wonder whether struggle, even violent struggle, might fill the gap.

That recurrence is the reason the poem deserves serious attention rather than a shrug. The longing it expresses is real, and it is not confined to one era. If we want to understand why people keep welcoming war, we have to take seriously the disappointment with peace that lies behind the welcome.

## Draft B

There is a Victorian poem in which the speaker looks out upon the peace of his country and finds it intolerable. It is not the peace of the saints he sees. It is the peace of the shopkeeper. Commerce has become the religion of the land, and Money is its god. Men cheat one another and call it business. They neglect the poor and call it economy. The whole society seems to him to have grown soft and mean together.

From this he turns with longing to the thought of war. He does not want it for its own sake. He wants it because it will break the spell. When the drums beat, he believes, men will forget their ledgers. They will find something to live for that is larger than themselves, and something to die for that is worth the dying. The selfish will be shamed, and the noble will come into their own.

It is easy for us to smile at this. We know, or think we know, what war is, and we are not inclined to take lessons in nobility from the battlefield. But I should not dismiss the poet too quickly. He has seen something true, even if he has drawn the wrong conclusion from it. A peace that means only that every man is left free to grow rich is not a very inspiring peace. It is no wonder that a generous young man should tire of it.

Nor is the mood peculiar to one poet or one age. It comes back with every generation. The young find the comfortable world of their fathers dull and a little shabby. They long for a cause that will ask everything of them. If no better cause is offered, they will take up the oldest one.

That is why the enthusiasm deserves to be taken seriously. It is not to be answered with a sneer. It is to be answered with a peace that asks as much of men as war does.

## Draft C

There is a Victorian poem, [title needed: the poem], whose speaker has grown heartily sick of peace. It is not the peace of the graveyard that offends him, but the peace of the counting-house, where a society that has made a god of money conducts its worship six days a week and rather more devoutly than it conducts the other on the seventh. The merchant cheats with a clear conscience. The poor are ground down with perfect respectability.

In such a world, the speaker argues, the virtues have nothing to do. Courage is unemployed. What is wanted, he thinks, is some great upheaval that will sweep the shopkeepers from the temple and give men once more a cause larger than their own comfort, a cause for which they might be willing to suffer and to die. He therefore turns his eyes toward war. Let the trumpet sound, he says in effect, and we shall discover that we are nobler than our ledgers.

It is easy to answer him. We know what war costs, and we know that the profiteer does very well out of it. The shopkeeper is not swept from the temple; he supplies the army. But the answer, however sound, does not dispose of the question.

For this enthusiasm is not peculiar to one poet or one decade. It returns with every generation, as regularly as the spring, and the young who feel it look at the world their fathers have made, find it mean and selfish, and imagine that nothing short of a battle would make it noble again. The mood deserves to be taken seriously, not because its remedy is right, but because its complaint so often is. A peace that offers men nothing to live for will not long be preferred to a war that offers them something to die for.

## Draft D

There is a Victorian poem, [title and poet needed], which I have never been able to read without a certain discomfort. It is not a bad poem. The trouble is that it is too good; it says with great earnestness a thing which a great many people have felt.

The speaker looks about him at a world at peace, and he does not like what he sees. It is a peace of shopkeepers. Every man is busy getting and spending, and the only god that is worshipped with any real devotion is money. Commerce has crept into every corner of life and has made it mean. The rich grind the poor, and the poor envy the rich, and nobody thinks of anything higher than his own advantage. The peace, he says in effect, is only a war carried on by baser weapons.

And so he longs for real war. He hopes that when the drums beat, men will forget their ledgers. They will remember that there are things worth dying for. The selfish will become generous, and the timid brave, and the nation will be purged of its greed as by fire. Battle will restore to life the nobility and purpose which prosperity has taken away.

It is easy to smile at this, and to point out that war has its own profiteers, and that the ledgers are kept very busily while the drums are beating. But I do not think we ought to smile too quickly. The poet was not a bloodthirsty man. He was a disappointed idealist, and his disappointment was real.

What is more, he was not alone. The same enthusiasm for battle appears in every generation. It seems to come up fresh with each new crop of young men who find the world too tame for them. It is not a thing to be dismissed with a sneer. It deserves serious attention, for it tells us something about what peace has failed to give.
