# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-05

## The passage (by the author)

But now, in America, there is a tendency that way. If a boy does not feel a pre-established harmony between his soul and the humanities, then give him an academic degree on something with which his soul will be in pre-established harmony. And if there is no pre-established harmony between his soul and any form of learning, then create institutions that will give him a degree with no learning to speak of at all. I do not mean to deny that many of our virtually valueless colleges were founded in the pathetic inherited conviction that learning and culture were too great goods not to be accessible to all who cared passionately for them. But I do believe that the reverence for learning and culture has been largely replaced by a conviction that anything which has so great a reputation as a college degree must be put within the reach of all, even at the risk of making its reputation a farce. The privileged have been unwilling that their children should be made to work; the unprivileged have been unwilling that their children should see anything of good repute, anything with a prestige value, denied to them. We have all demanded a royal road to a thing to which there is no royal road. The expensive schools lead their pupils from kindergarten to nature-study and eurhythmics, with basket-work and gymnastics thrown in; the public schools follow them as closely as they can. Of real training of the mind there is very little in any school. The rich do not want their children overworked; the poor want a practical result for their children's fantastically long school hours. So domestic science comes in for girls, and carpentering for boys. Anything to make it easy, on the one hand; anything to make a universal standard possible, on the other.

## Draft A

We have decided, it seems, that every American shall have a degree, and we have gone about the business in the simplest way: by making the degree cost nothing in particular. Our schools and colleges hand out their credentials to any one who will sit in the appointed chairs for the appointed number of years. Real study is not required of the candidate; it is merely permitted him, if he has a taste for it. The diploma has become, not a certificate of learning, but a receipt for attendance.

I do not blame the institutions alone. They have been pushed, and pushed from both ends of the social scale. The wealthy parent does not wish his child to work hard—he has not, perhaps, worked very hard himself, and he sees no reason why his son should. The poorer parent, on the other hand, wants for his child exactly the prestige the rich man's child enjoys, and he wants it on the same easy terms. Between the two, the college that asks for genuine effort is left with very few friends. Some one will say that this is democracy. Granted; but it is democracy of a curious sort, which levels by lowering the mark rather than by raising the pupil.

Nor is the lowering confined to the matter of effort. It extends to the very notion of what a school is for. One finds, more and more, that the crafts and the practical subjects are given the place of honour, and that the training of the intellect is treated as a quaint survival (to be indulged, like Latin verse, by the few who insist!). I have nothing against carpentry. I fancy a boy may learn a good deal from it. The point is that a school exists to discipline the mind, and a school that has forgotten this has forgotten its reason for being. We shall not make every one educated by calling every one so. We shall only make the word mean nothing.

## Draft B

We have decided, it would seem, that every American child is to have a degree; and since not every child can earn one, the degree must be made easy enough to fit. That is the whole secret of the matter. Our schools and colleges hand out their credentials, to-day, for something that no honest person would call study. I do not say the certificates are forged. I say only, and I say it with regret, that they certify very little. The boy who has sat through his years of lectures (and not always through the lectures!) comes out with the same parchment as the boy who has worked. Well: the parchment is what was wanted, and the parchment is what he has.

Some one will object, of course, that this is a democratic triumph. Granted; but it is a triumph of the wrong kind. The rich parent does not wish his son to be worked hard—it is somehow not quite the thing—and the poor parent, very naturally, will not see his son denied the prestige the rich boy carries off without effort. Between them they have got what they asked for: not an education but the label of one. And the schools, on the whole, have not resisted. They have turned, I fancy, with a certain relief from the training of the mind to the teaching of crafts and practical subjects, which can be graded without anguish and failed by nobody. One can make a very pretty bookshelf without ever having learned to think; and it's the bookshelf, not the thinking, that the parents come to admire. The point is that the intellect is not trained by accident. It has to be drilled, patiently, and drilling is unpleasant; and we have made unpleasantness the one thing a school may not ask of a child. I do not, mind you, blame the teachers alone. We have all wanted the prize without the race. We shall get exactly that.

## Draft C

I do not know how other people feel about it, but I am exceedingly uneasy when I look at what our schools and colleges are doing with the degree. It has become, apparently, a thing that every American child is entitled to, as he is entitled to a vote and a clean shirt on Sunday. And since not every child can earn it, the institutions have obligingly arranged that very few need try. They hand out credentials—diplomas, certificates, the whole gilded apparatus—that require little real study of any one; and the young man who holds them knows, if he is honest, that he has been let off.

This is not snobbishness; it is a matter of plain fact. Who asked for the change? Everybody, it would seem, and for opposite reasons. The wealthy parent does not wish his children to work hard; he did not, perhaps, himself, and he sees no reason why his son should be made unhappy over a Latin verb. The poorer parent—and I cannot find it in me to blame him very much—demands that his children have exactly the same prestige as the rich man's children, and he does not greatly care what the prestige is made of. Between the two, the school is squeezed until it gives way. It has given way, I think, almost everywhere.

Let us grant, by all means, that there is honour in a craft. Let us admit that a boy who can build a good table is better off than a boy who can build nothing. But there our respect for the practical bids us stop. The schools have not merely added carpentry and bookkeeping to the old studies; they have put them in place of the training of the intellect, and then they have called the result by the old name. That is where the deceit comes in. A degree which means anything means that some one has been made to think; a degree which everybody can have means, frankly, nothing at all [example needed].

## Draft D

There is a curious ambition abroad in American education: the wish that every young person should hold a degree, and the quiet willingness to make the degree mean less so that the wish can come true. We have not raised the student to the standard. We have lowered the standard to the student, and called the result opportunity.

The signs are everywhere once one looks for them. Institutions hand out credentials that require little real study. A diploma that once marked years of disciplined effort now marks, too often, years of attendance. The paper is the same size and bears the same seal, but it carries far less weight, and everyone who hires or admits on the strength of it has begun to suspect as much.

The pressure comes from both ends of society at once. Wealthy parents, anxious that their children should not suffer, press the schools to spare them hard work; they want the reward without the struggle, and they have the influence to ask for it. Poorer parents, reasonably enough, see the prestige a degree confers and demand that their children have an equal share of it. Neither group is asking for more learning. Both are asking for the badge, and the schools, eager to please everyone, find it easiest to give the badge freely.

At the same time the curriculum drifts. Schools increasingly favour crafts and practical subjects over the training of the intellect. There is nothing shameful in a trade, and a good carpenter is worth more than a poor scholar. But a school that teaches only what can be sold on leaving has forgotten why schools exist. The purpose was to form a mind able to reason, to weigh evidence, to read hard things and not be defeated by them.

A degree that anyone can have is a degree that proves nothing. If we want universal education, we must be willing to make it real, not merely universal.
