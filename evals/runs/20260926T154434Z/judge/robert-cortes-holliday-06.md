# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-06

## The passage (by the author)

Joined presently by Mrs. Wharton, the party, bidding Mr. Brownell adieu, took a somewhat humorous departure (we felt) from the shop; Mr. James, with some suddenness, preceding out the door. Moving nimbly up the Avenue, he was overhauled by Mrs. Wharton under full sail, who attached herself to his arm. Her husband by an energetic forward play around the end achieved her other wing. In this formation, sticks flashing, skirt whipping, with a somewhat spirited mien, the august spectacle receded from our rapt view, to be at length obliterated as a unit by the general human scene.

We saw Mr. James after this a number of times. Accompanied again by Mrs. Wharton, and later in the charge (such was the effect) of another lady, who, we understood, drives regularly to her social chariot literary lions. In something like six years' observation of the human being in a book shop, we have never seen any person so thoroughly in a book store, a magazine, that is, of books, as Mr. James. One can be, you know—it is most common, indeed—in a book store and at the same time not be in a book store—any more than if one were in a hotel lobby. Mr. James "snooked" around the shop. He ran his nose over the tables, and inch by inch (he must be very shortsighted) along the walls, stood on tiptoe and pulled down volumes from high places, rummaged in dark corners, was apparently oblivious of the presence of anything but the books. He was not the slightest in a hurry. He would have been, we felt, content and quite happy, like a child with blocks, to play this way by himself all day.

Happening, by our close proximity, to turn to us the first time in the shop that he required attention, upon each succeeding visit he sought out us to attend to his wishes. The position of retail salesman "on the floor" is one completely exposed to every human attitude and humour. Against arrogance, against contempt of himself as a shop person, a species of "counter-jumper," against irascibility, against bigoted ignorance, against an indissoluble assumption, perhaps logical, that he is of inferior mentality, this factotum has no defence. His very business is to meet all with amenity. It is his daily portion, included in the material with which he works.

## Draft A

When the business was done, the whole party left together, and the leaving was the best part of it. They went out arm in arm, and marched up the street as though they were a small procession that had forgotten what it was celebrating. People stepped aside to let them pass. Nobody in the group seemed to notice how they looked, or if they noticed, nobody minded. It was a comic exit, and I think they all knew it, which only made it funnier.

That was not the last we saw of him. He came back to the shop, not once but several times, and on those later visits he was a different kind of customer altogether. There was no party with him and no occasion to mark. He simply came in and browsed. And he browsed the way a child explores a room full of toys, with a total absorption that shut out everything else. He would stand in front of a shelf and lean in until his face was almost touching the spines, peering at each title as though it might tell him something if he looked closely enough. He moved slowly along the rows, stopping, bending, straightening up, stopping again. If you spoke to him while he was doing it, he took a moment to come back from wherever he had gone.

Each time, he asked for the same assistant. He did not want whoever happened to be free; he wanted that one person, and he would wait if necessary. I have thought since about what that meant for the assistant, because the job itself is a strange one. A shop assistant is paid, in part, to be polite to everyone who walks through the door, and that includes the customers who are rude, the ones who are impatient, and the ones who make it clear they think the person serving them is beneath their notice. Whatever scorn comes across the counter, the assistant has to absorb it and answer pleasantly. That is the job. There is no clause that excuses you when the customer is unpleasant.

Against that background, a customer who is merely eccentric, who peers at shelves and asks for you by name, is not a burden. Set beside the others, he may well have been the easiest part of the job.

## Draft B

They went out, when they did go, in a manner which the shop did not soon forget. The whole party of them linked arms at the door, the novelist somewhere in the middle, and off they went up the street in step, marching, as it were, to a band that nobody else could hear. The people on the pavement made way. The clerks stood at the window and watched them go. It was not dignified. It was a good deal better than dignified.

He came back, of course. He came back a number of times after that, alone, and on these later visits there was nothing of the parade about him. He browsed. Now, a great many people browse in a bookshop, or say they do; they drift along the tables with one eye on the clock and the other on the door. That was not his way. He browsed as a small boy looks into a toy-shop window, entirely, with the whole of his attention and none of it left over for anything else in the world. He would stoop until his nose was all but upon the shelf, and peer along the backs of the books one by one, and take down a volume, and put it back, and take down the next. You could have spoken to him and he would not have heard you. I do not think he knew he was in a shop at all.

And when at last he came up for air he would ask for the same assistant. Always the same one. If that particular young man was busy he would wait; if he was out to lunch he would wait longer.

Consider for a moment the lot of a shop assistant. His business, from the first of the morning to the closing of the doors, is to be civil. He is civil to the lady who wants a book whose title she has forgotten, by an author whose name she never knew, with a blue cover. He is civil to the gentleman who informs him that the whole establishment is run by fools. Whatever scorn a customer cares to pour upon him, and customers are free with it, he receives with a bow and a "Yes, sir," and is back at his post the next morning to receive some more. It is a trade in patience. Nobody much remarks it.

So it was something, in that position, to be asked for by name.

## Draft C

They left, as I recall, in a manner that was very nearly theatrical. Arm in arm, the whole company of them, they went out through the door and marched up the street in step, like a small and rather undisciplined regiment returning from some private victory. Passers-by turned to look. It was, so to say, an exit. One half expected a band.

He came back afterward, not once but again and again, and on these later visits he came alone. I have seldom seen anybody browse with such complete and unguarded absorption. He would stand before a shelf with his head thrust forward and his eyes brought down to within a few inches of the bindings, peering along the titles one after another, as a child peers into a shop window at Christmas, forgetful of everything else in the world, of the hour, of his hat, of the people who wished to get past him. Now and then he drew out a volume, turned it over, examined it as though it were a curious shell picked up on a beach, and put it back in precisely the wrong place. There was nothing of the celebrated novelist about him at such moments. He was simply a man enjoying himself.

And he always asked for the same assistant. Why this particular young man, nobody could say; he had been civil on the first occasion, or else he merely had a face that was easy to remember. In any case, the novelist would come in, look about, and inquire for him by name, and would wait, if necessary, until he was free.

Now the position of a clerk in a shop is a peculiar one, and it is perhaps not generally understood. He is there to be pleasant. He is there to be pleasant to everybody: to the customer who knows exactly what he wants and the customer who has no idea, to the customer who is charming and the customer who plainly regards him as something between a machine and a servant. If he is sneered at, he smiles. If he is contradicted, he agrees. If he is treated with open contempt, he says "Certainly, sir," and goes to fetch the book. That is the job. It is not, when you come to consider it, an easy one; and it is not a thing one learns from any manual.

## Draft D

They went out, the three of them, or however many there were of them, in a manner which we have not forgotten. They linked arms. Arm in arm, the novelist in the middle, they swung through the door, and, once upon the pavement, they fell into step and marched up the street like a little company of soldiers going off to a war of no great danger. We all watched them out of sight. It was, somehow, a very happy exit.

He came back. He came back a number of times after that. And it was then that we saw him as a browser. There are browsers and browsers. There is the browser who glances along a shelf as a man glances along a line of faces in a crowd, looking for one he knows. There is the browser who picks up a book, weighs it in his hand, and puts it down again. The novelist was not of these kinds. He browsed with the whole of himself. He bent to the lower shelves; he stretched to the upper ones; he put his face close, very close, to the backs of the books, and peered at them, one after another, as a small boy peers into the window of a toy shop. Nothing else existed for him. You might have dropped a stack of dictionaries behind him, and he would not have turned his head. It was a total, a childlike, absorption, and it was very pleasant to see.

Always he asked for the same assistant. And here, perhaps, it is in order to say a word concerning the lot of the assistant in a bookshop, or in any shop. His lot is to be polite. That is the whole of it. He is to be polite to the customer who knows what he wants and to the customer who does not; to the customer who is gracious and to the customer who is not. The customer may look at him as though he were a piece of furniture; he may speak to him as though he were something less than that. The customer may scorn him—openly, elaborately, with a curl of the lip. The assistant bows. "Yes, sir," he says. "Certainly, sir." It is what he is paid for. And so a customer who asks for him by name, and seems to be glad to see him, is a thing he remembers.
