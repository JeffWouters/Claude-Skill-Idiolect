# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-03

## The passage (by the author)

Ours is a commercial age, in which most people are bent on getting money. That is a platitude. It is also, intellectually speaking, a materialistic age, when most of our intellectual power is given either to prophylaxis, or to industrial chemistry, or to the invention of physical conveniences—all ultimately concerned with the body. Even the philanthropists deal with the soul through the body, and Christianity has long since become "muscular." How, in such an age, can culture flourish—culture, which cares even more about the spirit than about the flesh? It was pointed out not long ago, in an Atlantic article, that many of our greatest minds have dwelt in bodies that the eugenists would have legislated out of existence. Many of the greatest saints found sainthood precisely in denying the power of the ailing flesh to restrict the soul. There is more in the great mystics than psychiatry will ever account for. But science, in spite of its vistas, is short-sighted. It talks in æons, but keeps its eye well screwed to the microscope. The geologic ages are dealt with by pick and hammer and reduced to slides, and the lore of the stars has become a pure matter of mathematical formulæ. Human welfare is a question of microbes. Neither pundit nor populace cares, at the present day, for perspectives. The past is discredited because it is not modern. Not to be modern is the great sin.

So, perhaps, it is. But every one has, in his day, been modern. And surely even modernity is a poor thing beside immortality. Since we must all die, is it not perhaps better to be a dead lion than a living dog? And is it not a crime against human nature to consider negligible "the best that has been thought and said in the world"? It is only by considering it negligible that we can consent to let ourselves be overrun by the hordes of ignorance and materialism—the people (God save the mark!) of to-morrow. Let us stand, if we must, on practical grounds: the bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. As if our only guaranty that to-morrow would be tolerable were not precisely that it is sprung from a past that we know to have been, at many points, noble! It is pathetic to see people refusing to learn the lessons of history; it is a waste that no efficiency expert ought to permit. All learning is a text-book which would save much time to him who works for the perfection of the world. But I begin to think that our age does not really care about perfection; and that it Would rather make a thousand-year-old mistake than learn a remedy from history. So much the worse for to-morrow!

## Draft A

Now it has been remarked, not once but a great many times, that ours is an age of money and of bodies. I do not say it with any particular bitterness; it is merely a fact, like the weather. Science and industry—the two great engines of our time—attend almost wholly to our physical needs. They feed us, warm us, carry us about, and mend us when we break. For all this we ought, I suppose, to be grateful, and I dare say we are. But one notices that when the engines have finished with the body, there is very little steam left over for anything else. Culture is a thing one gets round to, like the attic; and one seldom gets round to the attic.

It follows, naturally enough, that the worst thing one can say of a man, or a book, or an idea, is that it is out of date. Not wicked, not foolish, not even dull—merely out of date. That is the final damnation. We should forgive a friend almost any vice sooner than an old-fashioned hat; and we treat the thoughts of our grandfathers much as we treat their hats. (It is always our grandfathers who are out of date, never ourselves!) The modern young person does not refute the past. He does not trouble to. He simply observes that it is past, and considers the argument closed.

Let us admit, by all means, that a great deal of the past deserves to be forgotten. Let us admit that reverence for antiquity has been made the excuse for a good deal of stupidity and not a little cruelty. Indeed, I cannot think who will deny it. But there our impatience ought to stop. History is not a museum of old hats; it is a record—the only record we have—of what happens when men behave in certain ways. Every folly we are about to commit has been committed before, and the account of what it cost is lying on the shelf for any one who will take it down. [example needed] To refuse to read that account because it is old is to throw away the one lesson we have paid for in advance.

And the waste is not the worst of it. A people that does not know where it has been cannot very well know where it is going. It walks forward with its eyes on its feet, admiring the excellence of its boots. Sooner or later it walks off a cliff that every generation before it had marked quite plainly on the map. I do not say that this will happen to us. I say only that we are doing nothing whatever to prevent it; and that a civilization which cares for its stomach and neglects its memory is laying up a very bad future for its children. It is our children, after all, who matter. We cannot give them a better world if we have thrown away the only guide to how worlds are lost.

## Draft B

We live in an age that measures most things by money and by the comfort of the body. This is not said as a sneer. The achievements of the age are real, and many of them are admirable. But an age has a character as well as a record, and the character of ours shows most clearly in what it chooses to value and what it quietly lets slip.

Look at where our science and our industry direct their energy. Overwhelmingly, they serve physical needs: how we are fed, housed, clothed, carried from place to place, kept warm and kept well. These are worthy aims, and no one who has been cold or hungry would wish them away. Yet when nearly all our best effort flows in one direction, the other directions are starved. Culture, which is concerned with what we think and feel and how we understand our lives, comes to look like an ornament, something pleasant to have once the serious business is done. History, which is concerned with how we came to be where we are, begins to look like a hobby.

Alongside this runs a peculiar fear. In our time the worst thing that can be said of a person, an idea or a practice is that it is out of date. Not that it is false, not that it is cruel, not that it is foolish, but simply that it is old. To be behind the times is treated as a failing more serious than most moral faults. Under that pressure, people are reluctant to defend anything merely because it has lasted, and the fact that something has been tested over generations counts against it rather than for it.

The cost of this attitude is easy to underestimate. History is not a museum of curiosities. It is the record of what people before us tried, what worked, what failed and why. Every mistake made in the past was paid for by someone, and the lesson is there for anyone willing to read it. When we turn away from that record, we do not escape its lessons; we simply throw them away and prepare to pay for them again. [example needed]

Nor is the damage confined to what we lose. A people that does not know its own past has no clear sense of the direction it has been travelling, and so no good way of judging where it is heading. It becomes easy to mistake novelty for progress, and to walk confidently into dangers that an earlier generation could have described in detail. Ignoring history does not free us for the future. It leaves us exposed to it.

None of this requires us to give up what the age does well. It asks only that we remember that people are more than bodies with wants, and that the future is built on the past, whether or not we care to look.

## Draft C

We live, it is constantly borne in upon me, in an age that thinks chiefly of the body and the purse. I do not say this as a laudator temporis acti—or not wholly so. Science and industry have done wonders, and one would be ungrateful to deny it. But their wonders are all of one kind. They feed us, warm us, carry us about, cure us of our ailments; they attend, that is, to our physical needs, and to very little else. What cannot be eaten, worn, or sold has a way of dropping out of the reckoning.

Culture is precisely the thing that cannot be eaten, worn, or sold. It is not surprising, therefore, that it should be held cheap. Nor is it surprising that history should be held cheaper still. For history is, among other things, the record of what people thought and felt before our own brilliant arrival; and the present age has made up its mind that there is nothing to be learned from them.

Consider the reproach we most dread. It is not that we are cruel, or stupid, or dishonest. It is that we are out of date. To be behind the times is, to-day, the unforgivable sin—worse than meanness, worse than folly. A man may be forgiven for knowing nothing; he will not be forgiven for knowing only what was known yesterday. Our grandparents, I think, would have found this a very odd scale of values. I find it odd myself.

Some one will object that the past had its own follies, and that we are well rid of them. Granted; but we cannot be rid of them unless we know what they were. The point is that history is not a museum but a warning. Every generation that has ignored it has had to learn its lessons over again, and at full price [example needed: a mistake repeated through ignorance of history]. Nothing is so expensive as experience bought twice.

Let us face it squarely. A people that cares only for what it can use will not long know what it is using, or why. It will have machines without purposes, and comforts without content. It will be rich, to be sure, and healthy, very likely, and bored past bearing. And it will walk into the future as a man walks into a dark room whose shape he has never troubled to learn—confidently, and straight into the furniture.

I am not asking that we give up our railways or our drains. Heaven forbid! I am asking only that we keep a corner of the mind for the things that do not pay. The body will look after itself; it always has. It is the rest of us that is in danger, and the danger is not that we shall be poor but that we shall be empty. We are spending an inheritance and calling it progress. Let us at least read the will.

## Draft D

We live, it is constantly impressed upon us, in a practical age. Science and industry, which might have given us leisure for the things of the mind, have turned nearly all their energy to the things of the body: to what we eat and wear, to how we travel and how we are warmed, to the thousand comforts a prosperous society counts as necessities. I do not despise these comforts; no one who has gone without them will. But I notice that the age measures itself almost entirely by them, and that whatever cannot be sold, or eaten, or ridden in, is regarded with a faint and courteous impatience. Culture, in such a world, is a luxury for the idle (and the idle, God save the mark, are no longer in fashion!). History is something worse. It is out of date.

Well: to be out of date is, apparently, the one unpardonable sin. One may be vulgar, one may be ignorant, one may even be dishonest, and the world will find some excuse; but one must not be old-fashioned. I have heard the charge brought against a book, a house, an opinion, a whole generation [example needed: a real instance of the charge], and always with the same finality, as though nothing further need be said. It is a curious standard for a people who pride themselves on reasoning. The newest thing is not necessarily the truest, any more than the oldest is; the question is simply whether a thing is sound. Yet we have come, I fancy, to confuse the calendar with the judgment, and to suppose that yesterday has nothing to teach because it is yesterday.

The point is that history is not an ornament but an inheritance. It holds, in the plainest sense, the experience of every one who has gone before us: their errors and their recoveries, the ways by which societies have prospered and the ways by which they have perished. To neglect all that is not merely to be uncultivated. It is to throw away, unopened, the one book of instructions we possess, and then to wonder why the machine keeps breaking in the same places. Some one will object that circumstances change, and that the lessons of the past no longer apply. Granted; but human nature changes a good deal more slowly than circumstances do, and it is human nature that history chiefly records. A generation that will not learn from its predecessors must learn everything over again, at its own expense—and, what is harder, at the expense of those who come after it. That is the real danger. Ignorance of the past is a private misfortune; it becomes a public peril the moment it begins to shape the future.
