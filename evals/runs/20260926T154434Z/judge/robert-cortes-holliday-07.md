# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-07

## The passage (by the author)

Succeeding visits caused us to suspect that Mr. James's ideas of the machinery of business are somewhat naive. He seemed to regard us as, so to say, the whole works. It entered our head that maybe Mr. James thought we received and answered all manner of correspondence, editorial as well as that connected with the retail business, opened up in the morning, read, accepted, and rejected manuscript, nailed up boxes for shipment, swept out the shop, and were acquainted perfectly with all confidential matters of the House. "I wrote you" (us), "you know," he said. And he referred by the way, apparently upon the assumption that the matter had been laid before us, to business of which we could not possibly have cognizance. And then he desired to send some books. Fumbling in his breast pocket, he produced a letter, from which he read aloud a list of his own works apparently requested of him. Carefully replacing his letter, he said: "I should like to send these books to my sister-in-law." With that he started out.

Now, it was not a difficult problem to assume that this could be no other than Mrs. William James, still, it is customary for purchasers to state the name of the person to whom goods are to go, and many people are sceptical that the salesman has it down right even then. "Your sister-in-law, Mr. James, is———?" we suggested. "Oh, yes, of course—of course; Mrs. William James; of course—of course," Mr. James said. Now, certainly, he supposed (it was evident) he had got finally settled a difficult and complicated piece of business. Mrs. William James's regular address we might reasonably infer. Still it might be that she was at the moment somewhere else, on a visit. It were better to have Mr. James give his order in the regular way. "And the address?" we mentioned. "Oh, yes—oh, yes; of course—of course," Mr. James said apologetically. Then, pausing a moment to see if there was anything more in this bewildering labyrinth of details to such a complex transaction, he departed, taking, as he drew away, his hat, as Mrs. Nickleby says, "completely off."

## Draft A

Of the workings of a shop the novelist had, it was soon plain to us, only the haziest notion. It was his idea, so far as we could make it out, that the salesman who had first waited upon him was, in effect, the firm. This one man, he appeared to suppose, took the order, and wrapped the parcel, and addressed it, and carried it to the post, and kept the books, and sent out the bill. That there might be a shipping department, a mail-order department, a credit department, a gentleman somewhere upstairs whose whole life was spent among accounts—of all this he had not the faintest suspicion.

Once he came in to order some books. They were copies of his own works. He wished them sent to a relative of his, a lady. He named the titles, he told us how many, he was very clear and very pleasant about it; and then, with a nod and a smile, he went out of the door. He had not said who the lady was. He had not said where she lived. The order stood upon the pad complete in every particular except the two which were necessary to fill it.

It was not simple to set right. Every detail had to be drawn out of him, one at a time, as you might draw a timid animal from under a porch. What was the lady's name? Ah, her name. Yes, of course. He gave it. And her address? Her address—he thought a moment—her address was [address]. Each particular came forth separately, after a little pause, as though it were being fetched from some distant room of his mind where it was not often wanted. And at each step he was sorry. He apologised. He apologised very sincerely, with a genuine distress, for giving so much trouble. He really was very sorry.

We did not mind. One does not, somehow, mind that kind of thing in that kind of man. It was only that one saw, at such a moment, how the world of affairs looks to one who does not live in it: a single obliging person behind a counter, who knows everything, and does everything, and to whom it is quite unnecessary to explain.

## Draft B

He had, it became clear, only the vaguest idea of how a shop actually works. In his mind there seemed to be one salesman, and that one salesman did everything. The same man who greeted him at the door would presumably also find the books, wrap them, write up the order, take the money, arrange the post, keep the accounts and answer the letters. The notion that a firm might divide its work among different people, each with a separate job and a separate desk, does not appear to have occurred to him. He spoke to whoever was in front of him as though that person were the whole business.

This was charming in its way, but it made the simplest transaction surprisingly hard. On one visit he decided he wanted copies of his own works sent to a relative. That part was straightforward enough; the books were on the shelves and the order could be written down. Then he thanked everyone, gathered himself together and left. It was only after the door had closed that anyone realised he had not said who the relative was. There was no name. There was no address. There was an order for a parcel with nowhere to send it.

When he came back, the missing details had to be drawn out of him one at a time. What was her name? He told us, and then looked slightly surprised that we had needed it. Where did she live? He had to think. Which street, which town, which number? Each answer came only after a question, and each question seemed to strike him as a small revelation, as if he had assumed the information would somehow travel from his head to the parcel without passing through anyone else. It was not that he was unwilling. He simply had not imagined that the salesman, the one who did everything, did not already know.

And at every step he apologised. He was sorry for the trouble, sorry for forgetting, sorry for taking up our time. The apologies were sincere, and they made it impossible to be irritated with him. A man who writes whole books full of carefully arranged detail, and who cannot remember to give a shop a relative's address, is a contradiction, but it is a very human one.

## Draft C

Of the way in which a shop is carried on he had, so far as one could discover, no notion whatever. It was not that he held any wrong ideas about it. He simply had not given the matter a thought, and saw no reason why he should.

A bookshop of any size is a fairly complicated affair. There are the men who sell, and the men who wrap, and the men who keep the accounts, and the men downstairs who send the parcels out, and somewhere behind a door a person who orders the books in the first place. None of this had reached him. In his mind the firm consisted, I believe, of one salesman: the salesman he happened to be talking to. That salesman took the order, found the book, made out the bill, tied up the parcel, carried it to the post, and, for all the novelist knew, wrote the book too. It was a simple and a trusting view of commerce, and there was something rather charming in it.

One day he wished to send copies of his own works to a relative of his. A handsome thought. He chose the books with care, and said that they were to go to her, and thanked the salesman, and went away. Well, to whom were they to go? He had not said. Where did she live? He had not said that either. The books sat upon the counter, done up and addressed to nobody, while the shop considered what to do.

When at length the question was put to him, the information had to be got out of him a piece at a time. Her name, first. Then the street. Then the number of the house. Then the town. Each item came out slowly, as though it had to be fetched from some distant room of his mind where it was not often wanted. And with each one he apologised. He was very sorry. He had quite forgotten. It was stupid of him. He apologised, I think, more often than there were details to supply.

## Draft D

Of the manner in which a bookshop is actually conducted he possessed, I think, only the haziest conception. It was not that he was indifferent to the subject; he was, on the contrary, unfailingly courteous and attentive whenever it arose. But it seemed never to have occurred to him that an establishment of any consequence might employ more than one individual, or that the gentleman who had just found him a book was not also the gentleman who kept the accounts, wrapped the parcels, wrote the letters, ordered the stock, swept the floor and, for all one knows, owned the building. In his mind, so far as one could make it out, there was the shop, and there was the salesman, and the salesman did everything.

One afternoon he announced that he wished to order some copies of his own works. They were to be sent to a relative of his, a lady. This was entirely agreeable, and the instructions were recorded with appropriate ceremony: so many copies of this title, so many of that, the bindings chosen, the whole matter settled. He thanked us warmly, took up his hat, and went out into the street.

He had not left her name. He had not left her address. He had not so much as mentioned the city in which she resided.

When he next appeared, the omission was presented to him as delicately as possible.

"Your relative, sir. Where are the books to go?"

"Why, to her, of course."

"Yes, sir. And her name?"

He supplied it, after considerable reflection. The street had to be drawn out of him separately, and then the number, and then the town, each particular arriving separately, like a bashful guest who must be coaxed into the drawing-room. At every stage he apologised. He was exceedingly sorry to occasion so much inconvenience. He had supposed, it appeared, that we should somehow know, the salesman being, after all, the universal functionary who knew everything. It was impossible to be exasperated with him. One simply wrote it down.
