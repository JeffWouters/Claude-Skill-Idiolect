## The passage (by the author)

The ride is 31 minutes. Seven stops. I know them by the sounds as much as the sights. The first stop has a long curve before it, and the wheels squeal. The third has a bridge, and the carriage goes hollow for a moment. The fifth is by the canal. I look for the heron. It is there about one day in three. Grey and still on the far bank. Then gone.

I used to work on the train. Laptop open, emails done before the office. I stopped two years ago. Now I look out of the window. Or I read. Or I just sit.

It is the only half hour in the day with nothing to do. No one can reach me. Well, they can. But I do not answer.

In winter the ride starts in the dark. By the canal the sky is grey. By the last stop it is light. You can watch it happen. The windows go from black mirrors to glass. Your own face fades out and the fields come in.

In summer it is light the whole way. Less to watch. But the fields are green and there are horses at stop four.

The way home is different. The 17.51. It is full, and hot, and people are tired. I stand most days. That is fine. I stand by the door and hold the rail and look at nothing.

I know this is a small life, in a way. The same train. The same seat. The same heron. But the sameness is useful. It clears a space. When nothing changes, you start to see the small things that do.

The frost on the fence at stop two. The day the canal froze. The new graffiti on the bridge, a yellow fox, one metre tall.

Last week the boy with the cello was not there. Nor the next day. Then on Friday he was back. With a new case. Black this time, not brown.
