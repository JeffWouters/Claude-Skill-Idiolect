## The passage (by the author)

Why is it that we continue to ask for estimates when so few of us believe in them? The question is not entirely rhetorical, since the practice of estimation survives in almost every software organisation despite a long and well-documented history of disappointment, and the persistence of a practice that consistently fails usually suggests that it is doing something other than what it claims to do. Arguably the estimate is not a prediction at all; it is a negotiation conducted in the vocabulary of prediction, a way of reconciling what the business wants with what the engineers fear, in which both parties agree to pretend that the resulting figure describes the future. I suspect most experienced engineers understand this intuitively, even if they would not articulate it in quite those terms. The difficulty arises when the pretence is forgotten—when the negotiated figure hardens into a commitment, and the commitment becomes a measure by which people are judged.

It may be helpful to consider what we are actually doing when we produce an estimate for a piece of software work. We are imagining a sequence of activities that has not yet happened, in a system we only partially understand, subject to interruptions we cannot foresee, and we are compressing all of that uncertainty into a single figure that must fit neatly into a planning document. The act is, in some sense, heroic; it asks the imagination to perform a task for which it is poorly equipped, and it rewards confidence over accuracy. Is it any wonder that the results are unreliable? What is more surprising, perhaps, is how rarely the unreliability is acknowledged in the planning process itself, as though each new estimate were an entirely fresh attempt rather than the latest entry in a long series of similar misjudgements.

## Draft A

Ask most software teams whether they believe their own estimates and the answer, given privately, is usually some version of no. Yet the estimates continue to be produced, week after week, with considerable ceremony and very little conviction. Why should this be?

Part of the explanation, I think, is that estimates are rarely only what they claim to be. On the surface they are forecasts, attempts to say how long a piece of work will take. Underneath they function as a kind of bargaining between the business and its engineers: one side hoping for a smaller number, the other quietly padding against the unknown, and both aware that the figure eventually agreed upon reflects the negotiation at least as much as the work. There is nothing especially sinister in this. Bargaining is a reasonable way of reconciling competing pressures, provided everyone understands that it is taking place.

The trouble begins when the number outlives the conversation that produced it. An estimate offered tentatively, with all its caveats, has a way of reappearing weeks later as a commitment, stripped of its qualifications and attached to someone's name. At that point the engineers are no longer being held to a forecast but to a promise they never quite made, and it is hardly surprising that they approach the next estimate with even greater caution.

Beneath all of this lies a fact that is widely known and rarely said aloud: forecasting unfamiliar work is inherently unreliable. If a team has never built something before, it cannot know in advance where the difficulties will lie, and no amount of careful decomposition will change that. Perhaps the most useful thing an organisation could do is simply to acknowledge this openly—to treat an estimate for novel work as a statement of present ignorance rather than future certainty. The numbers would be no more accurate. They might, however, finally be trusted for what they are.

## Draft B

Ask most software teams whether they trust their own estimates and you'll get a knowing laugh. Yet they keep producing them, sprint after sprint. The reason isn't that engineers are bad at arithmetic. It's that estimates are doing a different job from the one they claim to do.

In practice, an estimate is often a negotiation. The business wants to know when something will be ready and how much it will cost; the engineers want enough room to do the work properly. The number that ends up on the plan is less a forecast than a settlement between those two pressures. Everyone involved half-knows this, which is exactly why nobody quite believes the result.

That arrangement can work tolerably well as long as the number stays what it is: a rough, shared guess. The trouble starts when it turns into a binding commitment. Once a figure appears in a roadmap or a customer conversation, it stops being an estimate and becomes a promise, and every later discovery that the work is bigger than expected is treated as a failure rather than as new information.

Underneath all of this sits an uncomfortable fact. Forecasting unfamiliar work is inherently unreliable. If a team has never built something quite like this before, it cannot know in advance where the hard parts are. Much of software development is exactly that kind of work. Yet this limitation is rarely acknowledged out loud, because saying it feels like refusing to cooperate.

The honest move is to name what an estimate is for, keep it separate from commitments, and say plainly how uncertain it is.

## Draft C

Why do software teams go on producing estimates that almost nobody in the room believes? You may have sat through a planning session in which a number was proposed, questioned, adjusted and finally accepted by people who each privately doubted it. It may be that an estimate, in most organisations, is not really a forecast at all but a negotiation.

The business needs a date to plan around; the engineers need room to do the work properly; the estimate becomes the place where those two needs meet and bargain. Seen in that light, the number is less a prediction than a settlement, and there is nothing especially wrong with that, provided everyone understands what kind of number it is.

The trouble begins when the settlement quietly turns into a commitment. A figure offered as a rough guess in one meeting reappears, a few weeks later, as a deadline in someone else's plan, stripped of the caveats that accompanied it. The engineers who gave it are then held to it as though it had been a promise, and they learn, not unreasonably, to pad their next estimate accordingly.

There is also a more basic difficulty, which is that forecasting unfamiliar work is inherently unreliable. If the work had been done before, it would not need doing again; the parts that matter most are precisely the parts nobody yet understands. I suspect most engineers know this, and most managers do too, and yet it is rarely said aloud, because saying it seems to excuse the team from any commitment at all.

Perhaps estimates would be trusted more if we asked less of them: not a promise about the future, but an honest account of how little of it we can see.

## Draft D

Why do software teams go on producing estimates that almost nobody involved really believes? The usual answer is that estimation is simply difficult, which is true, but it may be that the persistence of the practice has less to do with accuracy than with negotiation. An estimate is, in some sense, the opening position in a conversation between the business, which wants to know what it can promise, and the engineers, who want room to do the work properly; each side adjusts its number with the other in mind, and both understand, without quite saying so, that the figure is a bargaining token rather than a prediction.

There is nothing especially harmful in this, so long as everyone remembers what kind of number it is. The trouble begins when the estimate is written down, passed along and quietly converted into a commitment, at which point a guess made under uncertainty acquires the moral weight of a promise. You may have seen a team held to a date that no one on it ever thought realistic, simply because the date had been said aloud once and could not afterwards be withdrawn.

It seems to me that the more honest course is to acknowledge what most engineers already know, which is that forecasting unfamiliar work is inherently unreliable. We can estimate with some confidence the things we have done before; we cannot estimate the things we have not, because the very reason the work is unfamiliar is that we do not yet know what it contains. This is rarely said in planning meetings, arguably because saying it feels like an admission of incompetence. Perhaps the estimate we distrust is not a failure of engineering but a failure of candour.
