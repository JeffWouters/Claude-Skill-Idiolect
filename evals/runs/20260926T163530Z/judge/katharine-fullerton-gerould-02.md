# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-02

## The passage (by the author)

Strictly speaking, in Candide's "best of possible worlds" lies should not pass human lips. There are many people who stick to the literal interpretation of the precept: ladies, for example, who retire to the back porch before they permit their maids to tell the unwelcome caller that they are "out." There, presumably, they gaze at the blue sky, and congratulate themselves on their unimpeachable veracity. Yet even scrupulous people allow their servants to say they are out when they are in, because "out" is conventionally understood to mean many things. On the other hand, Mr. Chesterton tells us that, under certain conditions, mere silence is the most damnable lie of all. The matter is not so simple as it seems: its intricacies may become a morass for the unwary, and an enchanted garden for the casuist.

Very few people, I fancy, would say, after deliberation, that no lie was ever justified. To be sure, I once heard a serious young man protest that Shakespeare had damned Desdemona by allowing her, at her last gasp, to exculpate Othello. I have also known people who objected vehemently to the late Mark Twain because he said so many things that were not so. But there are occasions when lies are taken for granted, even by the law. A man on trial for his life is supposed to tell the truth, but not if it will incriminate him. A wife is not dragged to the witness-stand against her will to testify against her husband—no one would legitimately expect anything but perjury from her. I do not see much difference between legally permitting a man to say "Not guilty" when he is guilty, and legally permitting him to lie. Is there any solitary maiden lady who would not willingly give the midnight marauder to understand that her husband was just coming down the stairs, armed to the teeth? A man is not supposed, except by an extinct type of Puritan, to "give away" the lady who has made sacrifices for him; and even the extinct type of Puritan would hardly expect you to tell your hostess that her dinner-party had been dull. From this heterogeneous group of examples, one may infer that there are lies and lies; and while it is never permissible to lie, it is sometimes quite unpermissible to do anything else.

## Draft A

Is it ever right to lie? The moralists have always said no, and most of us, I think, would say no too if we were asked in church. But we are not often asked in church; we are asked at the front door, in the witness-box, at the dinner-table, and there the answer is not nearly so simple. One has only to watch a well-ordered household for a morning to see how much of its peace rests upon polite untruth. The servant who tells a caller that her mistress is not at home, when her mistress is upstairs all the while, deceives nobody and offends nobody. It is a convention, and every one understands it. Yet it is not, in the strictest sense, the truth. Nor is silence always innocent. A man may never utter a false word and still leave his hearers believing what is false—which is, I suppose, the most respectable way of lying there is.

The law, which ought to know better than any of us, is curiously tolerant here. A defendant is not required to condemn himself out of his own mouth; he may deny what he did, and no one thinks the worse of the court for letting him. A wife is not asked to testify against her husband. The courts do not, of course, call this lying. They call it something longer. But the principle is plain enough: there are loyalties which the law itself sets above the bare recital of fact.

And then there are the cases where deceit is not merely tolerated but required. If a burglar asks me where the silver is kept, I am under no obligation whatever to tell him; I am under some obligation, rather, to send him to the wrong cupboard. If a hostess has done her best and her dinner is a failure, the guest who tells her so has not served truth—he has only served himself. Some one will object that this is the thin end of the wedge. Granted; but the wedge was in long before we came. The point is not whether we shall ever lie, since we plainly shall, but whether we know why we are doing it. Honesty is not a rule. It is a discipline, and a harder one than the moralists allow.

## Draft B

Is lying ever justified? Most of us were raised on a simple answer: no. A lie is a lie, and a decent person does not tell one. The trouble begins the moment we look at how honest people actually live, because the plain rule turns out to have more exceptions than we like to admit.

Take the oldest domestic convention of all. A caller arrives, and the servant at the door says her mistress is not at home. Everyone involved knows this may not be literally true. The mistress may be upstairs with a book. Yet nobody calls the servant a liar, because the phrase has come to mean something else: she is not receiving visitors. A convention has bent strict truth, and we accept it without a second thought.

Silence complicates things further. We tend to think that if we say nothing false, we have stayed honest. But silence can deceive as well as any sentence. Letting someone go on believing something you know to be wrong, when a word from you would correct it, is a kind of deception by omission. Honesty is not only about what we say; it is about what we allow others to believe.

The law, which we might expect to be strict, is surprisingly tolerant. Courts do not expect a defendant to testify against himself, and they do not punish him for pleading not guilty when he is in fact guilty. Spouses are often excused from giving evidence against each other. In both cases the system has decided that some untruth, or at least some withholding of truth, is a price worth paying for other goods: fairness, the bond of marriage, the right not to be one's own accuser.

Then there are the cases where deceit seems not merely allowed but required. If a burglar asks whether anyone is home, or where the valuables are kept, no one thinks you owe him an accurate answer. Warding off harm with a falsehood looks less like a sin than a duty. And on a gentler scale, a guest who praises a disappointing meal is sparing a host's feelings, not corrupting the moral order.

None of this makes lying good. It means honesty is a harder discipline than a single rule can capture. It asks us to judge who is owed the truth, what the truth will do, and when kindness or safety has the stronger claim.

## Draft C

Is it ever right to lie? The moralists have always found the question easy, and the rest of us have always found it hard. I do not think we are merely weaker than the moralists; I think we are more honest about what we actually do. For the plain fact is that the whole machinery of polite life runs upon small departures from the literal truth, and runs, on the whole, smoothly. When the maid tells a caller that her mistress is not at home, nobody is deceived, and nobody is meant to be. It is a convention, as well understood on the doorstep as in the drawing-room. Yet if we are to be strict, it is not true. And silence, which the strict would have us take refuge in, is no safer. One may deceive quite as thoroughly by holding one's tongue as by wagging it; any one who has ever let a false impression stand, because it was convenient, knows that.

Nor is it only the drawing-room that bends. The courts themselves, which exist to find out the truth, do not expect it of every one. A man on trial is not required to tell against himself, and the law does not look very hard at him if he tells something less than the whole story. A wife is not asked to condemn her husband. We allow, in short, that there are loyalties which may outweigh the oath—or at any rate that it would be cruel, and useless, to demand the contrary.

But I would go further than this. There are times when deceit is not merely permitted but required of us. If a burglar asks me where the silver is kept, I shall not tell him, and I shall feel no scruple whatever in sending him to the wrong cupboard. If a hostess has given me a dinner I did not enjoy, I shall thank her warmly; to do otherwise would be a brutality, not a virtue. I dare say the moralists would call this weakness. It seems to me a recognition that truth is not the only good in the world, and that a man who sacrifices every other good to it—kindness, safety, the peace of the household—has not so much served the truth as made an idol of it. Honesty is complicated because life is complicated. The difficulty is not in knowing that we must sometimes lie; it is in knowing when.

## Draft D

Is it ever right to lie? The moralists say no, and say it very loudly; and I do not know how other people feel about the moralists, but I have always suspected that they kept a servant.

For consider the servant. Her mistress is at home, sitting upstairs with a novel, and does not wish to be disturbed. The bell rings. "Mrs. So-and-so is not at home," says the maid—and the caller, who knows perfectly well what the words mean, goes away quite unoffended. Nobody has been deceived, you will say; it is a convention. By all means let us call it a convention. But it is not the strict truth, and the maid, if she were a very literal-minded girl, might be excused for thinking her mistress a liar. One is ready to grant that conventions of this sort do no harm. Yet they bend the truth, and we all bend it with them.

Nor is speech the only offender. Silence can deceive as well as any sentence; indeed, it can do so more safely, for no one can quote it against you afterwards. The man who lets a wrong impression stand, when a word would correct it, has told his lie without opening his mouth.

The courts, too, which ought surely to be the last stronghold of truth, are curiously tolerant. They do not expect a defendant to condemn himself; they do not ask a wife to give evidence against her husband. The law, in other words, has made room for untruth—or at least for the withholding of truth—where human nature would find it intolerable to do otherwise. I cannot think who would wish it changed.

And there are cases where deceit is not merely excused but required. If a burglar asks me, in the dark, whether there is any one else in the house, I am not bound to tell him about my sleeping children; I should be a monster if I did. If my hostess asks me whether I have enjoyed a dinner which was, frankly, very bad, I shall say that I have enjoyed it very much—and I shall be right. To tell her the truth would be to wound her for no purpose but my own satisfaction in being exact.

So honesty, which looked so simple in the copy-book, turns out to be a complex and misty matter. It is not that we must lie; it is that we must know when the truth itself would be the greater wrong.
