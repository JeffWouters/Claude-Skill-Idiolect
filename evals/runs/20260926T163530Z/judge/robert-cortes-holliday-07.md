# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-07

## The passage (by the author)

And when I myself do get there I always feel that it is an unusual thing that I have allowed so long a time to lapse since I came before. Because it is so unusually pleasant and restful a ride that it makes me sore to think what an unusually deuce of a thing I am put to every night going home in the rush hour to Dyckman Street on the subway.

It is an unusual thing (or, at least, so it seems to me) that in Philadelphia cards in windows advertising rooms to let should be (as they are) labelled "Vacancies."

It is an unusual thing that here so many undertakers' shops should be conducted in what appear to be private residences. It is an unusual thing that there should be so many ways of paying your fare on the street cars—in some you pay when you get on, in others when you get off. It is an unusual thing that in Philadelphia there are more different kinds of street lamps than (I suspect) there are in any other city in the world. There are powerful arc lamps, high on tall poles, cold white in their light. There are lower down, particularly pleasant in the twinkle of their numbers in Washington Square, gas lamps glowing a mellow yellow through their mantles. Various other kinds of lamps, too. But the ones I like best are those squat fellows throughout Independence Square. Octagonal iron-bound boxes of glass, small at the base, wide at the top, with a kind of ecclesiastical derby hat of iron as a lid. They somehow suggest to me the lamps which I fancy before Will Shakespeare's Globe Theatre.

Here golden Diana with her bow does not poise high on her slender Spanish tower. But from far above the "Public Buildings" Brother Penn looks down on more banks, United States Mints, trust companies, firms dealing in securities, places handling investments, and such-like business concerns than (one has a feeling) can be found in any other city in Christendom. There are too, I should guess, in Philadelphia about as many different styles and periods of architecture as in any other municipality between the two great seas: Georgian, Colonial, bay-window, London brick row, ramshackle frame, modern mansion, skyscraper, etc., etc., etc. And certainly I don't know where one could go to count more different kinds of porches. Nor where one could find so many such pleasant oddities of today as hitching-blocks, doorway foot-scrapers, and those old friends of our childhood the front yard stone storks.

## Draft A

Going to Philadelphia from New York is, first of all, a rest. I say that as someone whose ordinary day begins in a crowded subway car, pressed between strangers, holding on and hoping. The trip down was the opposite of that. There was a seat, there was a window, there was time to read or simply look out and think about nothing. By the time I arrived I already felt kindly disposed toward the place, which may explain why I noticed so much about it.

The first oddity was the way rooms are advertised. [example needed] It is a small thing, but it is the small things that tell you you are no longer at home. Then there were the funeral homes. In New York you expect an undertaker to occupy a shopfront or a purpose-built establishment. Here, a good many of them seemed to be in houses, ordinary residential houses on ordinary streets, with nothing but a discreet sign to separate them from the neighbours. I found myself looking twice at every front door.

The fare system on the city's transport was another puzzle. [example needed] I worked it out in the end, though not quickly. And I have never seen a city with so many kinds of street lamp. Every few blocks the design seemed to change, as if each neighbourhood had been allowed to choose its own and none had wanted to copy another.

Philadelphia also abounds in financial firms. Banks, trust companies, brokers and the like seem to be everywhere, and they give certain streets a solemn, prosperous air. Next to them, and all around them, the buildings are a mixture of styles that would never be allowed to stand side by side if anyone had planned it. Old and new, plain and ornate, share the same block without apology. I found I liked this more than I expected. It felt honest, like a city that had grown rather than been designed.

What stayed with me most, though, were the porches and the yards. So many houses have porches, real ones, meant for sitting on. And in the yards I saw old-fashioned ornaments of a kind I thought had disappeared long ago. [example needed] They were not ironic. Nobody had put them there as a joke. They were simply part of the place, like the lamps and the fares and the funeral homes in houses.

I came back to New York and my crowded subway car, and for a day or two I missed all of it.

## Draft B

To the New Yorker who goes over to Philadelphia for the day the first agreeable thing about the place is the journey there. I do not say this unkindly. I mean that a man who is accustomed, morning and evening, to being packed into the subway with several hundred of his fellow citizens, all of them apparently standing upon his feet, finds a seat by a window in a railway coach a positively luxurious experience. He may read his paper. He may look at the landscape, or not, as he chooses. Nobody pushes him. He arrives rested, which is more than he is accustomed to do at his office, and in a frame of mind to notice things.

And there are, I found, a good many things to notice. There is, for instance, the manner in which rooms are advertised. [example needed: how the rooms are advertised] I do not know why this should strike one as curious, but it does. Then there are the funeral homes. Where I come from, one thinks of an undertaker as keeping a shop, more or less like other people; in Philadelphia, it appears, he is quite likely to keep a house, a residence with curtains at the windows, looking for all the world as though a family lived there—which, in a manner of speaking, perhaps one does. The fare system on the cars is a study in itself. [example needed: how the fares work] And I have never, anywhere, seen so many different kinds of street lamps; it is as if every generation of the city's history had put up its own and none had ever been taken down.

One is impressed, too, by the extraordinary number of financial firms. Banks, trust companies, brokers, investment houses: they seem to occupy every other doorway in the older part of town, and to have occupied it for a very long time. The architecture is, to put it mildly, mixed. Buildings of every period and every taste stand shoulder to shoulder, rather like guests at a large reception who have not been introduced.

But what I liked best were the porches. Philadelphia is a city of porches, and on many of them, or in the little yards before them, one comes upon those old-fashioned ornaments which I had supposed had long since vanished from civilisation: [example needed: which yard ornaments]. They have not vanished. They are merely in Philadelphia.

## Draft C

Going over to Philadelphia is, for a New Yorker, a kind of rest cure. Consider the case. Morning and evening, day in and day out, you have been packed into the subway like a herring into a tin, holding on by one hand to a strap and by the other to your hat, your nose in some stranger's newspaper, your feet upon some stranger's feet. And now you sit in a train, in a seat, a seat of your own, and you look out of a window at the country going by. Nobody leans on you. You could, if you liked, read a book. It is almost too much.

Philadelphia, when you arrive, has its own way of doing things, and some of these ways are curious to the visitor. Take the matter of rooms. In New York a room is advertised, if at all, in the newspaper. In Philadelphia rooms are advertised in a fashion of their own, [example needed], and one stops upon the sidewalk to consider it. Take the matter of undertakers. The undertaker in Philadelphia does not always keep a shop. He keeps a house—a house much like the houses on either side of it—and you do not know that you are passing a funeral home until you read the little sign. Take the matter of carfare. I shall not attempt to explain the fare system. I did not understand it. I paid what I was told, and I rode where I was taken. And take the matter of street lamps. I have never seen so many kinds of street lamps. There are lamps of this pattern and of that; there seems to be a lamp for every taste. It is as though the city could never make up its mind, and so decided to have them all.

It is a city, too, of banks and brokers and trust companies. Wherever you turn there is a financial house, solid and respectable, with its name cut in the stone. Money, you feel, is taken seriously here.

The architecture is a mixture. A very old building stands beside a very new one, and a building of no particular period at all stands beside them both. Nobody seems to mind. And the houses have porches. People sit upon the porches. In the little front yards, among the shrubbery, there are ornaments of a kind which I had supposed had gone out with the horse-car—[example needed]—standing there with perfect composure, as though nothing had happened in the world for some time. Perhaps nothing has.

## Draft D

To go to Philadelphia from New York is, for a man who rides the subway twice a day, very much like going on a holiday. You sit down. That in itself is remarkable. Nobody's elbow is in your ribs, nobody's newspaper is in your face, and nobody is standing on your feet. You look out of the window at the country going by, and you feel, somehow, that you have been let out of school. I dare say the trip is not really long enough to rest anybody. But it seems so, after the other thing.

Philadelphia, when you get there, is full of small surprises for the New Yorker. Take the manner in which rooms are advertised. [example needed: how the rooms are advertised] I had never seen that done before, and I confess it puzzled me for some time. Then there are the funeral homes. In New York an undertaker keeps a shop; in Philadelphia, as far as I could make out, he keeps a house, an ordinary dwelling-house with steps and a front door, like anybody's, and you would hardly know the difference except for the sign. The fare system on the cars is another curiosity, which I shall not attempt to explain, since I never fully understood it myself. And the street lamps! I don't believe there is another city in the country with so many kinds. There seemed to be a different pattern on every other corner, as though the town had been collecting them, so to speak, for a century or so and could not bear to part with any.

It is a city, too, that abounds in financial firms. Bankers, brokers, trust companies and the like appear to occupy a remarkable proportion of the ground in the business district, and they have a solid, prosperous, reserved look about them. The architecture is a thorough mixture. A severe old building of brick stands shoulder to shoulder with something florid and new, and neither seems in the least embarrassed by the other. Out in the residential streets there are porches, a great many porches, where one imagines the family sitting of a summer evening. And in the little yards before the houses you come upon ornaments which I had supposed had quite disappeared from the earth: [example needed: the yard ornaments], placed there in perfect seriousness. I found them delightful. A New Yorker is apt to smile at such things. Perhaps, after all, the joke is on the New Yorker.
