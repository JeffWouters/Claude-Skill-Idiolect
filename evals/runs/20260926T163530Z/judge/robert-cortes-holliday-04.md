# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-04

## The passage (by the author)

Now I had plumb forgotten about this barber's celebrity in fiction when the other day I entered this shop. And I was struck with embarrassment by the immediate attentions of so very distinguished a figure as that which sprang forward to assist me out of my coat. I thought surely this gentleman must be some kind of an Ambassador, who had perhaps mistaken me for the President. A slimmish man, obviously very French. Amazingly, overwhelmingly polite. Fine, a very fine beard. Long. Swept his chest. Pointed. Auburn. Wavy. Silken. Shot delicately with grey. Beautifully kept. Responded gently to the breeze—waving softly to and fro. A most beautiful beard—oh, my! And a glorious crown of hair! It rose from the line of its parting in a billowing wave, then fell with a luxuriant and graceful sweep to his ear. Only when he had tucked me in the chair could I realize that this must be the head barber. I had never before had the honor of being served by, or even of having seen himself, the proprietor here.

Then I mentioned Mr. Merwin's book. He took from a drawer several copies of The Saturday Evening Post, in which periodical the story had appeared serially, proudly to exhibit them to me. So it was we fell to chatting of his place. He had been here some sixteen or eighteen years. Before he had opened his shop this room had been several tiny rooms; Cleveland Moffett had for a time occupied them as a residence, and had here written his first book. My friend gayly produced a copy of an old magazine article by Mr. Moffett in which mention was given the shop.

## Draft A

We had read the novel, of course, and it was in something of the spirit of a pilgrim that we went to look at the barbershop which figures in it. One does not ordinarily approach a barbershop with reverence. This one, however, had been in literature, and that makes a difference. We opened the door rather timidly.

The proprietor came forward to receive us. He was a Frenchman, and a very elegant one; and he possessed a beard, and a head of hair, of a magnificence I shall not attempt to describe, for I should not be believed. He bowed. He inquired, in the most courteous terms, how he might be of service. His manner was so grand, so finished, so altogether distinguished, that for a moment we had the impression of having wandered by mistake into an embassy, and of being received by the ambassador himself. We explained, somewhat apologetically, that we had come on account of the book.

Nothing could have pleased him more. He retired to a back room and returned carrying, with great care, a pile of magazines, the very issues in which the novel had first appeared as a serial. These he laid before us one by one, turning the pages to the passages concerning his establishment, and watching our faces while we read. He was, it was evident, exceedingly proud of them, as another man might be proud of a decoration. Then, having put the magazines away again, he gave us the history of the shop from its beginning: who had kept it, and when, and what alterations had been made. He mentioned, among other things, that the premises had at one time been occupied by a writer, [name needed], who had lived there before ever it became a barbershop. He spoke of this as one speaks of an ancestor. It seemed to us that the place had a literary destiny, so to speak, and that it knew it.

## Draft B

I had read the novel, and so I went to see the barbershop, which I understood still stood where the book had put it. I expected a shop. I was not prepared for the proprietor.

He was a Frenchman, and elegant in a way that most men do not attempt after breakfast. His beard was extraordinary, full and shaped with the attention other people give to a garden, and his hair matched it. He came forward to greet me with such ceremony, such a gracious incline of the head and such a warm and formal welcome, that for a moment I thought I had come to the wrong address and walked into an embassy. I half expected him to present credentials. I found myself bowing slightly in return, which I do not ordinarily do in barbershops.

Once the greeting was concluded he went to fetch something, and came back with a small stack of magazines. These were the issues in which the novel had first appeared as a serial, and he laid them out for me with the pride of a man showing family portraits. He turned the pages to the parts that concerned his shop and let me look, and he watched my face while I did, to make sure I was properly impressed. I was.

Then he told me the history of the place. He told it well and at length, as a man does who has told it before and has improved it a little each time. The shop had been there for [length of time needed], and before it was a barbershop at all, he said, a writer had lived in that very space. He gestured around the room as he said it, at the chairs and the mirrors, as though the writer might still be somewhere among them, waiting for a trim.

I did not get my hair cut. It seemed, somehow, too small a thing to ask of a diplomat.

## Draft C

I had long wished to see the barbershop which figures so prominently in [title needed: the novel], and one afternoon I went. I had expected, I suppose, an ordinary shop with a little literary dust upon it. I was not prepared for the proprietor. He was a Frenchman, and a very elegant one, and he possessed a beard and a head of hair such as I have seldom seen upon any living man; they were not so much grown as cultivated. He came forward to receive me with so grand a courtesy, with such an inclination of the head and such a gracious movement of the hand, that I felt for a moment I had strayed into an embassy. One would have said that he was about to present his credentials. I had merely come, as it were, to look.

He understood at once, however, what I was about, and he was delighted. From somewhere at the back of the shop he produced, with evident pride, the numbers of the magazine in which the novel had first appeared as a serial. He handled them tenderly; he turned the pages for me himself, and he pointed out the passages in which his establishment was described. It was plain that he regarded these magazines as something between a family Bible and a patent of nobility.

Then, settling himself comfortably, he gave me the history of the place. He told it well, and at some length, as a man tells a story he has told many times and has come to enjoy. The shop, it appeared, had not always been a shop. Before his time, a writer had lived in these very rooms, [example needed: who the writer was], and had done his work here, perhaps in the corner where the chair now stands. My host seemed to feel that this earlier tenant had, in a manner, prepared the ground for the novel. I did not dispute it. In the presence of that beard one does not dispute anything.

## Draft D

Our acquaintance with the barbershop was—at first—merely literary. We had read of it, as a great many people have, in the pages of a novel, and it had not occurred to us that it was a real place, with real chairs in it, until somebody told us the street. So we went there one morning to be shaved.

The proprietor received us himself. He was a Frenchman, and a very elegant Frenchman. He had a beard—we find it difficult to denote that beard. It was not a large beard merely; it was a beard of character, of great distinction, full and silken and spread upon his chest like an order of merit. His hair was of a piece with it, abundant, lustrous, swept back from his brow in a manner not common among barbers. "Good morning," he said, and bowed; and he said it in such a way, with such a grave and gracious air, that we felt for a moment that we had come to present our credentials and were being received at an embassy. We were quite abashed. It seemed hardly proper to ask a diplomat for a shave.

Presently, when he had learned that we knew the novel, his manner warmed. He went to a cupboard and brought out, very carefully, a little stack of magazines. These were the numbers, he explained, in which the story had first appeared, in parts. He turned the pages for us himself. Here was his shop. Here, in so many words, was he. He was very proud of them, and we could see that they were not often allowed out of the cupboard.

Then he told us the history of the place. It had been a barbershop for [example needed] years. And before that, he said, before ever it was a barbershop, a writer had lived in those very rooms—[example needed]. He said it as one names an ancestor. We looked about us at the chairs and the mirrors and the bottles with a new respect. We felt that we had been shaved, so to speak, in good society.
