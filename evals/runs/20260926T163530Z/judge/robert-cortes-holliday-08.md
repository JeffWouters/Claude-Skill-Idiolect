# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-08

## The passage (by the author)

Yes; that's what the gentleman said in his very cordial letter: would I care to look over a "real mortician's establishment in New York City?" I replied that nothing could give me greater pleasure. So at the time appointed a couple of days later his car came round for me.

When I told people of the visit I was about to make, they all laughed, very heartily. Now that brings to one's attention a curious thing: why should they laugh? Honestly, between you and me, think hard and tell me what really there is funny about going to see a burial establishment?

Paradoxical indeed is the attitude of mind of practically everyone toward this subject of being ushered out of life. Sundry totally contradictory emotions are aroused in the very same person by slightly different aspects of the same subject. If you remark that you are going to spend the afternoon at the undertaker's that is awfully amusing. At the same time, is not nearly everyone down in his heart a bit scared of undertakers' shops? Uncomfortable, gruesome places, would not most of us feel, to have next door?

At any rate, as we glided along I was told by the gentleman who had come to fetch me that the feeling was very general that the presence of a funeral director's establishment depreciated the value of property in the immediate neighborhood. Though, he asserted, this popular idea frequently had not at all been borne out in fact.

It developed (from his lively conversation) that nothing so much annoys a funeral director, or a mortician, as for a visitor to pull old gags which he thinks are smart—such, for instance, as the remark: "I see your business is pretty dead." I gathered that this jocular pleasantry was the stock joke of all near-wits who visited undertakers—I mean morticians.

## Draft A

I had been invited to go through a large funeral establishment. I mentioned this to my friends. It was a mistake. They thought it the funniest thing they had heard in years. One of them wished to know whether I intended to stay. Another asked me to pick out something nice for him while I was there. They were at it all evening.

Now, this is a curious thing. Nothing is joked about more than death. It is, as one might say, the stock-in-trade of the comic papers. And yet the man whose business is death, the undertaker, is regarded by the same people with a kind of shrinking. They will laugh at the grave, but they will cross the street to avoid the man who digs it. Why this should be so I do not know. Perhaps the joke is a way of whistling in the dark, and the undertaker is the dark.

My host was a quiet, pleasant man. He showed me about. He was not in the least sepulchral. He explained to me, among other things, a matter of real estate. It is commonly believed, he said, that when a funeral home comes into a neighbourhood the value of property there goes down. People protest. They sign petitions. But the belief, he said, is very often wrong. He spoke of it without heat, as a man who has had to explain it many times and expects to explain it many times more.

Then he spoke of jokes. Visitors, he said, will make jokes. They will make the same jokes. They seem to think that they are the first to make them. He mentioned [example needed]. He did not laugh when he mentioned it. He had heard it, I gathered, a great many times. Funeral directors, it appears, do not care for puns upon their trade. They have heard them all. They are, if one may put it so, dead tired of them.

I thought it best not to tell him about my friends.

## Draft C

When I mentioned that I had been invited to tour a large funeral home, my friends thought it was the funniest thing they had heard all week. [example needed] The jokes went on for some time, and afterwards it struck me as odd. We will joke about death readily enough. We make light of it at parties and in conversation. Yet the people whose job it is to deal with death, the undertakers, make us uneasy, even afraid. We would rather not think about them, and we certainly would rather not live next to one.

That last point came up early in my visit. My host, who was courteous and entirely unembarrassed about his trade, explained that it is a common belief that a funeral home lowers the value of the property around it. People assume that no one wants to buy a house next to such a place, and so they oppose them when one is planned for their street. He said that this belief is often simply wrong. ## Draft D

I have been invited, by a gentleman in the business, to go over a large funeral home, and I mean to go. When I mentioned this to my friends they thought it the funniest thing they had heard in a month. [example needed: what the friends said] I laughed too, of course. But on the way home it occurred to me to wonder why it should be so very comic. We are perfectly willing to make jokes about death, which is a serious matter; yet the undertaker, who is merely the man who attends to the arrangements, we regard with a sort of shudder. We would rather not have him to dinner. We would decidedly rather not have him next door.

This last point, my host tells me, is a common belief and very often a mistaken one. People are convinced, almost everywhere, that an establishment of his kind must lower the value of every house, shop and lot in the neighbourhood. He says that it frequently does nothing of the sort.
