## The passage (by the author)

But even Jeremiah, when he was denouncing the evils that would befall his country, had a saving clause in his gloomy predictions. All manner of evils would befall them unless they repented, and humanly speaking he was of the opinion that they couldn't repent. Said he: "Can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard his spots? then may ye also do good that are accustomed to do evil." Nevertheless this did not prevent him from continually exhorting them to do good, and blaming them when they didn't do it. Like all great moral teachers he acted on the assumption that there is more freedom of will than seemed theoretically possible. It was the same way with his views of national affairs. Jeremiah's reputation is that of a pessimist. Still, when the country was in the hands of Nebuchadnezzar and he was in prison for predicting it, he bought a piece of real estate which was in the hands of the enemy. He considered it a good investment. "I subscribed the deed and sealed it, and called witnesses and weighed him the money in the balances." Then he put the deeds in an earthen vessel, "that they may continue many days." For in spite of the panic that his own words had caused, he believed that the market would come up again. "Houses and vineyards shall yet be bought in this land." If I were an archaeologist with a free hand, I should like to dig in that field in Anathoth in the hope of finding the earthen jar with the deed which Hanameel gave to his cousin Jeremiah, for a plot of ground that nobody else would buy.

It is the moralists and the reformers who have after all the most cheerful message for us. They are all the time threatening us, yet for our own good. They see us plunging heedlessly to destruction. They cry, "Look out!" They often do not themselves see the way out, but they have a well-founded hope that we will discover a way when our attention is called to an imminent danger. The fact that the race has survived thus far is an evidence that its instinct for self-preservation is a strong one. It has a wonderful gift for recovering after the doctors have given it up.

## Draft A

It is the common opinion that the prophet of doom is a gloomy fellow, and that the moral critic who tells us what is wrong with us has given us up. I am inclined to think the opposite is nearer the truth. The man who has really given us up does not trouble to say so. He goes about his own affairs and leaves us to ours. It is only the man who expects something of us who will take the pains to be disagreeable.

Consider Jeremiah. No one ever had a worse reputation for cheerfulness. He foretold ruin in such detail that his name has become a word for complaint. Yet in the midst of his lamentations he was urging his people to repent, which is a waste of breath unless one believes repentance may do some good. And when the enemy held the land, and the whole country was, by his own account, as good as lost, he went out and bought a field. He had the deed properly witnessed and put away in a safe place, as a man does who means to have use of it. That is not the conduct of a pessimist. It is the conduct of a man who has read the worst of the present and has still a very good opinion of the future. He only declined to be hurried.

The reformer is of the same temper. When he tells us that we are on the edge of a precipice, he is paying us a compliment, though we are seldom in the mood to receive it as one. He assumes that we can see the precipice when it is pointed out, and that, having seen it, we will have the wit to step back. Nobody shouts a warning to a stone. The very violence of his language is a measure of his confidence. He would not raise his voice so high if he did not think we could hear.

And, after all, he has the facts on his side. The human race has been warned a great many times, and it is still here. It has fallen into more pits than the critics have been able to count, and it has a way of climbing out, a little muddy, but in fair spirits. Whatever else may be said of mankind, it has a remarkable knack of recovery. The prophet knows this, and that is why he prophesies.

## Draft B

We are in the habit of dividing mankind into optimists and pessimists, and of placing the prophets of doom, together with the whole company of moral critics, securely on the gloomy side of the line. The classification is convenient, but I am not sure that it is just. A man who spends his days assuring us that we are headed for ruin is not, as a rule, a man who has given us up. He would hardly take the trouble if he had.

Consider Jeremiah. No one ever foretold disaster with more energy, or with a more complete disregard of the feelings of his audience. Yet in the midst of his lamentations he never ceased to urge repentance, which is a strange thing to urge upon a people for whom there is no hope. When the enemy held the land, he bought a field in it. That was not the transaction of a speculator looking for a quick return. It was the act of a man who trusted that the country would one day be recovered, and who was willing to invest his money where his prophecy did not seem to be.

The reformers of our own day are of the same temper. They come to us with long catalogues of our sins and very short catalogues of our virtues, and we are naturally inclined to resent them. Yet a warning is itself a kind of compliment. Nobody shouts "Fire!" to a house that is already in ashes; he shouts because he believes the people inside can still get out. The reformer rings his alarm bell on the comfortable assumption that we have intelligence enough to find the door once we are thoroughly awake. His gloom is only the shadow cast by an extraordinary confidence in human nature.

There is, perhaps, some ground for that confidence. The race has survived a remarkable succession of calamities, most of which were announced beforehand, by persons of undoubted authority, as final. It has been conquered, plagued, misgoverned and preached at. It is still here. Whatever else may be said of humanity, it has shown a strong knack for recovery.

When the next prophet rises to tell us that all is lost, we need not be too much cast down. We may listen to him with respect, and even with a certain gratitude, as to a physician who would not bother to describe the disease if he thought it incurable. He is, underneath, the most hopeful man in the room.

## Draft C

We tend to think of the prophet of doom as the gloomiest person in the room. He stands at the gate and tells the city it will fall; he points at our habits and says they will ruin us. It is easy to hear only the ruin. But listen a little longer and something else comes through. The man who shouts a warning is, underneath, the most hopeful of us all.

Take Jeremiah. No one foretold disaster more thoroughly. He told his people that their city would be taken and that they had brought it on themselves. Yet in the same breath he urged them to repent, and repentance only makes sense if there is still something to be saved. More telling still is what he did with his own money. While the enemy held the country, he bought a field. It is hard to imagine a worse investment by any ordinary reckoning. He bought it anyway, because he trusted that the land would one day be worked again and that deeds and boundaries would matter once more. The prophet of ruin put his silver on recovery.

The moral critics and reformers of our own day are made of the same stuff. They tell us that we are wasting, poisoning, neglecting, that we cannot go on as we are. The tone is often severe, and the lists of our failings are long. But nobody bothers to warn a person he believes is beyond help. You do not shout at a man walking toward a cliff unless you expect him to hear you and step aside. The reformer's alarm is a compliment in disguise: it assumes that once we are alerted we will find the way out. His despair is a method; his real conviction is that we are capable of better.

And on the evidence, he has reason. Humanity has come through a great deal: [example needed]. Again and again the worst has been predicted, and again and again people have found some means of mending, rebuilding and carrying on. Our survival so far is not proof that we will always escape, but it does show a strong knack for recovery.

So the next time a critic tells us we are doomed, we might hear the other half of his message. He is buying a field in occupied country.

## Draft D

It is commonly supposed that the prophet of doom is a melancholy person who takes a secret satisfaction in our calamities. I have never found it so. The man who goes about telling us that we are on the road to ruin is, underneath his lamentations, the most hopeful of us all, for he would not take the trouble to warn us if he believed the case were altogether beyond remedy. Nobody shouts at a man who has already gone over the precipice.

Take Jeremiah, who has given his name to a whole literature of complaint. He foretold the ruin of his people with a thoroughness that left nothing to the imagination, and yet in the same breath he urged them to repent, which is an invitation no one extends to the hopeless. When the enemy held the land, he did a thing that must have seemed to his neighbours the height of folly. He bought a field. It was not a promising time for such an investment, but Jeremiah was looking beyond the siege to the day when his countrymen would again plant and reap in security. His prophecy said one thing; his purchase said another, and I am inclined to think the purchase was the truer utterance.

It is so with the moral critics of our own time. The reformer tells us that our institutions are rotten, that our amusements are degrading, and that the younger generation is going to the bad, and he says it with an earnestness that is rather alarming. But why should he say it at all? He warns us because he expects that, once we are alerted to the danger, we will discover some way of escape which he himself may not be able to point out. His severity is really a compliment to our intelligence.

Nor is his confidence without foundation. When one considers the number of occasions on which the human race has been on the point of destruction, one is impressed by the circumstance that it is still here. It has survived its wars and its pestilences and, what is perhaps more remarkable, its own follies, and such a record argues an extraordinary knack for recovery. The prophets have generally been mistaken about the end of the world, but they have been right about something more important. They have believed that we could be saved.
