# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-10

## The passage (by the author)

So I would write an article In Praise of Streets.

As I was saying (when that minister switched me off), I was strolling along the Embarcadero. Among all the different sorts of streets there are none I think more beguiling than those which lie along the water front of a town or a city. The water-front streets of all seaport cities, of course, partake very much of the same character. Particularly in the picturesque aspect of the shop windows.

Here along the rim of San Francisco Bay you pass the sparkling pier buildings (now and then of Spanish mission architecture) of the Toyo Kisen Kaisha Oriental S. S. Co., of the American Hawaiian S. S. Co., the Kosmos Line, and the Pacific-Alaska Navigation Co., among others. While on New York's West Street you see the structures of the White Star Line, the Cunard Line, the Red Star Line, erected in masonry of a sort of mammoth and glorified garage architecture, funnels and masts peeping over the top; and further down the frame sheds of the Morgan Line, the Clyde Steam Ship Company, Savannah Line, Lackawanna Rail Road, Hoboken Ferry, and so on. But the tastes of the sailor man as a shopper appear to be very much the same whether he is along the London docks, on West Street, by Boston piers or here on the Embarcadero. In this the West and the East do meet.

The æsthetic taste of the water front inclines, very decidedly, to the ornate. As (presumably) a present to a lady and a decoration for the home the favorite object seems to be a heavy china plate. A romantic landscape, or a moonlight scene, or perhaps a still life study of portly roses is "hand painted" in very thick pigment on its face. Its rim is plaited in effect, like the edge of a fancy pie, and through numerous openings in this rim is run a heavy ribbon by which to hang it on the wall.

## Draft A

There is something about a harbor street that draws people in, even people who have no business with ships. The streets that run along the water have a character of their own, rougher and livelier than the rest of the city, and they seem to belong less to the town behind them than to the sea in front. San Francisco's waterfront is one of the best of them.

Along the bay stand the piers of the shipping companies, one after another, each with its name painted large across the front. Anyone who knows New York will be reminded of the steamship buildings that line Manhattan's Hudson shore, the same row of broad sheds facing the water, the same sense that the whole world is only a voyage away. [example needed] Walking past them, you feel the pull of departure, whether or not you have any intention of going anywhere.

Across the street from the piers are the shops that serve the sailors, and here is a curious thing: they look the same in every port city. The window you pass in San Francisco could have been lifted from any other harbor town and set down here without anyone noticing. The same goods, arranged in the same way, wait for the same kind of customer, a man just ashore with money in his pocket and a few days to spend it.

What those shops sell leans heavily toward the showy. Nothing is quiet or plain if it can be made bright instead. Above all there are the plates: thick, heavy decorative plates, painted in strong colors, each one hung up by a length of ribbon so that it can go straight onto a wall. [example needed] They are not meant for eating from. They are meant to be carried home across an ocean and hung up as proof that the sailor has been somewhere and remembered someone. There is a whole philosophy of the sea in those plates, and in the streets that sell them: a taste for color, for keepsakes, and for something to show when the voyage is over.

## Draft B

I have always had a weakness for the streets that run along a harbour, and I am not certain that any of them pleases me more than the waterfront of San Francisco. There the piers of the shipping companies stand in a long row against the bay, each with its name painted large across the front, each looking out upon the water with a kind of dignified expectancy. A New Yorker, strolling there, is reminded at once of the steamship buildings along the Hudson shore of Manhattan, and he feels, I suppose, rather more at home than he had anticipated. The resemblance is not an accident. Ships are ships, and the business of receiving them seems to produce the same architecture wherever it is conducted.

The same is true, and even more remarkably, of the sailors' shops. You may walk into one of these establishments in any port city you please, and you will find it precisely like the one you visited in the last. I don't pretend to understand why this should be; it is simply one of those things. The window, the counter, the proprietor, the general atmosphere of an old sea chest recently opened: all are identical. [example needed: another port city visited]

What particularly distinguishes these places is their taste in merchandise. Nothing modest is admitted. The sailor, it appears, when he goes ashore with money in his pocket, desires something showy, something that will be recognised as a present from a considerable distance. And the shops have accommodated him. Above everything else they offer plates: thick, heavy, decorative plates, painted in the most enthusiastic colours, with a hole bored through the rim and a ribbon passed through the hole so that the thing may be hung upon a wall. They are not intended to be eaten from. I doubt whether anybody has ever eaten from one. They hang in rows about the walls of the shop, glowing, and they are, in their way, entirely irresistible.

## Draft C

Speaking of streets, there is nothing, to my mind, quite like the streets that run along a harbour. I do not know precisely why this should be so. They are not, as a rule, handsome streets. They are not clean. Nobody who has any business elsewhere goes near them. And yet I have never been in a port city without being drawn down to the water, as surely as if somebody had tied a string to my coat.

San Francisco's waterfront is a case in point. Along the bay, one after another, stand the piers of the shipping companies, each with its name painted up large, as though it were afraid you might mistake it for some other company. If you have walked along the Hudson shore of Manhattan you will know the sort of thing I mean; there, too, the steamship buildings stand in a row facing the river, and there, too, they have a great air of importance about them. The two waterfronts are a continent apart, but I could not honestly tell you, with my eyes shut, which of them I was on.

Then there are the sailors' shops. I have observed a certain outstanding fact about sailors' shops which I have not seen commented upon before. They are all the same shop. Whether you meet one in San Francisco or in New York or in [example needed], it is the same window, with the same things in it, and very likely the same man behind the counter. Just so. The sailor, it seems, goes round the world in order to find the store he left behind him.

And what is in the window? Showy things, chiefly. Nothing in a sailors' shop is modest. Above all there are the plates: thick, heavy plates, painted over in the brightest colours, with a bit of ribbon through a hole at the top so that you may hang them upon the wall. Why, you wouldn't think anybody would eat off them. Nobody does. They are not for eating; they are for having.

## Draft D

I have a weakness, I confess, for streets that run along the water. Put me down in any town that has a harbour and I will find my way to it, sooner or later, without anybody's assistance. There is something about the edge of a city, where the pavement stops and the shipping begins, that I have never been able to resist. And of all such streets the San Francisco waterfront is, to my mind, one of the most agreeable. [detail needed: a first-hand observation of the waterfront]

Along the bay stand the piers of the various shipping companies, one after another, each with its name across the front in letters large enough to be read from the deck of a passing vessel. They put one, naturally, in mind of the steamship buildings which line the Hudson shore of Manhattan. The resemblance is considerable. There is the same procession of great sheds, the same traffic of drays and trucks at the entrances, the same air of important business being done by somebody else.

But what I particularly enjoy are the sailors' shops. Now, it is a remarkable thing about sailors' shops that they are exactly alike in every port in the world. I have not, it is true, visited every port in the world; but I am quite prepared to believe it. You could be set down blindfolded before one of these establishments, have the bandage removed, and not know whether you were on the Pacific or the Atlantic. The merchandise, so to say, speaks one universal language.

It is not, I should explain, a language of restraint. The sailor, when he comes ashore with money in his pocket, does not want anything quiet. He wants something that will show. And so the windows are crowded with articles of the most conspicuous description. Chief among these, and most characteristic, is the decorative plate: a heavy, thick affair, painted in the brightest colours obtainable, and hung up by a ribbon. Why a sailor should want a plate to hang on the wall I cannot tell you. Perhaps that is the charm of it.
