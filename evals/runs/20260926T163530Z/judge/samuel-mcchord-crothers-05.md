# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-05

## The passage (by the author)

But to the Englishman this attitude seems somewhat superstitious. The period of opposition to a measure is not ended when it has passed Parliament and received the royal assent. The question is whether it will receive the assent of the people. Can it get itself obeyed? If it can, then its future is assured for many generations. But it must pass through an exciting period of probation.

If it is a matter that arouses much feeling the British way is for some one to disobey and take the consequences. Passive resistance—with such active measures as may make the life of the enforcers of the law a burden to them—is a recognized method of political and religious propagandism.

In periods when the national life has run most swiftly this kind of resistance to what has been considered the tyranny of lawmakers has always been notable. Emerson's "the chambers of the great are jails" was literally true of the England of the seventeenth century. Every one who made any pretension to moral leadership was intent on going to jail in behalf of some principle or another.

John Bunyan goes to jail rather than attend the parish church, George Fox goes to jail rather than take off his hat in the presence of the magistrate. Why should he do so when there was no Scripture for it? When it was said that the Scripture had nothing to say about hats, he was ready with his triumphant reference to Daniel III, 21, where it is said that the three Hebrew children wore "their coats, their hosen, their hats and their other garments" in the fiery furnace. If Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego wore their hats before Nebuchadnezzar and kept them on even in the fiery furnace, why should a free-born Englishman take his hat off in the presence of a petty Justice of the Peace? Fervent Fifth Monarchy men were willing to die rather than acknowledge any king but King Jesus who was about to come to reign. Non-juring bishops were willing to go to jail rather than submit to the judgment of Parliament as to who should be king in England. Puritans and Covenanters of the more logical sort refused to accept toleration unless it were offered on their own terms. They had been a "persecuted remnant" and they proposed to remain such or know the reason why.

## Draft A

There is an old English notion, which the lawyers do not much encourage, that a statute is not quite a law until the people have made up their minds to obey it. It has passed the Houses and received the royal assent, and it stands in the books in very good type. But it is on probation. Until the public has settled down under it, it remains a kind of proposal, and those who dislike it are at liberty to decline the proposal, provided they are willing to pay for the privilege. The Englishman who holds this view does not skulk. He does not evade the law in the dark; he breaks it in the daylight, and then presents himself to be punished with the air of a man who is keeping an appointment.

It is an expensive way of expressing an opinion, and for that reason it is not often abused. The man who argues that a law is bad may be only clearing his throat. The man who goes to prison rather than keep it has at least made his meaning plain.

The seventeenth century was rich in such plainness. The dissenters of that age had a great many opinions, and they had the inconvenient habit of acting upon them. Bunyan might have had his liberty at any time by the simple device of holding his tongue. He preferred to keep his tongue and lose his liberty, and it must be confessed that the world has been the gainer by the bargain. Fox was of the same obstinate temper. The authorities offered him conformity on easy terms, and he found the terms too easy to be trusted. He went to prison instead, and seemed to regard it as a reasonable place for an honest man to be.

What I most admire in Fox is not his courage, which was common enough among his people, but his way of finding authority for it. When he was taken to task for keeping his hat on in the presence of his betters, he did not plead his own conscience only. He appealed to the three Hebrews in Daniel, who were thrown into the furnace with their hats on. It was a good precedent, and he knew it. A magistrate may overrule a Quaker, but he hesitates to overrule the Book of Daniel. And so the hat stayed where it was, and the law, which had not yet been obeyed, had to wait a little longer to become a law.

## Draft B

We are accustomed to think of a law as finished business once it has passed through Parliament, been signed and sealed, and set down in the proper books, so that nothing is left for the citizen but to find out what it says and do it. The English have never taken this view of the matter. A statute, as they understand it, is only a proposal until the public has made up its mind to keep it. It becomes secure when people obey it without thinking. Not a day before.

Until that day comes there is a recognised way of objecting. The man who dislikes the law does not plot against it in the dark. He breaks it in the open, tells the magistrate why, and goes to prison with a good conscience and, as often as not, a good appetite. It is a costly form of argument, and for that reason a persuasive one. A government can answer a pamphlet with another pamphlet, a speech with a longer speech, and a riot with soldiers, but it has no ready reply to a man who would sooner sit in gaol than do what he thinks wrong.

The seventeenth century was rich in such arguments. The dissenters of that age did not ask to be let off. When the choice lay between conformity and prison, men like Bunyan and Fox chose prison. They were not rebels in the ordinary sense. They had no wish to pull down the state; they only declined to let the state tell them how to pray. A jailer might well find them trying company, for a prisoner who is content to be in prison takes away most of the satisfaction of keeping him there.

Fox had a particular quarrel about his hat. He would not take it off where custom required it, and when he was asked to explain himself he did not appeal to the liberties of Englishmen or to any clause in any charter, but went back to the three Hebrews in Daniel and found his precedent there. The argument was perhaps not one that would satisfy a court of law. But it satisfied Fox.

That is the peculiar strength of the tradition. The law is not defied by a mob with torches but by one man with his hat on, who is willing to be punished and entirely unwilling to change his mind.

## Draft C

There is an old English habit, older than most of our institutions and a good deal hardier, of meeting a bad law not with argument alone but with open refusal. It is worth understanding what that habit assumes, because it assumes something about law itself that the textbooks tend to leave out.

A statute, once passed, looks finished. It has been debated, voted, signed and printed. Yet in a real sense it is not secure until the public obeys it. Until that moment it is a claim rather than a settled fact, and the claim can be contested. Those who think it unjust have a choice that is not quite rebellion and not quite submission: they may decline to comply, say plainly that they decline, and take whatever punishment the law provides. They do not hide, and they do not fight the constable at the door. They simply let the state discover how much it will cost to make the law real.

The seventeenth century is full of such people. The dissenters of that age were told how to worship, where to preach and whom to acknowledge, and a great many of them would not do it. Bunyan is the familiar case, and Fox beside him. Both could have bought their freedom with a little conformity. Both chose prison instead, and not as a gesture made once and then regretted, but as a settled course. The prison became, in effect, their reply to the statute. Every month they spent there was a public statement that the law had not yet won.

Fox gives the tradition its most characteristic flavour, because the thing he refused to do was so small. He would not take off his hat. To the magistrates this looked like mere insolence, and they treated it so. Fox did not answer them with a theory of civil liberty. He answered with Scripture, pointing to the three Hebrews in Daniel, who stood before the king's command and would not bend to it. The appeal was shrewd. It placed his stubbornness inside a story every listener knew and most of them admired, and it made the bench look rather less like guardians of order and rather more like the king in that story.

That, I think, is the heart of the thing. Open disobedience does not deny the authority of law. It puts that authority on trial, in public, and lets the country decide.

## Draft D

There is an old English habit of dealing with an unwelcome law which the law-makers themselves have never quite understood. It is not the habit of the conspirator, who works in the dark and hopes not to be found out, but of the man who stands in the open, announces plainly that he will not obey, and then waits patiently for the constable. He does not ask to be excused; he asks only to be punished, and he takes a certain quiet satisfaction in the transaction.

The theory is simple enough. A statute is not secure merely because it has been passed by a majority and printed in a book with the other statutes. It is secure only when the public obeys it, and until that time it is on trial, so that those who dislike it may defy it openly, provided they are willing to accept whatever punishment the courts can devise. The law has its penalty and the objector has his conscience, and it remains to be seen which will tire first. I do not know that this is sound political philosophy. It has a way of working.

The dissenters of the seventeenth century understood the method very well. Bunyan and Fox and many humbler men were offered an easy way out, for they had only to conform, and instead they chose the prison, where they seem to have found, on the whole, a tolerable place in which to think. [example needed: a scene from their imprisonment] One cannot argue very long with a man who prefers the jail to your arguments. After a while it is the argument that begins to look foolish.

Fox had a particular difficulty about his hat. He would not take it off in the presence of magistrates, and when they demanded his reasons, he did not appeal to the ancient liberties of Englishmen but cited instead the three Hebrews in Daniel, who kept their hats on in the fiery furnace. It was perhaps not the strongest point in the law of evidence. But it was hard to answer, and the magistrates, who knew their Bible as well as he did, must have felt it.

We are apt to smile at the hat. Yet the hat was the whole question in miniature; a people who will go to prison over a hat will not long be governed by statutes they have not accepted, and in the end it is the statutes that have had to come round.
