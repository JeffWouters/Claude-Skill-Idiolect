## The passage (by the author)

And the "galley slaves," those poor devils who for years have lived under the whip of copy day every day. How they dream of the "real" things they might do, given time. If (they think) the Lord would only subsidize them! Now and then the Devil takes one of them and does this very thing. The happy man gets some sort of a sinecure. All he has to do is to go write. And (in all probability) that's all there is to that story. He is like those things Riley tells about who "swaller theirselves." He gets nothing written.

What do you write with? And why do you write with whatever it is you write with instead of with something else? Why did Mr. Howells (in all the writing of his which I have seen) use a script-letter typewriter instead of a Roman-letter machine? Why does Mr. Le Gallienne do so much of his copy (if not all of it) by hand? Why is it that Mr. Huneker could never either dictate or learn to run a typewriter? How is it possible for those Englishmen—Swinnerton and Bennett, for instance—to put forth in a few months whole novels in the monkish hand of an illuminated missal? (I have seen the original manuscript of "The Old Wives' Tale," every page like a copper-plate engraving, and hardly a correction throughout.) And why is that it seems to me most natural to write some things with a pen, others with a pencil, most things on a typewriter, and yet again mix the use of all three implements in one composition? I cannot tell you.

## Draft A

Ask any overworked journalist what he wants most, and he will tell you: time. Time to think, time to read, time above all to write the book he has been carrying around in his head for years. He pictures the empty weeks ahead of him, the quiet desk, the long mornings with nothing to interrupt him. Then, now and then, somebody gives him exactly that. And very often nothing comes of it. The weeks pass, the desk stays quiet, and the book stays in his head. It seems that for some of us the pressure of a deadline is not the enemy of the work but the thing that makes it happen at all. Take the pressure away and the machinery stops.

The tools a writer uses are just as personal as the conditions he works under. Some authors will write only by hand, trusting the slow movement of the pen to keep pace with their thinking. Others cannot imagine working without a typewriter and the steady rattle of its keys. Some dictate, walking up and down the room while another person takes down the words. And a good many use a mix of all of these: a first draft in pencil, corrections in ink, a clean copy on the machine. [example needed] There is no right method, only the one that happens to work for a particular person on a particular day.

Place matters too, and here writers split into two camps. There are those who must live in the middle of what they describe. They need the streets outside the window, the voices in the next room, the smell and noise of the world they are putting on paper. Without that, the writing goes thin. And then there are those who have to go far away before they can write about home at all. Distance gives them the clarity that closeness takes away; only from a foreign room can they see their own town plainly. [example needed] Neither camp is wrong. Each writer has to find out, usually the hard way, which one he belongs to.

## Draft B

Every newspaper man I have ever known has told me, at one time or another, what he would accomplish if only he had a little leisure to himself. He would write the book. He has carried it about in his head for years; it is all there, he explains, excepting the mere mechanical business of putting it down. What he requires is a month, or a summer, away from the city desk and the telephone and the managing editor and the continual demand for another paragraph before the paper goes to press. Well, now and again somebody presents him with the month. Nothing whatever comes of it. He sits in an agreeable room with a considerable quantity of clean paper, and he looks out of the window, and he sharpens his pencils, and he goes out for a walk, and the book does not arrive. The thing he needed, it appears, was not freedom at all, but somebody standing over him.

The instruments of the trade are another curious matter. One author will tell you that he cannot think except with a pen in his hand; the ink, he believes, is somehow connected with his intelligence. Another has never produced a line that was not pounded out upon a typewriter. A third walks up and down the room and dictates, which strikes me as rather like proposing marriage through an interpreter. And there are those who employ everything by turns: the pen for the first draft, the pencil for the corrections, the machine for the fair copy. I don't know that it makes a particle of difference to the reader.

Then there is the question of where. Some writers, I am informed, must live in the very middle of whatever it is they are describing, and cannot compose a paragraph about a place they have not breathed and eaten and slept in. Others, quite as distinguished, can't see their own town at all until they have put a considerable distance between themselves and it. [example needed: a writer who wrote of home from far away] Possibly it is only a matter of temperament, like the pen and the typewriter.

## Draft C

Consider the case of the overworked journalist. He has, as he will tell you any evening you care to listen, no time. Early and late, week in and week out, he is at the beck and call of the city desk, and the thing he longs for above all else is a little leisure. Give him but a month of it, he says, and he will write the book that is in him. Very well. Suppose for the sake of our discussion that he gets the month. I have observed a number of such cases, and I have noticed one outstanding fact about them which I do not recall having seen commented upon before. At the end of the month there is no book. There is not, as a rule, so much as a chapter. There is a very clean desk and a great deal of sharpened pencils. Just so. It takes a good deal of being busy to get anything done.

Then there is the question of tools. One author will have nothing to do with anything but his own handwriting; to him a machine is an affront. Another cannot think at all except to the sound of a typewriter. A third walks up and down the room and dictates, which strikes me as a very grand way to go about it. And a fourth (as the case may be) uses a pen for this, a pencil for that, and the machine for the rest, and could not tell you why if you asked him. I have heard that each of them believes his own way to be the only sensible one.

And finally there is the matter of where. Some writers, it appears, must live right in the thick of the thing they are writing about. They must smell it and hear it at all hours. Others, when they wish to write about home, go as far away from it as they can readily get. Why, you would think they could not see the place at all until they had put an ocean between themselves and it. [example needed] It is very curious, but there it is.

## Draft D

Every newspaper man of my acquaintance longs for leisure. He will tell you so at any hour of the day or night, and particularly at the hour when the city editor sends him out again on some errand of no consequence. If only he had a little time to himself, he says, he would produce the thing he has always intended to produce. Well, give it to him. Give him a month, a summer, an entire year of unoccupied days. What happens? Nothing whatever happens. He sits down before a clean sheet of paper, and the clean sheet of paper, so to speak, looks back at him; and presently he gets up and goes out for a walk. It seems that a considerable number of men do their best work only when they have not the time to do it. [example needed: a real instance of a journalist given free time who produced nothing]

Then there is the question of implements. One author will accept nothing that does not come from his own hand, slowly, in ink. Another cannot think at all except at the typewriter, and he pounds away at the machine as though it owed him money. A third walks up and down the room and dictates to a stenographer. And I suppose there are some (I suspect they are the majority) who employ a little of everything: a pen for the beginning, a pencil for the middle, and the machine when the manuscript has got to be finished and sent off. Which method is the correct one? I don't know that anybody knows.

Place is altogether another matter. Certain writers, it appears, must live in the very thick of whatever they describe. They must hear the conversation in the street and smell the cooking in the hallways, or they cannot write a line about any of it. Others do precisely the reverse. They travel as far away as they can manage, to some foreign country perhaps, and there, with the whole width of the ocean between themselves and home, they write about home better than they ever did while they lived in it. It is a curious business, literature.
