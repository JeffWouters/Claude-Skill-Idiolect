# Judge packet synthetic-idris-08

## The passage (by the author)

What is a conversation when the people having it are never awake at the same moment? The conventional answer is that it is simply a slower version of the ordinary kind, in which a question is left in the evening and an answer is found in the morning, and the only real cost is a little delay. Most of the advice written about distributed teams follows from that assumption; it concerns overlap hours, handover notes and the careful scheduling of the few meetings that everyone can reasonably attend. Yet anyone who has worked this way for long knows that something more than speed is altered, since a message written to a colleague who is asleep is not quite a message at all but a small letter, composed without the correcting glance of a listener. I suspect that working across time zones is less a problem of logistics than a problem of etiquette, and that the difficulties we attribute to distance are, in some sense, difficulties of manners that nobody has yet written down.

Consider the experience of opening your laptop in the morning to find that the working day on the other side of the world has already happened without you. There is a long thread of messages, a decision that seems to have been reached, a question addressed to you that was asked in hope and has since been answered, less satisfactorily, by someone else. You may recognise the faint sensation that follows, which is perhaps part relief and part exclusion; the work has moved on, and your contribution, if it comes now, will arrive as a reopening rather than a reply. The colleagues who wrote those messages were not trying to leave anyone out—they had a deadline, a customer, a deployment that would not wait—and yet the effect is that one group quietly becomes the place where things are decided and the other becomes the place where things are reviewed. Is it any wonder that people in the later time zone often begin to write defensively, anticipating objections they will not be awake to hear?

## Draft A

How should a team talk to itself when half of it is asleep? The usual advice on distributed work treats asynchronous communication as ordinary conversation made slower, and so it concentrates on scheduling: finding the hours of overlap, rotating the inconvenient meetings, making sure that nobody is always the one dialling in at an unreasonable hour. These suggestions are sensible enough, and I would not argue against any of them. It may be, however, that they address the wrong difficulty, since the problem with writing to colleagues who are asleep is not chiefly that their reply will be late.

What a message sent across time zones lacks is the live feedback that ordinary conversation provides without our noticing. In a meeting, or even at the next desk, we see at once when a remark has landed badly, and we can soften it or explain it before any harm is done; in a message read many hours later, the remark simply stands, and the reader must decide alone what we meant by it. I suspect this makes the matter less one of logistics than of etiquette, and an etiquette that is almost never written down, so that each team arrives at its own conventions by accident and each newcomer must learn them by trial and error.

There is also a quieter imbalance that the scheduling advice tends to overlook. Colleagues in the later time zone often wake to find that decisions have already been made in their absence, so that one region is, in effect, always deciding while the other is always reviewing. You might expect this to produce resentment, and perhaps it sometimes does; more often, I think, it produces a particular style of writing, careful and defensive, in which every message anticipates the objections it cannot wait to hear. It seems to me that a team which noticed this pattern, and agreed openly on which kinds of decision should wait for everyone, would do more for its distributed members than any rearrangement of the calendar.

## Draft B

Most advice about distributed teams treats time zones as a scheduling problem. Find the hours of overlap, protect them, put the important meetings there, and let everything else happen asynchronously. Underneath that advice is an assumption: that asynchronous work is simply conversation with longer pauses. You say something, the reply comes later, and otherwise it is the same exchange.

It is not the same exchange. When you talk to someone live, you get feedback all the time. You see confusion before it turns into disagreement. You notice when a point lands badly and soften it. You can tell whether someone is on board or only being polite. A message sent to a colleague who is asleep gets none of that. It arrives complete, with no chance to adjust, and it is read hours later by someone who cannot ask what you meant until you are asleep yourself.

That makes cross–time zone communication less a scheduling question than an etiquette question. And the etiquette is mostly unwritten. How much context should a message carry? When is it fine to decide something and when should you wait? How direct can you be in text before it reads as curt? Teams answer these questions implicitly, and different people answer them differently, which is where the friction starts.

The sharpest version of it shows up in the mornings. People in a later time zone wake up to find that decisions have already been made. Nothing sinister happened; the people who were awake simply moved things forward. But over time a pattern sets in. One region decides, and the other reviews. One side shapes the work and the other reacts to it.

People in the later zones respond in a predictable way. They start writing defensively. Messages become longer and more hedged, anticipating every objection, because there will be no chance to clarify before the next set of decisions is made. That costs time and energy, and it rarely feels good to write or to read.

Better scheduling will not fix this on its own. What helps is making the etiquette explicit, so both regions know how decisions get made and who gets to shape them.

## Draft C

Most of what is written about communication in distributed teams treats the difficulty as one of timing. Asynchronous work, on this view, is simply conversation that happens more slowly, and the remedy lies in better scheduling: finding the hours of overlap, protecting them, and arranging the important discussions so that everyone can attend. This advice is sensible as far as it goes, and I would not wish to dismiss it, but it seems to me that it misses what is most distinctive about writing to someone who is asleep.

When we speak to a colleague in real time, we are corrected constantly and almost without noticing, since a puzzled expression, a pause or a question asked halfway through a sentence tells us how we are being received, and we adjust before the misunderstanding has had time to settle. A message sent across several time zones has none of this. It must stand on its own until morning, and whatever tone it carries will be read without the small signals that would ordinarily soften or clarify it; the problem is therefore perhaps less a matter of logistics than of etiquette, and of an etiquette that very few teams have troubled to write down.

The consequences are most visible, I suspect, in the experience of waking to find that decisions have already been made, so that for those in the later time zone the working day begins with a set of conclusions reached while they slept and their role quietly shifts from deciding to reviewing. One region deliberates; the other approves or objects after the fact. Over time this might encourage a particular kind of writing among those who find themselves on the receiving end—careful, anticipatory, composed to forestall objections before they arise rather than to invite the discussion that the arrangement has already made difficult. It is an understandable response, and in the circumstances perhaps a rational one. It is also, I think, a sign that something in the arrangement deserves rather more attention than the question of which hours overlap.

## Draft D

Most of the advice about working across time zones treats asynchronous communication as a slower version of ordinary conversation. The recommendations follow naturally from that assumption: find the hours in which regions overlap, protect them carefully, schedule the important meetings there, and accept that everything else will simply take a little longer. There is nothing wrong with this advice as far as it goes. But it rests on a picture of the problem that seems to me incomplete, since it frames the difficulty almost entirely as one of timing.

Consider what actually happens when we write to a colleague who is asleep. In a live conversation we adjust constantly, often without noticing: a puzzled expression prompts us to rephrase, a hesitation tells us we have been too blunt, a question arrives before a misunderstanding has had time to settle. None of that feedback is available to a message sent into someone else's night. It will be read hours later, by a person who cannot ask what we meant, and who will fill the gaps with whatever interpretation their morning mood supplies. Seen this way, the challenge is less a matter of scheduling than of etiquette—and an etiquette, moreover, that most teams have never written down or even discussed.

The consequences are sharpest for those who wake to find that decisions have already been made. Over time a pattern can settle in almost unnoticed: one region deliberates and decides, while the other region's role shrinks to reviewing what has been decided in its absence. Is it any surprise that people in the later time zones begin to write differently in response? Their messages grow longer and more guarded, anticipating objections, hedging every proposal, trying to secure their position before the next handover leaves them once again with a settled outcome. This defensive writing is often read as a personality trait or a lack of confidence, when it may be a perfectly rational adaptation to the structure in which they work. If that is so, the remedy lies not in more overlap but in agreeing, explicitly, how decisions are to travel across the gap.
