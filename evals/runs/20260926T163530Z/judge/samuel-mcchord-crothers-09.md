# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-09

## The passage (by the author)

You see, there is a fact which we do not mention in our circulars. There is a great deal of land lying out of doors. Some land is in great demand, and the real trick is to find out what that land is. You can't go out on the plains of Wyoming and give an acre of land the same value which an acre has in the Wall Street district. I speak from experience, having tried to convince the public that if the acres are real, the values I suggested must be real also. People wouldn't believe me, and I lost money.

And the same thing is true about improvements. They must be related to the market value of the land on which they are placed. A forty-story building at Goshenville Corners would be a mistake. There is no call for it.

This is the mistake which I fear you have been making. Your novel is a carefully prepared structure, and must have cost a great deal, but it is built on ground which is not worth enough to justify the investment. It has not what we call "site value." You yourself declare that you have no particular interest in the characters you describe at such length. All that you have to say for them is that they are real. It is as if I were to put up an expensive apartment-house on a vacant lot I have at North Ovid. North Ovid is real, and so would be the apartment-house; but what of it?

There are ninety millions of people in this country, all with characters which might be carefully studied, if we had time. But we haven't the time. So we have to choose our intimates. We prefer to know those who seem to us most worth knowing. You should remember that the novelist has no monopoly on realism. The newspapers are full of all sorts of realities. The historian is a keen competitor.

## Draft A

I have had some dealings in real estate, and in the course of them I acquired certain principles which I should like to pass on to a young novelist of my acquaintance, since they cost me a considerable sum and I see no reason why he should not have them for nothing. The first is that land has no value in itself. It is worth precisely what people are willing to give for it, and they will give very little for a lot where nobody particularly wishes to live. The second is that a building must suit its site. I once put up [example needed: the building that did not suit its site], and I paid handsomely for my education.

Now my friend the novelist is also a builder. He has chosen his ground and is raising upon it an expensive structure, which has already cost him [number needed: how long] of conscientious labor. I do not question his workmanship. But I fear the ground is poor. His characters are true to life; that is perhaps the most that can be said for them, though he says it himself with evident pride. They are exactly like the people who live on our street, and nobody would cross the town to call on them.

It is not that I object to reality. I am in favor of it, in its proper place. But the reader is a busy man with only so many evenings in the year, and he must choose his acquaintances with some discrimination, naturally preferring those who are worth knowing to those who merely happen to exist. If he wants the plain facts about his neighbors, the morning paper will furnish them, and the historian will do as much for his grandfathers. What he asks of the novelist is something that neither of them can supply. A man may build honestly on a bad lot, but he need not be surprised when nobody moves in.

## Draft B

You will forgive a man who deals in land for offering advice about books, but I have read yours, and it seems to me that your trouble is one I know well from my own trade.

When I first began to build, I believed that a good building was valuable in itself. I put up solid walls, sound roofs and honest fittings, and I put them wherever I happened to own the ground. I lost money doing it, and it took me longer than I like to admit to see why. Land is worth what people want it to be worth, and nothing more. A lot has value only because there is demand for it, and a building has value only when it suits the site it stands on. The finest house in the world, set down where nobody wishes to live, is a loss the day the last brick is laid.

Your novel strikes me as that sort of house. You have spent a great deal of labour on it, and the labour shows. The workmanship is careful. But you have built on poor ground. Your people are true to life, and that is all they are. They talk as such people talk and do what such people do, and when I had finished with them I could not think why I had been asked to spend the evening in their company.

Consider your reader as I consider a buyer. He has only so many hours, and he means to spend them well. He does not choose his acquaintances in a book at random any more than he chooses his neighbours at random; he looks for people worth knowing. If he wants the plain facts of ordinary life, the newspaper brings them to his door every morning, and the historians have set down the rest at greater length than he will ever read. You cannot compete with them on their own ground, and you need not try.

So before you raise another story, look hard at the site. Ask whether anyone wants to live there. [example needed] Then build something that suits it.

## Draft C

You will forgive a plain man who deals in land for offering counsel to a novelist. I have read your book, and I read it, I must confess, with the eye of my trade. What I saw was a great deal of careful construction on a lot that nobody wants.

In my business we learn early, or we learn expensively, that land has no value in itself. A lot is worth precisely what people will pay to occupy it, and they will pay only where they already desire to be, so that the most beautiful acre in the county may be worth less than a narrow frontage beside the railway station. The building, too, must suit its site. A handsome warehouse in a quiet residential street is not an investment; it is a monument to the owner's want of judgment. I know this because I have erected such monuments myself, and the education cost me more than I care to reckon. [example needed: a real venture that failed]

Your novel appears to me to be costly work on poor ground. You have spent labor enough on it to raise a city block. The walls are straight and the joints are true. But what have you built upon? Your characters are true to life, and that, so far as I can discover after a diligent inspection of the premises, is the whole of what can be said in their favor, since they are the sort of people one encounters every day and does not ask to encounter again.

It is here that you forget the reader, who is your only purchaser. His time is short, and he knows it. Out of all the people who might claim his evenings, he selects those who are worth knowing. Besides, reality is not scarce. The newspapers deliver it to his door each morning, and the historians have accumulated more of it than any reasonable man could consume in a lifetime of industrious reading. Why should he come to you for what is already on his doorstep?

My advice, then, is what I should give any builder. Before you lay another foundation, go out and look at the ground. Ask whether anybody wants to live there.

## Draft D

My dear sir, I have read your novel with the attention which a man in my line of business gives to any new enterprise, and I should like to offer a word of counsel from one who has paid rather dearly for his education. I deal in land. When I began, I supposed that land had a value of its own, as a man has a character. I soon learned otherwise. A lot is worth precisely what somebody wants it for, and not a penny more. Demand gives it value, and demand is a fickle tenant. I learned, too, that a building must suit its site. I once put up a handsome structure on ground where nobody cared to go, and I still have the privilege of looking at it. It was well built; the plumbing was excellent; the only thing wrong with it was that it stood where it stood. That lesson cost me more than I care to tell.

Now it seems to me that you have made the same mistake on a larger scale. You have put a great deal of costly work upon poor ground. Your characters are true to life; I do not dispute it. I have met every one of them, and I have taken some pains to avoid meeting them again. That they are true to life is no recommendation. So is the weather.

Consider the situation of your reader. He is a busy man with a limited amount of time, and he must choose his acquaintances with some care. When he opens a book he is not looking for more of the people he already knows; he has enough of them at home and at the office. He wants to meet somebody worth the trouble. If it is mere reality he craves, the newspapers deliver it every morning at a moderate price, and the historians will furnish it in bulk for those who like it seasoned with dates. You cannot hope to compete with them on their own ground.

My advice, therefore, is simply the advice of my own trade. Before you build again, look to your site, and ask whether anyone will want to come there.
