# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-01

## The passage (by the author)

If you get into a bathtub which is not quite the last word in bathtubs, that is a bathtub which has legs and spigots to turn on the water, you know where you are at all the while. You turn on the hot water in the amount desired. It comes out of the hot water spout. As desired you turn on the cold water. Out of the cold water spout comes it.

But, as you know, the last word in bathtubs is not simple and democratic like that. It is built onto the floor and has a clock-like dial on the wall. Dial marked at different points: "Cold," "Medium," "Hot," "Off." Turn little handle to regulate temperature and flow of water. All out of same pipe. Yes—but—dial untruthful—very. "Off" scalds you; "Medium" freezes you. Bad time trying to take last word in baths.

"Tub or shower?" Maybe you say "shower." And draw one of those police-court cells. Except the door, no opening in the little, square, completely cement room but the small hole in the center of the floor through which the water runs away. But that's not the way to look at it. These little catacomb-like chambers are æsthetic in their ascetic character. You may entertain yourself by fancying that you are St. Jerome, or somebody like that. In here nothing that it will hurt can get wet, and you can have a fine time making the whole room a merry-go-round of splashes. One disturbing thought may occur to you. If the door should stick you might not be found until the hotel got worried about your bill, when perhaps it would be too late.

Still, I think the chummiest bath-rooms are those with a bay-window; very reprehensible those which have no hooks on which to hang your pajamas and razor strop.

## Draft A

There is a great deal to be said for the old-fashioned bathtub, the plain white tub with a hot tap at one end and a cold tap beside it. You know where you are with it. You turn on the one, you turn on the other, you consult your hand, and you adjust matters as any reasonable person would. The modern hotel has abolished this arrangement. It has provided instead a tub built into the wall, an imposing affair, with a single glittering dial in the middle of it. The dial, I understand, is intended as an improvement. You turn it a little way and you are scalded. You turn it back, very cautiously, a hair's breadth, and you are frozen. Somewhere between these extremities there is supposed to exist a temperature suitable for a human being; I have never discovered it. I have come in the end to regard the dial, after a number of disagreeable evenings, as a riddle set for the guest by the management, like the crossword in the evening paper, only less rewarding.

The shower stall of bare concrete I confess I rather enjoy. There is something robust about it, something of the gymnasium, so to say, and one splashes about in it with considerable spirit. Still, I cannot entirely dismiss from my mind the question of the door. It is a heavy door. It closes with a very final sort of sound. Suppose—it is not impossible—that it should decline to open again? One pictures oneself, dripping and indignant, calling out hour after hour to an empty room, and the chambermaid (who has the afternoon off) not coming, and nobody in the hotel any the wiser until the morning.

The real grievance, however, is the hooks. There are none. A traveller arrives from the street with his overcoat and his hat and his damp towel, and there is nowhere whatever to hang anything. He puts it on the chair. He puts it on the floor. It is, I suppose, civilisation's way of suggesting that he go home.

## Draft B

Every hotel bathroom is a small examination, and I seldom pass it on the first try.

The old kind never gave me trouble. A plain tub stood on the floor, and above it were two taps, one marked hot and one marked cold. You turned one, then the other, dipped a hand in, and adjusted. It was a conversation between you and the water, conducted in terms both parties understood.

The modern bathroom has done away with conversation. The tub is built into the wall as if it had grown there, and in place of the two honest taps there is a single dial. It is a handsome dial, polished and silent, and it lies. Turn it a little and the water comes out cold. Turn it a little more and it is still cold. Turn it one hair further and you are scalded. Somewhere on that dial, I am told, there is a setting that produces a bath a human being could sit in, but I have never found it, and I have come to suspect the dial was designed by someone who takes his baths elsewhere.

I have more affection for the bare concrete shower stall, the kind that is really just a small grey room with a drain in the middle. There is something cheerful about it. You can splash as much as you like; nothing in there can be ruined. My one reservation is the door. It closes firmly, and while I stand under the water I find myself wondering how firmly, and whether anyone would think to look for me if it declined to open again. [example needed]

But the true failing of the modern hotel bathroom is smaller than any of these. It is the absence of a hook. There is nowhere to hang a shirt, a robe or a towel, so everything ends up on the floor, which is wet, naturally, because of the dial.

## Draft C

I have come to regard the hotel bathroom as a kind of examination which the management sets the traveller, and one which comparatively few of us pass. The old-fashioned tub presented no difficulty. It stood upon its four feet in the middle of the floor, and it had two taps, one marked "Hot" and one marked "Cold." You turned the one, and then you turned the other, and presently you had a bath. Nothing could be simpler. But the modern tub is built into the wall, as though ashamed of itself, and it is governed by a single dial. This dial has a disposition entirely its own. Turn it slightly to the right and you are frozen; turn it a hair's breadth further and you are boiled. I have stood before that contrivance, a grown man, with one hand in the stream, moving it by degrees as a burglar in the moving pictures manipulates the combination of a safe, and I have been defeated every time.

The shower stall is another matter. I confess I enjoy the bare concrete variety, which has the atmosphere of a small, damp cavern. There is something distinctly adventurous about it. Still, one cannot help considering, as the door clicks behind one, precisely how it is to be opened again. Suppose the latch should stick? A man could remain there until the chambermaid arrived in the morning—and she might not arrive in the morning.

But what I chiefly hold against the modern hotel room is its absence of hooks. There is a chair, to be sure, and there is the bed, and there is always the floor. Frequently there is a closet, furnished with several wire hangers which jingle together and positively decline to come loose from the rod. There is nowhere, however, to hang a coat in a hurry. I do not ask for luxury. I ask for a hook.

## Draft D

Speaking of travel reminds me of the hotel bathroom. I have in my time come into contact with a good many hotel bathrooms, and I have observed certain things about them which I do not recall having seen set down before. The old-fashioned tub is, of course, no puzzle at all. It stands upon its feet in the middle of the floor like an honest animal. It has two taps, one marked "hot" and one marked "cold," and a plug upon a chain. You turn the one a little and the other a little, and there you are. A child could do it.

But the modern tub is built into the wall, as if it had something to hide. It has no taps. It has a single dial, handsomely nickelled, bearing no word of any kind. You turn it gently to the left, and you are scalded. You turn it gently to the right, and you are in the North Sea. Somewhere between, I am told, there is a point of perfect comfort. I have not found it. I have turned that dial, early and late, with the patience of a safe-cracker, and the water has never once been what I had in mind.

Then there is the shower stall of bare concrete, a sort of small grey cell with a drain in it. I will own that I rather enjoy it. There is something bracing, something of the gymnasium and the barracks, in a bath of that kind. Only, the door is heavy, and it shuts with a very final sound; and I have stood there dripping and wondered who, in the event, would think to look for me.

And there is the room without a hook. A room without a clothes hook is—well, it is a room without a clothes hook. Your coat goes on the chair, your trousers upon the coat, your towel upon the floor. Just so. Nothing more need be said of such a room, and I shall say nothing more.
