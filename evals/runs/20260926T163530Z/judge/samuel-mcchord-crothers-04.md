# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-04

## The passage (by the author)

In the days of small things the settlers in the wilderness had large thoughts. They felt themselves to be historical characters, as indeed they were. They were impressed by the magnitude of the country and by the importance of their relation to it. Their language took on a cosmic breadth.

Ethan Allen could not have assumed a more masterful tone if he had had an Empire at his back instead of undisciplined bands of Green Mountain Boys. Writing to the Continental Congress, he declares that unless the demands of Vermont are complied with "we will retire into the fastnesses of our Green Mountains and will wage eternal warfare against Hell, the Devil, and Human Nature in general." And Ethan Allen meant it.

The love of the superlative is deeply seated in the American mind. It is based on no very careful survey of the existing world. It is a conclusion to which it is easy to jump. I remember one week, traveling through the Mississippi Valley, stopping every night in some town that had something which was advertised as the biggest in the world. On Friday I reached a sleepy little village which seemed the picture of contented mediocrity. Here, thought I, I shall find no bigness to molest me or make me afraid. But when I sat down to write a letter on the hotel stationery I was confronted with the statement, "This is the biggest little hotel in the State."

When one starts a tune it is safer to start it rather low, so as not to come to grief on the upper notes. In discussing the American temperament it is better to start modestly. Instead of asking what excellent qualities we find in ourselves, we should ask what do other nations most dislike in us. We can then have room to rise to better things. There is a family resemblance between the worst and the best of any national group. Kipling, in his lines "To an American," may set the tune for us. It is not too high. His American is boastful, careless, and irrationally optimistic.

## Draft A

It has often been remarked that Americans are fond of exaggeration, and I do not see much use in denying it. We like large statements. We like to be told that a thing is the biggest of its kind, and if it is not the biggest we are inclined to think there has been some mistake in the measuring.

The habit is an old one. The settlers on the frontier did not regard themselves as obscure persons engaged in clearing land. They thought of themselves as historic figures, and they talked accordingly. The same spirit survives in more peaceful forms. I once travelled through a number of Midwestern towns and found that each of them was able to boast of possessing the largest something in the world. The object of pride varied from town to town; the superlative did not. In one modest village the claim was made on behalf of the hotel, which did not appear to me to be very large at all. But the proprietor believed in it, and so, I think, did the villagers, and it would have been a churlish thing to dispute the matter.

When we come to estimate the national character, however, it is perhaps wiser not to begin with our own claims. We are too much interested in them. A better starting point is to be found in the complaints of foreigners, who have no reason to flatter us. ## Draft C

It has often been observed that Americans are fond of exaggeration. The charge is true, and I see no reason to deny it. But it is worth asking where the habit came from, and whether it is quite so bad as our critics suppose.

The frontier settler did not think of himself as a private person. He thought of himself as a historic figure, and he was not altogether wrong. When a man is laying the foundations of a commonwealth in the wilderness, with nothing between him and the future but his own axe and his own opinion of himself, it is natural that he should speak as if posterity were listening. Ethan Allen, when he had a grievance against Congress, did not write a modest letter of complaint. He threatened that body in sweeping terms, as one power addressing another. [example needed: Allen's actual words, if quoted]. It did not occur to him that he was being extravagant.

The habit has survived the frontier. I once made a trip through a number of Midwestern towns, and I found that each of them was prepared to show me the largest something in the world. The objects varied a good deal; the spirit did not. In one modest village the claim was made on behalf of the hotel, which to my untutored eye seemed a very ordinary hotel indeed, but I was assured that in some particular it surpassed every other on the globe, and I had no evidence to the contrary. It is a pleasant thing to live in a place that leads the world, even if the lead is a narrow one.

How, then, are we to judge the national character? I would not begin with what we say of ourselves, for that is apt to be generous. I would rather begin with the complaints of foreigners. They are not always fair, but they are seldom without foundation, and a wise man will take his enemies' view of him as the starting point for self-knowledge. Kipling wrote a poem on the subject [example needed: the lines or gist of the Kipling poem]. It is not flattering. Perhaps that is why it is useful.

## Draft D

Americans have always liked a large claim. It is one of the first things a visitor notices and one of the last things a native thinks to question. We do not merely have a town; we have the finest town. We do not simply build; we build the biggest. The habit is old, and it seems to have grown up with the country.

It goes back at least to the frontier. The men and women who settled the edges of the continent did not think of themselves as ordinary people doing ordinary things. They saw themselves as historic figures, actors in a drama that later generations would study. When Ethan Allen fell out with Congress, he did not lodge a modest complaint; he threatened it in sweeping terms, as though he were one sovereign power addressing another. [quotation needed.] There was something grand and something comic in it, and both have lasted.

I was reminded of this on a trip through a string of Midwestern towns. Each had its boast, and each boast was the same shape: the world's largest something. [examples needed.] The claims grew more ambitious as the towns grew smaller, until I reached a modest village whose hotel, a plain enough building, announced itself as the world's largest of its kind. Nobody seemed embarrassed. The claim was not really meant to be checked; it was a way of saying that this place, too, mattered, and that it would not be overlooked.
