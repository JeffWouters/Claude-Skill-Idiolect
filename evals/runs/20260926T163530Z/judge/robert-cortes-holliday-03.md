# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-03

## The passage (by the author)

But here's the riddle:

Strolling about New York, from river to river, uptown and down, one might readily fancy that here only the poor pass out of the world. Or that if the rich and fashionable ever die their bodies are mysteriously spirited away to destinations unknown; or are secretly preserved (presumably by some taxidermal process) in their homes.

Why? Well, where on Fifth Avenue is an undertaker's? True, a man I know declares there is a single one there. I am unable to find it. Where on any fine street of the metropolis? Why, yes; as a rare phenomenon. You do know, of course, that enormous place on upper Broadway. Sign says branches in Paris, London, Berlin, Petrograd.

Viewed through the great windows interior presents somewhat the effect of the Metropolitan Museum of Art. In foreground large harp, equally huge Chinese vase—probably of the Tang Wang period, on great marble pedestal enormous bronze of a mounted Diana repelling with spear attack of ferocious animal resembling tiger. Appropriateness of this sculpture somewhat puzzling. On wall, somewhat further within, immense tapestry. One door labelled "Delivery Entrance." All of this, of course, is magnificence as much as even the most covetous would crave.

But in New York this august undertaking hall is an anachronism. Here, for some reason mysterious, it is in shabby neighborhoods that the "parlors" of undertakers abound. You may find them sprinkled all about the lower East Side. Frequent on Hudson Street, and, say, on Varick. Quaint and curious places, these. Very human in their appeal. Tiny places, most of them.

One such cozy crib I know on Greenwich Avenue. Has a stained glass screen in the window, suggesting a good deal the style of window ornamentation popular with that American institution lately deceased—the saloon. The social spirit rife in small undertaking shops, at least in some of them, is pleasant to observe. Business there not being pressing, and life moving in these inns of death in a leisurely and quiet current, neighborly amenities appear to be much cultivated.

## Draft A

It is a curious thing, and one which I do not remember to have seen remarked upon, that in the wealthier quarters of the city there are practically no undertakers. You may walk the length of the fashionable avenues, past the great houses and the costly shops, and you will look in vain for the modest sign of the funeral director. One is almost driven to conclude that in these neighbourhoods nobody ever dies. Perhaps the rich have arranged that too. Or perhaps, when the inevitable does occur, the matter is attended to somewhere else, discreetly, as one sends out the laundry.

There is, to be sure, one conspicuous exception. Uptown there stands a funeral establishment of the most lavish description, and I confess that I have never passed it without a certain astonishment. It is filled with art objects, [example needed: the kinds of objects], collected, one would say, with more zeal than discrimination. It has less the air of a place of mourning than of an antique dealer's, or of a small museum which has somehow lost its way. It seems, in short, entirely out of place; and I have wondered whether the bereaved are expected to grieve or to bid.

The undertaker, for the most part, is a humbler person, and he lives among humbler people. His shop is small, and it is found in the poorer districts, among the other small shops of the street. Here he is very much a part of the neighbourhood. The neighbours gather before his door, and they talk, and they go on talking, about whatever neighbours talk about. Nobody is in any hurry. There is none of the sombre hush which the word undertaker calls to mind; there is only the ordinary, comfortable conversation of people who know one another well. Death, in these streets, is not a stranger to be kept out of sight; it is a neighbour, and it is taken, like everything else, at an easy pace.

## Draft B

Speaking of death reminds me of the undertaking business. I have recently been struck by a curious fact concerning it which I have not seen commented upon before, namely, where in the great city it is to be found. You may walk up the most fashionable of our avenues from one end to the other, past the great houses and the fine shops and the clubs, and you will not, so far as I have been able to observe, see an undertaker. Or, if there is one, he is so discreet about it that he might as well not be there. One is driven to the conclusion that in these parts people do not die. They are too well off. It would not, I suppose, be quite the thing.

I will make one exception. There is uptown a funeral establishment of the most lavish kind. It is filled with objects of art—vases, tapestries, bronzes, great carved chairs, pictures in heavy frames. Everything is so dressed up that you wouldn't think anybody had died at all. It looks like a museum, or a dealer's gallery, or the house of a rich man who collects. And it seems, somehow, in the wrong place, like a gentleman in evening clothes who has got off at the wrong station.

For the undertaker, as a rule, lives among the poor. He keeps a little shop, one room wide, in some street of tenements, between the grocer and the tailor. There is a palm in the window, perhaps, and a card with his name in black letters, and inside a desk and a couple of chairs. And about his door, when there is occasion, the neighbours gather. They stand on the sidewalk in their shawls and aprons and shirt-sleeves, and talk. They are in no hurry. They talk of the departed, and of the weather, and of the price of things, and of the last funeral, and of the one before that. There is no sense of the wrong place here. Death, in these streets, is a neighbour too, and is received accordingly, at an easy pace.

## Draft C

It is a curious circumstance, and I do not know that anybody has remarked upon it, that you may walk the length of the handsomest avenues in the city and scarcely pass an undertaker. There are jewellers there, and florists, and dealers in expensive carpets. There are establishments which sell nothing whatever, so far as one can determine, except an atmosphere. Of undertakers, however, there are practically none. One is driven to conclude that the wealthy, in this city, do not die. Or, if they do, they arrange it somewhere else, discreetly, as they arrange most things. It would be entirely consistent with their habits.

There is, to be sure, one exception, and a very magnificent one. Uptown there stands a funeral establishment of the most lavish description, crowded with objects of art. It contains bronzes, tapestries, carved chests and enormous vases on pedestals. These are the furnishings you would expect of a museum, or of the drawing-room of a collector. The interior is hushed and opulent; and yet there is something about the place that does not quite belong. It is as if a palace had been deposited by mistake to perform the business of a shop. One feels that a person would need to be exceedingly well connected to be buried from it.

The undertaker proper, the neighbourhood undertaker, is quite another affair. You discover him in the poorer districts, in a small shop among other small shops, with a modest sign over the door. His premises are generally open. People drop in; they do not appear to regard it as a melancholy place at all. The neighbours gather on the pavement in front of it and converse, of the weather and of prices and of who is ailing and who is recovering, at the unhurried pace of people who have lived a long time on the same street. Death, here, is a neighbour too. It is not concealed. It keeps a shop on the corner, and passes the time of day.

## Draft D

If you were a stranger to New York and judged the city only by where its undertakers keep shop, you would come to a strange conclusion about who dies here.

Walk the wealthy avenues and you will look a long while for a funeral business. There are jewelers, there are florists, there are shops that sell one kind of glove and nothing else, but of undertakers there are almost none. The impression it leaves is that rich people simply do not die, or at least not in the neighborhood. Perhaps they are too busy. Perhaps it is considered a lapse of taste, the sort of thing one's family would rather handle discreetly, somewhere else, the way one handles a divorce.

There is an exception uptown, a funeral hall so lavish it seems to have wandered in from some other trade. It is filled with art objects, pieces set out carefully in the rooms, and the whole place has the hushed, expensive air of a gallery where nothing has a price tag because anyone who needs to ask should not be there. [example needed] It is handsome, in its way, but it feels out of place, as if the building had been meant for something else and had only lately and reluctantly taken up the dead.

Most of the city's funeral businesses are nothing like that. They are small shops in the poorer districts, set in among the grocers and the shoe repairers and the laundries, with a modest window and a plain name over the door. They are not hidden and they are not grand. They are simply part of the street.

And what struck me most about them is the company they keep. Neighbors gather outside, or just inside the door, and talk. Nobody hurries. They lean against the wall and trade news and ask after each other's families, and the conversation moves at an easy pace, the way conversations do in a place where people expect to see one another again tomorrow. Death, in these districts, has a storefront and regular hours, and nobody seems to find that especially remarkable.

It may be the most sensible arrangement in the city.
