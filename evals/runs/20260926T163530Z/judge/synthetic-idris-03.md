## The passage (by the author)

What might a more honest version of the interview look like? It seems to me that it would begin by admitting, to ourselves and to the candidate, that an hour is too short to know anyone, and that what we are really doing is trying to form a reasonable guess about what it would be like to work together. We might, then, choose problems that resemble the work as it is lived—reading someone else's code, asking questions about an unclear requirement, explaining a decision to a sceptical colleague—rather than puzzles chosen for their difficulty. We could invite candidates to ask us the hard questions as well, and answer them without the polish we reserve for recruitment pages, since they are deciding about us as surely as we are deciding about them. None of this needs an elaborate process or new tools; it needs only a willingness to treat the interview as a conversation between equals, which is slower, less tidy and considerably harder to score.

The interview, like so many rituals of working life, persists because everyone involved understands the pretence and nobody quite wishes to be the first to stop observing it. Arguably, it is one of the few occasions on which an organisation meets a stranger with its guard up and its best face on; the stranger, of course, does the same, so that each side ends up judging a performance rather than a person. There is something quietly forgivable in that, since both parties are acting out of the same ordinary anxiety about being found wanting. Perhaps the kindest interview is not the one that reveals the most about a candidate, but the one that leaves them, whatever the outcome, with the sense of having been met with generosity by people who were just as nervous as they were.

## Draft A

It seems to me that a more candid approach to interviewing would begin with an admission that most interviewers are reluctant to make: an hour, or even several, reveals remarkably little about how someone will actually work. We compress a relationship that may last years into a single afternoon and then speak of our conclusions with a confidence the evidence hardly supports. Acknowledging this does not make the interview useless, but it does suggest a different way of conducting it. Rather than puzzles and brain-teasers, which test mainly the ability to solve puzzles and brain-teasers, we might offer tasks that resemble the work itself; rather than treating the candidate's questions as a formality at the end, we might welcome the difficult ones, since they tell us something about what the person values and are, in any case, entirely fair.

Part of what makes interviews so unreliable is that both sides are performing. The candidate presents a version of themselves suited to the occasion, and the interviewer presents a version of the team and the organisation that is similarly polished, so that each ends up judging an act. This is understandable—both parties are nervous, and nervousness encourages performance—but it is worth remembering, if only so that we hold our judgements a little more loosely.

Perhaps the best measure of an interview, then, is not whether it produced the right decision, which we can rarely know, but how the candidate felt on leaving it. Did they feel they had been treated generously, given a fair chance to show what they could do and an honest picture of what they would be joining? If so, the interview has done something worthwhile whatever the outcome.

## Draft B

What can an interviewer honestly expect to learn about a person in an hour? Very little, I suspect, and it may be that the most useful thing an interview process can do is admit as much. We tend to treat the conversation as a reliable instrument, capable of revealing how someone will think and behave over years of working together, when it is at best a brief and heavily staged glimpse. A more candid approach would begin from that admission and design around it: fewer brain-teasers, which measure mainly a talent for brain-teasers, and more realistic tasks that resemble the work the person would actually be asked to do.

There is also the matter of performance, which runs in both directions. The candidate presents a confident, well-rehearsed version of themselves; the interviewer presents a team that is calm, organised and pleasant to work in. Each side is therefore judging an act, and each knows, at some level, that the other is doing the same. This is understandable rather than cynical, since both parties are nervous and both want the meeting to go well. It seems to me that the sensible response is to invite the candidate to ask difficult questions and to welcome them when they do, rather than treating a sharp question as a sign of awkwardness. You might learn more from how someone probes your weaknesses than from how they describe their own.

Perhaps the fairest test of an interview is how the candidate feels afterwards, whatever the result. The best ones leave people with the sense that they were treated generously, that their time was respected and their answers taken seriously, even when the answer they receive at the end is no.

## Draft C

Most job interviews rest on a polite fiction: that an hour of conversation tells you whether someone will do the job well. It does not. An hour reveals very little, and a more candid approach would start by admitting that out loud.

Once you accept the limit, the format changes. Instead of brain-teasers and trick questions, give candidates realistic tasks that look like the work they would actually do. A puzzle about ping-pong balls tests how someone handles puzzles about ping-pong balls. A small, real problem from the team's own week tells you far more, and it tells the candidate something useful about the job too.

It also helps to be honest about what the interview really is. Both sides are performing. The candidate presents the most capable, confident version of themselves. The interviewer presents the company as organised, friendly and exciting. Each is judging an act put on by the other. That is not a scandal. Shared nerves make it understandable: the candidate wants the job, and the interviewer wants to make the right call and not look foolish doing it. Everyone in the room is a little anxious, and anxious people perform.

A candid interview makes room for that by inviting the other side's hard questions. Welcome tough questions from candidates rather than treating them as a sign of attitude. Someone who asks why the last person left, or what goes wrong on the team, is doing their own due diligence, and they are giving you a better conversation than a rehearsed pitch ever could. ## Draft D

It seems to me that a more candid approach to job interviews would begin with an admission that most organisations are reluctant to make: an hour spent in conversation reveals rather little about how someone will actually work. We tend to treat the interview as a reliable instrument, and we build elaborate processes around it, yet anyone who has hired for long enough will recognise how often the confident judgement of the interview room fails to survive the first months of real collaboration. None of this means the interview is worthless. It means only that we might hold its conclusions more lightly, and design it with its limits in mind.

The first consequence is a preference for realistic tasks over brain-teasers, since a problem that resembles the work is at least measuring something close to the work, whereas a puzzle chiefly measures familiarity with puzzles. The second is a genuine welcome for difficult questions from the candidate, who is, after all, making a decision of their own and deserves the information to make it well. A candidate who asks something awkward about the team or its practices is perhaps showing exactly the kind of judgement we claim to be looking for.

There is also the uncomfortable fact that both sides are performing. The candidate presents a composed and capable version of themselves; the interviewer presents an organisation that is more coherent and more welcoming than it may be on an ordinary day. Each, in other words, is judging an act. This is understandable, given that both parties are nervous, and the nerves are shared even when they are not admitted. Would the conversation improve if we acknowledged this openly? I suspect it would, at least a little. Perhaps the best measure of an interview is not whether it selected the right person but whether every candidate, whatever the result, leaves feeling that they were treated generously.
