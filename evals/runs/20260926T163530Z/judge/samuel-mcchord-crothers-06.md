# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-06

## The passage (by the author)

It was this pleasure which I enjoyed in a ramble with a friend through the New Forest. The day was fine, and it would have been a joy to be under the greenwood trees if no one had been before us. But the New Forest had a human interest; for on such a day as this, William Rufus rode into it to hunt the red deer, and was found with an arrow through his body. And to this day no man knows who killed William Rufus, or why. Though, of course, some people have their suspicions.

Many other things may have happened in the New Forest in the centuries that have passed, but they have never been brought vividly to my attention. So far as I was concerned there were no confusing incidents. The Muse of History told one tragic tale and then was silent.

On the other side of the Forest was the Rufus stone marking the spot where the Red King's body was found. At Brockenhurst we inquired the way, which we carefully avoided. The road itself was an innovation, and was infested with motor-cars, machines unknown to the Normans. The Red King had plunged into the Forest and quickly lost himself; so would we. There were great oaks and wide-spreading beeches and green glades such as one finds only in England. It was pleasant to feel that it all belonged to the Crown. I could not imagine a county council allowing this great stretch of country to remain in its unspoiled beauty through these centuries.

We took our frugal lunch under a tree that had looked down on many generations. Then we wandered on through a green wilderness. We saw no one but some women gathering fagots. I was glad to see that they were exercising their ancestral rights in the royal domain. They looked contented, though I should have preferred to have their dress more antique.

## Draft A

It is a curious circumstance that a forest so extensive and so ancient should have only one story to tell, and that the story should be a killing which nobody has ever solved. Everything else that happened in the New Forest seems to have been forgotten. What is remembered is that King William Rufus went out hunting there and did not come back alive, and that no one has ever been able to say with certainty who was responsible. The woods have kept the secret. They have kept it so long that the secret has become, in a manner, the whole of their history.

We did not go looking for the spot. There was a road that led in that direction, but it was crowded with automobiles, each carrying its load of travellers toward the place where history had happened, and we had no particular desire to join the procession. So we turned aside and wandered among the old trees. They were venerable and unconcerned, and they did not seem to care in the least who had been killed beneath them. I was grateful that the Crown still owned them. There is something to be said for a landlord who is too dignified to improve his property; the forest has remained wild because nobody with authority has ever considered it worth while to make it otherwise.

We had our picnic in the shade. [example needed: a detail of the picnic] While we sat there we noticed some women gathering firewood, as they were entitled to do by old right. They did not look like people who were taking anything. They looked like people who were collecting what was theirs.

It occurred to me that this was a better kind of history than the other. The king was killed once, and the question has been debated ever since. But the women had been exercising their privilege quietly all along, and nobody debated that at all.

## Draft B

The New Forest has had a long life and only one adventure. Or rather, it has had only one that anybody remembers, which for a forest comes to the same thing. Centuries ago King William Rufus went hunting there and did not come home. Somebody killed him, and nobody has ever found out who. The trees have been living on the story ever since. It is not much to live on for so many generations, but they make it go a long way, as old families do with a single distinguished ancestor.

We had no intention of solving the mystery. It has been in abler hands than ours, and has been all the better for being left alone. A murder that is cleared up becomes a matter for the police; a murder that is never cleared up becomes a matter for the imagination, and is at the service of every passer-by.

There was a road through the forest, and it was full of motor cars. They were going somewhere in a great hurry, and I have no doubt they arrived. We let them go. It seemed to us that the best way to see a forest was to get lost in it, and we turned aside among the old trees, which had not been planted for anybody's convenience and did not propose to be hurried. We were grateful that the land belonged to the Crown. A private owner would long ago have improved it. The Crown, being above such ambitions, had been content to let it stay wild, and there are times when a little royal neglect is the best thing that can happen to a landscape.

We ate our luncheon under a great tree, and felt ourselves to be poachers of a very mild kind. Not far off some women were gathering firewood. They did it without apology, for it was their right, and had been their right for longer than anyone could tell. It was the only law in the forest that seemed older than the killing of the King.

## Draft C

Most woods have a great many stories and remember none of them. The New Forest remembers only one, and it is not a cheerful one. A king rode out to hunt there, and he did not ride back. Somebody killed William Rufus among the trees, and nobody has ever settled who it was or why. The forest has kept the secret admirably; it has had nothing else to keep. And so for all the centuries since it has been, in the popular mind, the place where a king was killed.

We did not go to look for the spot. There was a road that led toward it, and the road was full of automobiles, each carrying a family that had come to see where history had happened and was now waiting its turn to see it. We left them to their pilgrimage and turned off among the old trees, where there was no history to speak of and a good deal of shade. It was pleasant to wander without an object; perhaps that is the only proper way to see a forest. I found myself grateful to the Crown, a thing that does not often happen to me, for having owned the land so long and so jealously that nobody had been allowed to improve it. What the kings kept for their own sport they had, without intending it, kept wild for us.

We ate our lunch under the trees. While we sat there some women came by gathering firewood by an ancient customary right. They did not look at us, and they did not look like people who were thinking about William Rufus. The dead wood was theirs, and they were taking it home.

It occurred to me that they had the better part of the forest. We had come for its one story. They had come for its sticks.

## Draft D

The New Forest has, so far as the ordinary visitor remembers, exactly one story. Somewhere among these trees King William Rufus was killed while he was out hunting, and nobody has ever settled who did it or why. Everything else that has happened here, over all the centuries since, seems to have been absorbed into the woods without leaving a mark. It is an odd thing for a place to be known by: not a founding, not a battle, but a single arrow and an unanswered question.

We did not go looking for the spot. The main road through the forest was solid with cars, glinting in a long line, each one full of people presumably on their way to see the forest from the road. We left it as soon as we decently could and struck off among the trees, with no plan beyond walking until we felt like stopping.

It was the old trees that made the day. Some of them must have been standing for a very long time, broad and twisted and quite indifferent to us, and between them the ground ran on in a way that ground seldom does in the south of England now, unfenced and unimproved. I found myself grateful, not for the first time, that the forest belongs to the Crown. Whatever one thinks of the arrangement in general, it has kept this particular country wild when almost any other owner would long ago have tidied, divided and sold it.

We ate our picnic in a clearing, sitting on the roots, and for an hour nothing happened except the light moving about. Later we passed some women gathering firewood, loading up what the forest had dropped. They were doing it, we learned, by old right, a privilege that has come down with the land itself. It seemed a fitting sight. The killing of a king is the forest's one remembered story, but its real life has been quieter, and it is still going on.
