# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-07

## The passage (by the author)

Let us by a soft answer turn away the wrath of the critic. Doubtless we ought not to have done so. Our excuse is that, at the time, we could not help it. We may make the further plea, common to all soft-hearted sinners, that if we hadn't wept, other people would, so that no great harm was done, after all.

But letting bygones be bygones, and not seeking to justify the enthusiasms of the nineteenth century, one may return to Dickens as to the home of one's childhood. How do the old scenes affect us? Does the charm remain? When thus we return to Dickens, we are compelled to confess the justice of the latter-day criticism. In all his writings he deals with characters and situations which are wholly obvious; at least they are obvious after he deals with them. Not only is he without the art which conceals art, but, unlike some novelists of more recent fame, he is without the art that conceals the lack of art He produces an impression by the crude method of "rubbing it in." There are no subtleties to pique our curiosity, no problems left us for discussion, no room for difference of opinion. There is no more opportunity for speculation than in a one-price clothing store where every article is marked in plain figures. To have heartily disliked Mr. Pecksniff and to have loved the Cheeryble Brothers indicates no sagacity on our part. The author has distinctly and repeatedly told us that the one is an odious hypocrite and that the others are benevolent to an unusual degree. Our appreciation of Sam Weller does not prove that we have any sense of humor save that which is common to man. For Mr. Weller's humor is a blessing that is not in disguise. It is a pump which needs no priming. There is no denying that the humor, the pathos, and the sentiment of Dickens are obvious.

## Draft A

There was a time when people wept over Dickens without shame, and it is awkward to admit that we were once among them. We do not defend those tears now. We excuse them, as one excuses the enthusiasms of youth, with a slight cough and a change of subject. It was all a long while ago, we explain, and the times were sentimental, and everybody was doing it. [example needed: a scene that once moved readers]

The modern critic has made it clear that we ought to have known better. He points out, and I think rightly, that the characters in Dickens are wholly obvious, their virtues and their vices painted on the outside where no one can possibly miss them. The emotional effects are equally plain, for the author tells us when to laugh and when to cry and does not leave the matter to our discretion. There is nothing to interpret. A reader who comes to such a book expecting to exercise his subtlety will find that it has all been exercised for him beforehand, and that he has been given nothing to do but agree.

This is a serious charge. When I confessed that I liked Sam Weller, I had supposed that I was showing some discernment, and it now appears that I was showing none whatever. Anybody would like Sam Weller; the author had arranged it so. And when I detested Pecksniff, I was not detecting a hypocrite by my own penetration. I was merely reading the label which the author had pasted on him in large letters, so that even the most careless reader could not mistake the contents of the package or claim any credit for having noticed them.

It is humbling to learn that one's judgments were not one's own. There is some consolation in the thought that they were at least correct, and the critic who has seen through Dickens has not, so far as I can discover, arrived at any different opinion of Pecksniff.

## Draft B

There was a time when people wept over Dickens, and were not ashamed of it. They read his books aloud by the lamp, and when they came to the sad chapters the reader's voice broke and the listeners reached for their handkerchiefs. We do not defend those tears nowadays. We excuse them. We speak of them as we speak of the crinoline and the horsehair sofa, as things that belonged to a simpler age and are not to be too severely judged.

The modern critic has his explanation, and I am not disposed to quarrel with it. He tells us that Dickens is wholly obvious. His characters are all on the surface; his pathos is laid on with a trowel; his humor announces itself before it arrives. There is nothing left over for the reader to find out. He has only to open the book and receive what is handed to him.

It must be confessed that this is true, and that it takes a good deal of the credit out of our old enthusiasm. When I was fond of Sam Weller I thought it showed a certain breadth of sympathy in me. When I detested Pecksniff I took it as evidence of a fine moral discrimination. I now perceive that I was doing exactly what I was told. Dickens never trusted me to form an opinion of my own. He saw to it that I liked the people I was meant to like and hated the people I was meant to hate, and he did not leave the matter to chance. Every judgment was made for me before I came to it, and made so plainly that a child could not miss it.

There is a certain humiliation in discovering that one's virtues were supplied by the author. To love Sam Weller is no more a sign of insight than to feel warm in front of a fire. The fire does the work. And yet, now that the critics have taken away my merit, I find that I still sit by the fire.

## Draft C

There was a time when people wept over Dickens and were not ashamed of it. We do not defend them now. We excuse them. They lived, we say, in a simpler generation, when a sorrowful chapter was an event in the household and nobody had yet been instructed to distrust a lump in the throat, and we extend to their tears the same indulgence we extend to their furniture. We do not share the taste, but we are willing to believe it was sincere.

The modern critic has gone further, and he is right to. He observes that everything in Dickens lies on the surface. The good people are good and the wicked people are wicked, and the author takes particular care that we shall not mistake one for the other. The pathos is laid on where pathos is wanted and the laughter where laughter is wanted, and there is nothing left over for the reader to discover by himself. A character in Dickens has no secrets from us, for Dickens has no secrets from us. There is nothing to interpret.

This is a just criticism, and it takes away rather a good deal of the credit we used to give ourselves. It is no proof of insight to be fond of Sam Weller. Everybody is fond of Sam Weller; that is what he is for. Nor does it show any remarkable moral penetration to detest Pecksniff, since the author has done the detesting beforehand, thoroughly and with evident enjoyment, and merely invites us to join in. The judgment is handed to us ready made, like a parcel from the shop. All we have to do is carry it home.

But perhaps the pleasure need not be taken away along with the credit. We might still laugh at Sam Weller and still enjoy disliking Pecksniff, so long as we do not mistake the enjoyment for discernment. The reader of Dickens is a guest who has been conducted through every room of the house by an attentive host, and he should not afterwards boast of having explored it.

## Draft D

Anyone who comes back to Dickens now has to get past his earlier readers first. They wept over him, openly and in great numbers, and they said so. The modern reader is inclined to treat those tears with a kind of embarrassed tolerance. We do not defend them; we excuse them, as one excuses the enthusiasms of a relative who was young in a different decade. They did not know better, we suggest, and it did them no harm.

The critics of our own time have gone further, and on the main point I think they are right. Dickens's characters are wholly obvious. His emotional effects are wholly obvious too. When he wants us to grieve, the grief is prepared, announced and delivered, and there is no mistaking the moment it arrives. When he wants us to laugh, the joke is labelled before it is told. Nothing is left in shadow. There is, in the strict sense, nothing to interpret, because the author has already done all the interpreting and handed us the result.

This has a curious consequence for the reader who enjoys him. Suppose you are delighted by Sam Weller, as nearly everyone has been. Or suppose you detest Pecksniff, as you are plainly meant to. Neither response tells anyone anything about you. It shows no discernment, no special insight into character, because Dickens has spelled out every judgment in advance. You have not noticed Pecksniff's hypocrisy; you have been told about it, repeatedly and with gusto, and you have agreed. Liking Sam Weller is not a critical opinion. It is closer to accepting an invitation.

That is a real limitation, and it explains a good deal about why rereading him can feel strange. The pleasures are all still there, but they are pleasures of recognition rather than discovery. One goes back not to find something new but to be given the same thing again.

Whether that settles the question of his worth is another matter. It does settle the question of the tears. They were not a sign of shallow readers. They were the response the books were built to produce.
