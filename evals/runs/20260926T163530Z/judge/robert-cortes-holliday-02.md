# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-02

## The passage (by the author)

Has Mr. Cole, or anyone "near and dear" to him, recently met with any "accident" at the hands of robbers? No. He will not, then, have a revengeful feeling toward any person charged with crime? Not at all. Would he give the same weight to the "story" of a "self-confessed thief and murderer" that he would to the testimony of a "man of probity"? Probably not. Now, doubtless, Mr. Cole is a reader of newspapers. He has, of course, seen this "literature" (with a sneer), this "newspaper hysteria" about a "c-r-i-m-e wave" (tongue in cheek). Well, can Mr. Cole go into the jury box and look at this case detached from the "atmosphere" now "being created by the newspapers"? Finally, is Mr. Cole acquainted with anyone connected with the police department?

Mr. Cole, for some reason, strikes out.

Third man accepted. He comes around from behind it to enter the jury box. At the gateway, while defendant stands and faces him, some more rigmarole-mumble-jumble business.

Suddenly my friend is called. His business? asks district attorney. A writer, he replies. Defendant and his attorney exchange strange glances. Undoubtedly there is something low and suspicious about a fellow with such a business. Attorney for the defense comes forward hurriedly. Soon takes my friend in hand. He at once adopts the sarcastic. My friend's work must require unusual "observation." He must be "gifted" with "great powers of de-duct-shun" (said out of one corner of his mouth). Of course, he has too a "fine imagination." By the way, what is the nature of his writing? Has he written any novels?

No, my friend says, he is a humorous writer. "A what?" exclaims the lawyer, his mouth remaining open. Then, "Like Don Markee?" "Somewhat," says my friend. Lawyer visibly pales. Withdrawing toward counsel table, looks back at the accused, who vigorously shakes his head.

## Draft A

The other day I went with a friend of mine to the criminal courts, where he had been summoned to serve upon a jury. To be summoned is one thing; to serve is quite another, as we presently learned.

The first candidate called was a stout, pleasant man with a watch-chain, as respectable-looking a man as you could readily imagine. Had he, the district attorney inquired, any prejudice against the defendant? He had not. Had he read of the case in the newspapers? He had not. Did he read, as a rule, the accounts of crimes in the newspapers? Well, now and then. Now and then, it appeared, meant pretty regularly. Was he related to any member of the police? He had, he said, a brother-in-law on the force. How long had his brother-in-law been on the force? Some years. Were they on good terms? Very good terms. The stout man was thanked, and excused, and went out looking somewhat hurt, as if he had been told that his face did not suit.

Then my friend was called. The lawyer for the defence, a lean man with a remarkable voice, asked him his occupation. My friend said that he was a writer. "A writer!" exclaimed the lawyer, and turned half round to the room, as though he wished everybody to have the benefit of it. "A writer. And what, may I ask, do you write?" The inflection he gave to the word was not flattering. One gathered that writers, in his experience, were a class of person to be looked into.

My friend replied, very modestly, that he wrote comic pieces.

The effect was remarkable. The lawyer stopped where he stood. The defendant, who until then had sat looking at nothing in particular, looked up quickly at my friend, and then at his lawyer, and then at my friend again, with an expression of the most lively alarm. The two of them conferred in a whisper. My friend was excused. I do not know that I have ever seen a man so anxious not to be written about.

## Draft B

The business of choosing a jury for a criminal trial is, I discovered, a slow and curiously intimate proceeding. A candidate is called forward, and sits down, and is at once subjected to a catechism concerning the state of his soul. Had he any prejudice, one way or the other, in such a case as this? Did he read about crimes in the newspapers? Did he believe what he read? Had he a brother, or a cousin, or a friend of a cousin, in the police department? The man we watched answered all these questions very gravely, with an air of wishing to be helpful, and was then, for no reason that anybody announced, politely dismissed. He went out looking injured, as a person does who has passed an examination and been refused the diploma.

Then my friend was called. The lawyer for the defense, a gentleman of considerable eloquence, asked him his occupation. My friend replied that he was a writer. There was a pause. "A writer," said the lawyer, turning the word over as though it were an unfamiliar coin, and he looked round at the room with an expression which invited everybody to share his amusement. What, he wished to know, did a writer write? Were his writings of a nature to be read by anyone?

My friend explained, uncomfortably, that he wrote humorous pieces. It was the worst thing he could have said; the lawyer's face fell. The defendant, who until then had sat perfectly still and taken no apparent interest in the matter, looked up with unmistakable alarm, and the two of them exchanged a glance—it was a very eloquent glance—as if a man who made jokes for a living could not possibly be trusted with anybody's liberty. I have wondered since whether they were not, in their way, entirely right.

## Draft C

I went along one morning to keep a friend company while he waited to be picked, or not picked, for a jury in a criminal trial. We sat in a long room on hard benches and watched the process at work, which is slower and odder than I had imagined.

The candidate ahead of him was called up and questioned with great care. Did he have any bias that might keep him from judging fairly? Had he been following the news about crime, and had it shaped his opinions? Did he have any ties to the police, a relative on the force, a friend, anyone at all? He answered each question plainly and, as far as I could tell, well. Then he was thanked and dismissed, and nobody explained why. He walked out looking faintly insulted, as though he had passed every test and failed the course.

Then it was my friend's turn. He gave his name and, when asked his occupation, said he was a writer. The defense lawyer brightened at once. A writer, he said, and repeated the word to the room as if it were a small, amusing animal someone had brought into court. What sort of thing did a writer do all day? Did he find it steady work? The lawyer was clearly enjoying himself, and a few people on the benches laughed along with him.

My friend explained, patiently, that he wrote comic pieces.

The effect was remarkable. The lawyer stopped smiling. He turned and looked at the defendant, and the defendant looked back at him, and for a moment the two of them wore exactly the same expression, the expression of men who have just realized that someone in the room may be taking notes. I do not know what they imagined he would write, but they plainly did not want to be in it.

## Draft D

I sat in the courtroom one morning while a friend of mine was up to be examined for a jury, and I watched the selection of jurors for a criminal trial with considerable interest. It is a slower business than one would think. The first man called was a respectable-looking fellow who took every question with great care, as if it were a trap. Had he any prejudice against the defendant? He had not. Did he read about crimes in the newspapers? He did, now and then, as everybody does; he did not think it had done him any harm. Had he any relatives or friends on the police force? Here he paused, and then admitted that he had. Upon this, after some consultation in low voices at the table, he was thanked very kindly and dismissed, and he went out looking, I thought, rather relieved and a little hurt, as a man does who has been refused a thing he did not particularly want.

Then my friend took the chair. The lawyer for the defence, a brisk man, asked him his occupation, and my friend said that he was a writer. The lawyer repeated the word with elaborate surprise, turning half round toward the benches, as though he wished all of us to share the joke. A writer! Did the gentleman call that work? Did he find that it kept him busy? There was some laughter, which the lawyer received with evident satisfaction; my friend bore it very well.

The lawyer then wished to know what sort of thing he wrote. My friend said, simply enough, that he wrote humorous pieces for the magazines. The effect of this was remarkable. The lawyer stopped smiling at once. The defendant, who until then had seemed perfectly indifferent to the proceedings, sat up and stared at my friend in open alarm. It was clear that neither of them wished to have his fate settled by a humorist.
