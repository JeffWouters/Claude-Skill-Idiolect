# Judge packet synthetic-noor-04

## The passage (by the author)

Most logs I split in four. Some in six. Nothing wider than my hand. That is the rule: small pieces dry faster and burn cleaner.

Then the stack. It goes against the side wall of the house, under the eave, on two old pallets. The pallets keep it off the ground. Without them the bottom row turns soft and grey by February. I found that out the first year, when I stacked straight onto the paving.

Bark up on the top row. Bark down lower. At each end the pieces go crossways, one row on the next, like a tower. The ends hold the middle. The middle leans a little in toward the wall. Not much. Two fingers.

I leave a gap of about ten centimetres from the bricks. The air has to move behind it. The west wind does most of the drying. It comes down the side passage all winter.

Here is the count for this year. Two cubic metres. Nine hours of splitting over three weekends. One wheelbarrow, 61 loads. A stack 3.2 metres long and 1.4 high. One blister, left palm.

Next door, Maren does the same job with a hydraulic splitter she hires for a day. 45 euros. She is done by lunch. She waves over the fence while I am still on the first pallet.

And I have thought about it.

It would be quicker. It would be easier on the back. But the maul is quiet, and it was my grandfather's, and I like the weight of it coming down. I like to know each log once before I burn it. That is most of the reason. The rest is habit.

By the end of the third Sunday the drive is bare again. Just bark and chips and a few pale splinters in the gravel. I sweep them into a sack for kindling. Nothing wasted.
