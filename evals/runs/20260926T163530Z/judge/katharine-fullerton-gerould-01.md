# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-01

## The passage (by the author)

An isolated case is never worth much. But one has only to consider conditions at large to see that this has everything to make it typical. One has only to look at any official record of immigration, any chart of distribution of population by races, to see how the old American stock is being numerically submerged. If you do not wish to look at anything so dull as statistics, look at the comic papers. A fact does not become a stock joke until it is pretty well visible to the average man. Our forefathers cared immensely for education; they felt themselves humble before learning; and their schools followed, soon and sacredly, upon their churches. They stood in awe of the real thing; and they had no illusions as to the ease of the scholar's path. They legislated for their schools solemnly, and if not with complete wisdom, always at least with accurate ideals. Educational (like all other) legislation nowadays is largely in the hands of illiterate people, and the illiterate will take good care that their illiteracy is not made a reproach to them. If any one chooses to say that culture must always be in the hands of an oligarchy, and that the oligarchy has not been touched, I will only ask him to consider the pupils and the teaching in most private schools. In the end, prestige values are going to tell; and the vast bulk of our population will see to it that the prestige values are not absolutely unattainable to them. The great fortunes have made their way to the top—yes, really to the top. In many cases there has been time for a quick veneer of grammar to be laid over their original English. In many cases there has not; and no one cares. The custodians of culture cannot afford to care; for their custody must either be endowed or be forsaken.

## Draft A

I heard, not long ago, a small story which seemed to me to say more about American education than a shelf of official reports: [example needed: the anecdote about declining learning]. It is a trifle, I grant; but trifles are often the most honest witnesses we have. One need not be a statistician to see what lies behind it. The population figures tell us that the old settler families no longer make up the majority of the country [number needed: their present share], and the comic papers, which are perhaps rather wiser than the census, have been telling us the same thing for years [example needed: the popular joke]. The people who once shaped the nation's mind do not shape it any more. This is not a lament. It is simply a fact, and we had better look at it plainly.

Our grandfathers revered schooling—foolishly, now and then, I fancy, but always sincerely. They thought a learned man a person to be looked up to, and they did not much mind if he was poor. To-day the schools are governed by men who were poorly taught themselves, and who sit in legislatures deciding, with a confidence that might be touching if it were not so costly, what other people's children shall learn and how little of it they shall be expected to remember. It is not surprising that they resist standards. A standard is a measuring-rod, after all; and no one cares to be measured and found short, least of all in public. The newly rich, meanwhile, rise without polish (and do not appear to miss it!), while the colleges and libraries that guard learning must go to them, hat in hand, for money. But he who pays the piper has always called the tune. Learning has not been abolished in America; it has merely been put in the keeping of people who do not know what it is worth.

## Draft B

There is a small anecdote that gets passed around about the decline of learning in this country, and it is worth taking seriously, not because it is true in every detail but because of what it reveals about who now holds the keys to American education. [example needed: the anecdote itself]

Start with the numbers. The population data show that the older settler families, the ones who built the first schools and colleges, no longer make up the majority. Popular humor has noticed this before the sociologists did. The jokes that once poked fun at newcomers now poke fun at the old stock, as if their manners and their bookishness were quaint leftovers from a vanished country. Humor rarely lies about who is winning.

Earlier generations treated schooling with something close to reverence. A parent who had never finished the grades would still sacrifice to keep a child in school, and a town measured itself by its schoolhouse and its library. Learning was not merely useful; it was a mark of seriousness.

Today the people who control education are often those who had little of it. Legislators with thin schooling write the budgets, appoint the boards and set the rules. When someone proposes real standards, they resist, and it is not hard to see why. A standard that would be demanded of a child would, if applied honestly, expose the lawmaker who votes on it. Nobody likes to pass a law that shames himself.

Alongside them come the newly rich. They rise quickly, with money but without polish, and they do not always see the point of the things money cannot buy. The awkward fact is that the institutions guarding learning, the colleges, the museums, the endowed chairs, depend on exactly this money. They cannot afford to offend it.

So the anecdote is not really about one foolish remark. It is about a change of hands, and about what the new owners are willing to keep.

## Draft C

I was told the other day a small story which I cannot get out of my head: [example needed: the anecdote about declining learning]. It is a trifle, and I should not repeat it if it did not seem to me to explain a good deal, for the story is not really about one family, or one town, but about who is now in charge of American education. We need not go to the census for the answer, though the census will give it to any one who asks [number needed: population figures on the older settler stock]. The comic papers have known it longer. Their jokes about the old families, the thin Puritan households with their dwindling nurseries and their faded portraits, are not jokes at all; they are the plain news that the stock which built the schools has ceased to be the majority, and is fast ceasing to be even a large minority.

Now that older generation, whatever its faults, revered schooling. It went without things in order to send its sons to college, and it thought a man who could not spell had something to be ashamed of. To-day the schools are governed, very largely, by men who were poorly taught themselves. I do not say this to sneer. But it is natural enough that a legislator who never learned his grammar should not care to vote for standards which would show him up before the whole State; no one likes to pass a law that shames him. So the standards go, quietly, one by one. Meanwhile a new class has come into money—a great deal of money, and very suddenly—without having had time to come into manners as well. I have nothing against new money; all money was new once. The trouble is that the colleges and libraries and museums, which ought to be guarding learning against every one who would cheapen it, must go to these very families, hat in hand, for their endowments, and they can hardly bite the hand that feeds them. Perhaps that is the real meaning of my little story. I am afraid it is.

## Draft D

I was told the other day a little story—[example needed]—which amused me at the time, and which has not ceased to trouble me since. It is not, I think, a story about one man or one town. It is a story about who now has the say in American education; and that, frankly, is not a laughing matter.

Now it has been pointed out often enough, by people who count heads for a living, that the old settler families are no longer the majority among us. The census says so; [example needed]. The comic papers say so too, and the comic papers are seldom wrong about such things, for they must be understood or starve. The jokes have changed their butt. It is always the descendant of the Pilgrims (it is always he!) who is now the quaint survival, the figure of fun.

I do not mind the jokes. What I mind is what has gone with the old majority. Our grandfathers revered schooling. They were not all learned—not by any means—but they knew that learning was a thing to be looked up to, and they paid for it, and went without for it. Their grandsons, it seems, have handed it over to lawmakers who never had much of it themselves. One is ready to grant that a legislator need not read Greek. But one may ask that he should not stand in the way of standards; and he does stand in the way, because standards would shame him. No man votes willingly for the yardstick that proves him short.

Meanwhile the newly rich families rise, as they have always risen, without polish; that is no crime, and polish may come in a generation or two. But the colleges and libraries and museums, which are supposed to guard learning, must go to these families for money. And he who pays the piper, I have been given to understand, is apt to call the tune. So the little story is not, after all, so very little. It is the whole matter in small.
