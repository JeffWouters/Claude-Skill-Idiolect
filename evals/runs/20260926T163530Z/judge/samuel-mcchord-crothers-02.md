# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-02

## The passage (by the author)

What we call the awakening of the social conscience marks an important step in progress, But, like all progress, it involves hardship to individuals. For the higher moral classes, the saints and the reformers, it is the occasion of wholehearted rejoicing. It is just what they have, all the while, been trying to bring about. But I confess to a sympathy for the middle class, morally considered, the plain people, who feel the pinch. They have invested their little all in the old-fashioned securities, and when these are depreciated they feel that there is nothing to keep the wolf from the door. After reading a few searching articles in the magazines they feel that, so far from being excellent citizens, they are little better than enemies of society. I am not pleading for the predatory rich, but only for the well-meaning persons in moderately comfortable circumstances, whose predatoriness has been suddenly revealed to them.

Many of the most conscientious persons go about with an habitually apologetic manner. They are rapidly acquiring the evasive air of the conscious criminal. It is only a very hardened philanthropist, or an unsophisticated beginner in good works, who can look a sociologist in the eye. Most persons, when they do one thing, begin to apologize for not doing something else. They are like a one-track railroad that has been congested with traffic. They are not sure which train has the right of way, and which should go on the siding. Progress is a series of rear-end collisions.

There is little opportunity for self-satisfaction. The old-fashioned private virtues which used to be exhibited with such innocent pride as family heirlooms are now scrutinized with suspicion. They are subjected to rigid tests to determine their value as public utilities.

Perhaps I may best illustrate the need of some receivership by drawing attention to the case of my friend the Reverend Augustus Bagster.

## Draft A

There was a time when a decent person could feel reasonably sure of being decent. He paid his debts, was kind to his family, did his work honestly and went to bed with a quiet mind. That time seems to be over. The public conscience has woken up, and one of its first acts has been to wake up everybody else.

The comfortable, well-intentioned citizen now learns, often over breakfast, that he is harming society. The things he buys, the way he invests his savings, the house he lives in, the vote he casts or fails to cast: each turns out to have consequences he never considered, and most of them are bad. He did not mean any harm. He simply did not know. But not knowing is no longer an excuse, and the discovery sits heavily on precisely the people most anxious to do right.

The careless are untroubled; it is the conscientious who suffer. They have become a nation of apologisers. They apologise for their comfort, for their ignorance, for having attended to one obligation while another went unmet. The trouble is that the duties have multiplied faster than anyone can rank them. Should one's first care be for one's own household, one's neighbours, the distant stranger, the generations not yet born? Every claim is urgent and every claim is just, and the conscientious person, trying to honour them all, ends by feeling guilty about each.

Worse, the old private virtues no longer count for much on their own. Gentleness, patience, personal honesty are now weighed by their public usefulness, and found wanting if they do not visibly improve society. It is a strange reward for goodness. The awakened conscience asks a great deal of those who are willing to listen, and very little of those who are not.

## Draft B

There was a time when a comfortable citizen, who paid his debts and was kind to his family and went to church with reasonable regularity, could lie down at night with the pleasant assurance that he had done no harm in the world. That time is past. The public conscience has awakened, and one of the first things it has done is to inform a great many well-intentioned people that they have been injuring society all along without knowing it. The discovery is not an agreeable one. It is hard to learn in middle life that his investments, his servants, and even his charities have been contributing to the general mischief.

The effect upon the conscientious is rather distressing to witness. They have become a race of apologisers. They apologise for their incomes and for their pleasures, and they apologise for not doing more than they are doing, though they are already doing a good deal. The trouble is not that they lack a sense of duty; it is that they have too many duties and no one to tell them which comes first. Should a man attend to his own household, or to the needs of the city in which he lives? Each claim is urgent, and each makes him feel guilty for neglecting the others.

What is hardest to bear is that the private virtues are no longer taken at their old valuation. They are now judged by their public usefulness. It is not enough to be honest and gentle; a man must be able to show what his honesty and gentleness have accomplished for the community. I have in mind a clergyman of my acquaintance who is a case in point. ## Draft C

There was a time when a comfortable citizen who paid his debts, was kind to his family, and went to church with reasonable regularity might sleep soundly. He had done what was expected of him. He had not set out to injure anybody, and it did not occur to him that he had. But the public conscience has lately been awakened, and like most things that are awakened suddenly, it is not in the best of tempers. It has discovered that a great many well-meaning persons have been doing harm to society without the least intention of it, and it has undertaken to tell them so.

The effect upon the conscientious is very distressing. The hardened sinner is not much troubled; he never listened to his conscience and sees no reason to begin with the public one. It is the good man who suffers. He has been told that his investments, his purchases, his very comforts are woven into a web of social injury, and he cannot find the end of the thread. He goes about apologizing. He apologizes for what he does, and then, on second thought, for what he has left undone. He has a great many duties, and they all come to him at once, each insisting that it came first. He would gladly obey them if they would only agree among themselves.

Nor is he allowed the consolation of his private virtues. These used to be counted to his credit, as a matter between himself and his Maker. Now they are brought out and examined as to their public usefulness, and many of them do not pass. Patience, it seems, may be only a failure to protest; contentment may be a kind of neglect.

I have in mind a clergyman of my acquaintance, [example needed: the clergyman's circumstances and what befell him]. He had, as far as I could judge, all the virtues that were once thought becoming to his calling. But he had begun to doubt whether they were of any use to anybody, and it was a doubt that did not improve his sermons.

## Draft D

There was a time when a man who paid his debts, loved his family and kept his temper was allowed to consider himself a respectable member of society. He did not ask much of the world, and the world asked little of him, so that he went to bed at night with a quiet conscience. It was not an exacting conscience. But it was his own, and it knew its place.

All this has been changed by the awakening of the public conscience. The comfortable and well-intentioned citizen has been informed, without warning, that he is doing harm, and that he has been doing it for years without the slightest suspicion; his investments, his purchases and even his charities have been working mischief in quarters he never visited and among people whose names he never knew. The discovery is not pleasant. To be told that you are a sinner is an old story, and one that can be borne with fortitude by anyone who has heard it every Sunday. To be told that you are a social nuisance is another thing.

The effect upon conscientious people is painful to observe. They have fallen into a habit of perpetual apology. They apologise for their comforts and for their economies, for what they do and for what they have neglected to do. Which duty comes first they cannot tell, and so they approach every one of them with a deprecating air, as though it perhaps belonged to somebody else and they had taken it up by mistake.

What troubles them most is that the private virtues no longer count for what they did. Kindness, thrift and domestic fidelity are now judged by their public usefulness, and when they are brought before that tribunal they do not always make a good showing. I have in mind a clergyman of my acquaintance, [example needed: what the clergyman did or said that shows the point]. Nobody doubted his goodness. The question was whether his goodness was of any use.

I would not have the public conscience go to sleep again. I only wish it were a little more considerate of those it has so rudely awakened.
