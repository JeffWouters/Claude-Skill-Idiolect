# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-03

## The passage (by the author)

"The other day they found a ruined Arch of Marcus Aurelius in Tripoli, and began to restore it. New Italy is delighted at this confirmation of its claims to sovereignty in North Africa. The newspapers treat Marcus Aurelius as only a forerunner of Giolitti. By the way, I never heard of Giolitti till I came over here. But it seems that he is a very great man. But when ancient and modern history are mixed up it's hard to do any clear thinking. And when you do get a clear thought you find out that it isn't true. You know Dr. Johnson said something to the effect that that man is little to be envied whose patriotism would not gain force upon the plain of Marathon, or whose feelings would not grow warmer among the ruins of Rome. Marathon is a simple proposition. But when one is asked to warm his enthusiasm by means of the Roman monuments, he naturally asks, 'Enthusiasm over what?' Of course, I don't mean to give up. I'm faint though pursuing. But I'm afraid that Rome is not a good place to rest in."

"I'm afraid not," I said, "if you insist on keeping on thinking. It is not a good place in which to rest your mind."

I think Bagster is not the first person who has found intellectual difficulty here. Rome exists for the confusion of the sentimental traveler. Other cities deal tenderly with our preconceived ideas of them. There is one simple impression made upon the mind. Once out of the railway station and in a gondola, and we can dream our dream of Venice undisturbed. There is no doge at present, but if there were one we should know where to place him. The city still furnishes the proper setting for his magnificence. And London with all its vastness has, at first sight, a familiar seeming. The broad and simple outlines of English history make it easy to reconceive the past.

## Draft A

The visitor who comes to Rome with his head full of romantic expectations is likely to be bewildered. He has prepared himself to meditate upon the fall of empires, and he finds that the empire has not altogether consented to fall. The ancient city is not left in decent retirement, as a proper ruin should be. It is continually being called upon to give evidence in the affairs of the present.

I had a companion who felt this keenly. He had come to Rome resolved to be enthusiastic, and he tried. But his enthusiasm had a way of failing him at the critical moment. It was one thing to stand before a triumphal arch and think of the legions; it was quite another to be told that modern Italy had restored an arch in Libya in order to lend the authority of antiquity to the politics of the moment. He could admire the Cæsars as dead men, but he found it difficult to admire them as the supporters of a present political claim. The past, which he had hoped to contemplate at a comfortable distance, kept stepping forward and asking for his vote.

The truth is that the mixing of ancient and current history muddles the mind. One cannot think clearly about two things at once when each is pretending to be the other. The traveller who wishes to feel the grandeur of antiquity is interrupted by the newspapers, and the reader of the newspapers is interrupted by antiquity. Neither receives his undivided attention, and he goes away with an impression that is neither history nor politics, but a confusion of the two.

Other cities are kinder to the stranger. Venice gives one clear impression, and it is precisely the impression he came to receive. London, in its own very different fashion, does the same. In each place the traveller finds what he expected to find, and he is grateful. It is only in Rome that he is obliged to revise his expectations on the spot, and to wonder whether it was the city or himself that was mistaken.

## Draft B

The traveller who comes to Rome with his romance already packed is likely to find that it does not fit. He has come to muse among ruins, and he finds that the ruins have not been left to him. They are in use. The present has laid hold of them and is making them earn their keep.

I had a companion who felt this keenly. He had read his history and was prepared to be moved by it. But it is hard to be moved in the right direction when modern Italy keeps taking the ancient stones and setting them up as arguments. He was shown, with a good deal of pride, how an arch in Libya had been restored, and he was given to understand that the arch was not merely a monument but a title deed. Rome had once been there, and therefore Italy had a right to be there now. My companion tried to be enthusiastic. He was a well-disposed man and did not like to disappoint his hosts. But he confessed to me afterward that he could not tell whether he was expected to admire an antiquity or to vote on a question of foreign policy, and that the uncertainty took all the warmth out of him.

The difficulty is not peculiar to him. When ancient history and current history are mixed together, one does not get a richer compound; one gets confusion. The mind that is trying to think about the Caesars is interrupted by the newspapers, and the mind that is trying to understand the newspapers is led off among the Caesars. Neither line of thought is allowed to come to its conclusion. One ends with a vague feeling that something very important is going on, and a great disinclination to find out what it is.

Other cities are kinder to the visitor. Venice gives him Venice, and nothing else. It is exactly what he had been told it would be, and he has only to look. London is equally obliging in its own way. It gives one clear impression, and the impression agrees with what he brought. He goes home satisfied, having seen what he came to see. Rome gives him everything at once, and he goes home bewildered.

## Draft C

The traveller who comes to Rome with romantic expectations is likely to be bewildered. He has prepared himself to meet the past, and he finds that the past will not stay where it belongs. It keeps coming forward to take part in the affairs of the present, and the present, not to be outdone, keeps reaching back to borrow the dignity of the past.

My companion felt this keenly. He had come to be moved by the ruins, and he was willing to be moved; but he found it hard to summon the proper enthusiasm when he saw how modern Italy puts its ancient monuments to work. An arch in Libya had been restored, and the restoration was offered not simply as a service to learning but as evidence in support of present political claims. The old stones were being called as witnesses in a case that was still before the court. He was not sure whether he was expected to admire them or to vote for them.

The trouble is that ancient history and current history do not mix well in the mind. Each is interesting enough by itself. When they are stirred together the result is a kind of mental fog, in which the Cæsars and the newspapers become confused, and one is no longer certain which century one is thinking in. The ruins cease to be ruins and become arguments. It is a strain upon a visitor who only wished to meditate.

Other cities are kinder. Venice gives one clear impression, and it is the impression one came for. London, too, is exactly what the traveller expected, and he can take it in without any feeling that he is being asked to revise his opinions. Rome gives many impressions at once, and some of them contradict the others.

Perhaps that is the secret of its hold upon us. But it is not what the romantic visitor bargained for.

## Draft D

Rome is a hard city to arrive in with romantic expectations. The visitor comes prepared to be moved: to stand among broken columns and feel the weight of centuries, to be alone with the ancients for an afternoon. What he finds instead is a place where the ancient world will not stay ancient. It keeps being pressed into service for the present, and the effect is bewildering.

I travelled with a companion who wanted very much to feel the proper enthusiasm and found that he could not. The difficulty was not the ruins themselves but what was being done with them. Modern Italy has a habit of using its antiquities to bolster present political claims. An arch in Libya, for instance, had been restored, and the restoration was not simply an act of scholarship; it was presented as a sign that the old empire's reach gave the new state a right to be there. My companion would look at a triumphal monument and begin, dutifully, to imagine legions returning home, and then he would remember the newspaper, and the legions would turn into an argument. I came to think the trouble lies in the mixing. When ancient history and current history are poured into the same vessel, thought is muddled. One cannot properly admire the past when it is being used as evidence in a present quarrel, nor judge the present quarrel clearly when it comes dressed in togas. The mind is asked to hold two sets of feelings at once and ends up holding neither firmly.

Other cities are kinder to the traveller. Venice gives one clear impression, and it is the impression one came for: the water, the palaces, the sense of a city that has finished its history and now simply is. London too, in its different way, matches expectation; it is what one had read it would be, and it does not ask to be interpreted as a claim. Rome gives no such single picture. It offers two, laid over each other, and asks the visitor to sort them out himself.
