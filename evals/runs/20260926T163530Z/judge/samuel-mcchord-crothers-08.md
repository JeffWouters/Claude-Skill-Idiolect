# Judge packet samuel-mcchord-crothers-08

## The passage (by the author)

I cannot take with perfect seriousness Matthew Arnold's oft-quoted lines:—"Achilles ponders in his tent, The kings of modern thought are dumb. Silent they are, though not content, And wait to see the future come. They have the grief men had of yore, But they contend and cry no more."

If that is ever the attitude of the best minds, it is only a momentary one of which they are quickly ashamed. Achilles sulked in his tent when he was pondering not a big problem, but a small grievance. The kings of modern thought who are described seem like kings out of a job. We are inclined to turn from them to the intellectual monarchs de facto. They are the ones who take up the hard job which the representatives of the old regime give up as hopeless. For when the king has abdicated and contends no more—Long live the King!

The real thinkers of any age do not remain long in a blue funk. They always find something important to think about. They always point out something worth doing. They cannot passively wait to see the future come. They are too busy making it.

Matthew Arnold struck a truer note in Rugby Chapel. The true leaders of mankind can never be mere intellectualists. There must be a union of intellectual and moral energy like that which he recognized in his father. To the fainting, dispirited race,—"Ye like angels appear, Radiant with ardour divine, Beacons of hope, ye appear! Languor is not in your heart, Weakness is not in your word, Weariness not on your brow; Ye alight in our van: at your voice Panic, despair, flee away."

When those whom we have looked upon as our intellectual leaders grow disheartened, we must remember that a lost leader does not necessarily mean a lost cause. When those whom we had called the kings of modern thought are dumb, we can find new leadership. "Change kings with us," replied an Irish officer after the panic of the Boyne; "change kings with us, and we will fight you again."

## Draft A

There is a picture of the modern mind, drawn by Matthew Arnold in one of his poems, which has been much admired and a good deal imitated. The thinkers of our time are shown sitting apart, brooding over the ruins of old beliefs, too wise to be deceived and too weary to do anything else. They wait. What they are waiting for they do not know, and they do not seem in any hurry to find out. It is a melancholy picture and a very beautiful one, and I do not believe a word of it.

For it is not the way real thinkers behave. They may sit down among the ruins for a little while; any man is entitled to a rest after a long walk. But it is not in their nature to sit there long. Before the dust has settled they are measuring the ground and wondering what may be built upon it. Despair is a luxury for those who have nothing to do, and the thinker has always a great deal to do. He is too much interested in what comes next to spend his days mourning over what has gone before.

Arnold himself knew better, and said so when he was not writing about the modern mind in general but about his own father. In that poem the leader is not a spectator of his age. He is one of those in whom intellect and moral force have come together, so that what he sees he is bound to act upon. He does not ask whether the times are favorable. He goes out and makes them a little more so. This seems to me a truer picture, and not only of one good man.

I am not disposed to deny that leaders sometimes lose heart. They are human, and the road is long. But the cause does not stand still while they recover. ## Draft B

There is a poem of Matthew Arnold's in which the modern mind sits apart and broods. It has seen the old faiths go, it has no great confidence in the new ones, and it waits, with a melancholy that is not without a certain dignity, for something to happen. The picture is a handsome one, and a good many readers have been glad to recognise themselves in it. It is flattering to be told that one's low spirits are the low spirits of an age.

But I doubt whether the picture is true of the people who actually do the thinking. The brooding mind is a real enough creature, and it writes very good verse. It is not, however, the mind that leads. The real thinker may be as troubled as anybody at the passing of what he was brought up to believe; he may even sit down among the ruins for an afternoon. He does not sit there long. Before the dust has settled he is measuring the ground, and before the old house is quite cleared away he has begun to argue about the plan of the new one.

Arnold himself knew this, and he said it better in another poem, the one he wrote about his father. There the leader is not a spectator at all. He is a man in whom the intellect and the moral force have come together, so that he sees what ought to be done and has the heart to do it. It is a much less fashionable figure than the brooder. It is also, I think, a much more accurate portrait of the men who have in fact carried the world forward.

It may be objected that leaders, too, lose heart. They do, and it is no great matter. When the men at the head grow weary, other men take their places, and the work goes on under new management. ## Draft C

There is a fashionable picture of the modern mind which I do not believe. It shows the thinkers of the age standing among the ruins of old certainties, too honest to believe what their fathers believed and too discouraged to believe anything else, and so falling silent. They brood. They wait. They regret. The picture has a certain melancholy dignity, and it has found its finest expression in a poem of Matthew Arnold's, [title needed], where the modern spirit is shown suspended between one world that is dead and another that cannot yet be born.

It is a beautiful poem, and as a description of how things feel at a bad moment it may be true. As a description of what thinking people actually do, it is not. Brooding of that passive kind is a mood, not a vocation. The real thinker may pass through it, but he does not settle there. Before long he is at work again, and the work is precisely the shaping of whatever is to come next. The old certainties go; he begins, at once, to lay down new ones, or at least to clear the ground on which others will.

Arnold himself, oddly enough, supplies the better picture. In the poem he wrote about his father, [title needed], he shows a very different kind of leader: one in whom intellect and moral force are not separated but joined, so that thought turns naturally into direction and conviction into action. That figure does not sit among the ruins. He gathers people, steadies them, and leads them on. If we want a portrait of what the best minds of any age are for, it is that poem we should take, not the other.

And suppose the leaders do lose heart, as leaders sometimes do. The work does not stop because they have stopped. Others step into their places, often men nobody had thought of, and carry it forward. There is a remark attributed to an Irish officer after the Boyne, [quotation needed], which puts the point with a soldier's bluntness: a defeat of the men in charge is not a defeat of the cause, and the fight can always be taken up again under new command.

The silence of despair is real enough. It is simply never the end of the story.

## Draft D

It has become the fashion to speak of the leading minds of our time as though they had fallen silent in despair. The old faiths are gone, we are told, and the new ones are not yet born, and between the two the thinker sits with folded hands and broods. Matthew Arnold has put this mood into a poem, and he has done it so well that many readers have taken the poem for a description of the facts. [example needed: a line or image from the poem] The modern mind, as he presents it, is a melancholy spectator. It watches the procession of events and is too sorrowful to interfere.

I do not believe it. The mood is genuine enough, but it is temporary, and it is not the characteristic mood of the people who count. A man who really thinks cannot brood for very long. Sooner or later his thinking gets the better of his melancholy, and he begins, almost without meaning to, to shape the thing that is coming next. The despair is an interval between two kinds of work.

Arnold himself knew this better than his poem admits. In the verses he wrote about his father he drew quite another picture, and to my mind a truer one. There the leader is not a spectator at all. He is a man in whom intellect and moral force are united, who sees clearly and acts upon what he sees, and who carries others along with him because he does not stop to wonder whether the journey is worth while. That is the kind of leadership the world has always had when it needed it; the brooding figure is only what we see in the pause.

And if the leaders themselves lose heart, the situation is still not so desperate as it appears. They are replaced. An Irish officer after the Boyne understood this perfectly and said as much. [example needed: the officer's words] His point was not that leaders never fail, but that their failure is not the end of the enterprise. When one company of captains grows discouraged, another is always waiting, impatient to take its turn.
