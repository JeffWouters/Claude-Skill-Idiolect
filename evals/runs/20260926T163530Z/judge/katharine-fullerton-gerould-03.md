# Judge packet katharine-fullerton-gerould-03

## The passage (by the author)

I may have seemed to be showing rather the reasons for the extirpation of culture among us than the fact of the extirpation. Perhaps that is not the best way to go to work. But the actual evidence is so multitudinously at hand that it was hardly worth while beginning with solemn proofs of the fact. In all branches of art and learning we have a cult of the modern. Modern languages rank Latin and Greek in our schools and colleges; practical and "vocational" training is displacing the rudiments of learning in all of our public and many of our private institutions for the teaching of the young; the books admitted to the lists of "literature" include many that never have been and never will be literature. I found, a few years ago, the following books on a list from which students of English were allowed to choose their reading for the course—this, in one of the old and respectable high schools of Massachusetts, not twenty miles from Boston: Soldiers of Fortune, Pushing to the Front, Greifenstein, Doctor Latimer, The Prisoner of Zenda, The Honorable Peter Stirling, The First Violin, and "any of the works of Stewart Edward White." These, and many others, may be, in their way, good reading, but there is no excuse for offering them to the young student of English as examples of "literature."

Standards of beauty and truth are no longer rigidly held up. In philosophy we have produced pragmatism; in art we have produced futurism—and what not, since then?—in literature we have produced the pathologic and the economic novel, and no poetry worth speaking of. The "grand style" has gone out; and the classics are back numbers. Our children do not even speak good English; and no one minds. They cannot be bored with Scott and Dickens; they cannot be bored with poetry at all. And why should they, when their fathers and mothers are reading Laddie and The Sick-a-Bed Lady, and their clergymen are preaching about The Inside of the Cup—or the latest work dealing with the slums by some one who was slum-born and slum-bred and is proud of it? You can be slum-born and slum-bred and still achieve something worth while; but it is a stupid inverted snobbishness to be proud of it. If one had a right to be proud of anything, it would be of a continued decent tradition back of one. The cultured person must have put in a great many years with nothing to show for it; his parents have usually put in a great many years, for him, for which they have nothing to show. There is nothing to show, until you get the complex result of the disciplined and finished creature. "Culture" means a long receptivity to things of the mind and the spirit. There is no money in it; there is nothing striking in it; there is in it no flattery of our own time, or of the majority.

## Draft A

Anyone who wants evidence that serious cultural standards are disappearing in America does not have to look very hard. The signs are everywhere, and most of them are presented to us not as losses but as progress.

Begin with the schools. The subjects that once formed the core of an education, the old languages, the classics, the long reading of history and literature, have been pushed to the margins. In their place come modern subjects and vocational courses, which promise something practical and immediate. There is nothing wrong with learning a trade. What is wrong is the assumption that this is all education is for. The clearest example I know is a reading list issued in Massachusetts, of all places, which counted popular fiction as literature. [example needed: titles on the list] The state that once set the tone for American learning had quietly decided that whatever people happen to be reading is, by definition, worth reading.

The same drift shows up well beyond the classroom. Art, philosophy and fiction have each walked away from the ideals that used to steady them. Art no longer aims much at beauty, philosophy no longer aims much at wisdom, and fiction no longer aims much at showing us how to live. Each has found it easier to entertain, to shock or to comment on the passing moment. And the public has gone along. Children prefer light reading, which is natural enough in children. But their parents prefer it too, and that is harder to excuse. A household where nobody reaches for a difficult book is a household where the habit will not be passed on.

This matters because real cultivation is slow. It is not something a person acquires in a semester or a family acquires in one lifetime. It takes generations of steady effort, most of it unrewarded. A father reads to his children things they will not appreciate for many years. A mother insists on standards of speech and manners that win her no thanks. None of this earns money, and very little of it earns flattery. Cultivation is, in the plainest commercial sense, a bad investment. That is exactly why it is rare, and why it is so easily lost when a society begins to judge everything by what it pays.

There is one more symptom worth naming. It has become fashionable to boast of humble origins, to announce that one grew up with no books in the house and made one's way without any of that old-fashioned polish. The boast is meant as modesty, but it is really a kind of reverse snobbery. It turns the absence of cultivation into a badge of honor and quietly suggests that those who did inherit it should apologize. A society that sneers at inherited learning will not have much of it to inherit.

None of these signs is decisive on its own. Taken together, they point in one direction, and it is not upward. If we want serious standards to survive, someone will have to keep them without expecting to be paid or praised for it.

## Draft B

It is not pleasant to say that a thing one loves is dying, and I should be glad to be told I am wrong. But the evidence accumulates, and I do not see how any one who looks at it honestly can escape the conclusion that the serious standards of culture are vanishing from American life. Take the schools first, since it is in the schools that a nation shows what it really values. Everywhere the older studies are giving way to the modern and the vocational; the boy is to be fitted for a trade, and the girl for an office, and whatever cannot be sold across a counter is put down as a luxury. The painter no longer tries to paint what is beautiful; the philosopher has ceased to ask what is good; the novelist is content to be amusing, and is not always that. And the public does not complain. Children, left to themselves, reach for the lightest thing on the shelf, which is natural enough in children. What is not natural is that their parents should do the same, and should be rather proud of it. [example needed: an instance of parents preferring light reading]

The truth is that real cultivation is a slow and thankless business. It is not got in a season, nor in a lifetime; it takes generations of patient effort, most of it unrewarded. It brings in no money. It wins no flattery—quite the contrary, for the cultivated man is suspected of thinking himself better than his neighbours, and his neighbours make him pay for it. Small wonder that people who have to choose between culture and comfort choose comfort. But I confess I have less patience with a newer habit, which is the boasting of humble origins. A man tells you, with the air of one producing a certificate of virtue, how little his people had [example needed: an instance of boasting of humble origins]. It is meant to be modest. It is not modest in the least; it is snobbery turned inside out, and quite as tiresome as the other kind. There is no merit in having come from nothing, as there is no merit in having come from something; the merit lies in what one does with the inheritance. And if the inheritance is to be thrown away—if every family is to begin again from the beginning, and be proud of it—then the standards will not merely vanish. They will not be missed.

## Draft C

I do not know how other people feel about it, but I am exceedingly uneasy about the state of culture in this country. I do not mean that we have no books, or no pictures, or no schools; we have more of all three than ever before. I mean that the serious standards by which such things used to be judged are quietly going out of use, and that hardly any one seems to notice.

Look first at the schools. They favour, more and more, the modern subjects and the vocational subjects—the things that will get a boy a place in an office or a girl a place behind a counter. One is ready to grant that boys and girls must earn their living. But the older studies, which had nothing to do with earning a living, are being pushed to the wall. I have seen a reading list put out in Massachusetts—in Massachusetts, of all places!—which counted popular fiction of the day as literature, side by side with [example needed]. If this can happen in the old home of the Puritan schoolmaster, I cannot think where it will not happen.

Nor is it only the schools. Art has given up its older ideals; so has philosophy; so, most of all, has fiction. They no longer try to lift the reader to anything; they are content to meet him where he is. And where he is, it must be admitted, is not very high. The children prefer light reading, which is natural enough in children. But the parents prefer it too, which is not natural at all, or was not, in the generation before them. The mother who once read aloud from [example needed] now reads, if she reads at all, the magazine story (it is always the magazine story!) and is quite pleased with herself for doing so much.

Now real cultivation is not a thing that can be got in a hurry. It takes generations of effort, and the effort is not rewarded. It does not earn money; if anything, it costs money. It does not win flattery; the cultivated man is more likely to be laughed at than admired. His father and his grandfather worked for it without any hope of return, simply because they believed it worth having; and he inherits it, as he inherits the family silver, only because they did not sell it. Break that chain once, and it will not be mended in a single lifetime.

This is not snobbishness. Indeed, the snobbery nowadays runs the other way. It is quite the fashion to boast of humble origins, to tell the world that one's grandfather could not read, and that one has got on very well without reading either. I have been given to understand that this is democratic. It seems to me simply snobbery turned inside out—a pride in having nothing, which is no better than a pride in having much, and is a good deal more dangerous. For the man who is proud of having no culture will certainly not trouble to acquire any; and he will see to it, if he can, that nobody else is admired for having it.

## Draft D

It is not pleasant to say that serious standards of culture are disappearing in America, and I should be glad to be told that I am wrong. But the evidence has a way of piling up. Look first at the schools. They have turned, more and more, to modern and vocational subjects—to what will be useful on Monday morning—and the old studies that were useful to nobody in particular, and therefore to everybody, have been quietly pushed into a corner. I do not say that a boy should not learn a trade. I say only that he should not be told the trade is an education. In Massachusetts, of all places, a reading list was drawn up for the schools on which popular fiction was counted as literature [example needed: the titles on the list]. One reads such a list twice, to be sure one has not misread it; and then one sits down.

Nor is it only the schools. Art, philosophy and fiction have each, in their own fashion, abandoned the older ideals they once served. The painter no longer tries to see truly, the philosopher no longer tries to think things through to the end, and the novelist no longer tries—or so it seems to me—to show us anything we did not know before we opened his book. The children, meanwhile, prefer light reading; that is natural enough, and it was always so. What is new is that the parents prefer it too. It used to be the business of the grown-up to hold up something a little beyond the child's reach. Now the grown-up has stopped reaching.

Why should this be? Partly, I think, because real cultivation is so very slow. It is not got in a season, or in a college course, or even in a single life. It takes generations of patient and unrewarded effort; and the effort earns nothing that the world knows how to count. It will not make a man rich. It will not make him admired, either, except by the few who have made the same effort and know what it cost. A society that measures everything by money or by flattery will naturally conclude that such labour is wasted. Why toil for a thing that pays in neither coin?

There is one more symptom, and perhaps the oddest. We have all met the man who boasts of his humble origins—who tells us, with a kind of defiant relish, how little schooling he had and how well he has done without it. He thinks himself the opposite of a snob. He is nothing of the sort; he is simply a snob turned inside out. To be proud of never having read a book is as foolish as to be proud of having read a great many, and rather less forgivable, since it closes the door on the next generation. The old snobbery at least paid learning the compliment of envy. This new kind does not even do that.
