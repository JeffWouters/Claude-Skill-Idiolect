# Judge packet synthetic-idris-02

## The passage (by the author)

What is a handover really for? The official answer is simple enough: when someone leaves a team, the knowledge that lived in their head must be moved somewhere else before they go, and so we arrange a few meetings, write a document with a hopeful title and transfer whatever systems still bear their name. Yet anyone who has sat through one of these conversations, on either side of the table, knows that very little of what matters changes hands in them. The person leaving is tired, a little guilty and already half elsewhere; the person receiving does not yet know which questions to ask, since the questions only appear much later, at inconvenient hours, in front of a failing deployment. It may be that the handover survives less as a transfer of knowledge than as a ritual of departure, a way for a team to mark that someone is going and that the work will somehow continue. I suspect that is not a lesser purpose, and that it deserves more seriousness than we usually give it.

Consider the experience of the one who is leaving. They sit down with a blank page and try to write out everything they know, and discover almost at once that most of it cannot be written, since it is not really a set of facts but a feel for the system—which alerts can safely wait until morning, which colleague in another team will answer a message kindly, which part of the codebase is fragile in ways no test will reveal. What ends up on the page is the part that was easiest to articulate, which is rarely the part that was most valuable. Who among us has not opened a colleague's parting document, full of care and good intentions, and found that it answers every question except the one we have? There is no failure of effort here; the difficulty is that the knowledge was never held as knowledge at all, but as habit, attention and a long accumulation of small judgements.

## Draft A

What is a handover meeting really for? When an engineer leaves, the usual practice is to schedule a session or two in which they walk a colleague through the systems they looked after, the open tickets, the scripts whose purpose is known only to them. The intention is sensible enough, and nobody would argue against it. It may be, however, that such meetings pass on remarkably little. The recipient sits through an hour of explanation without yet knowing which parts will matter, and so cannot know what to ask; the questions that would have been worth asking tend to arise weeks later, when the person who could have answered them has already gone.

Part of the difficulty is that what a departing engineer knows is mostly not the kind of thing that can be written down. Their documentation, however careful, records what they did; it rarely records the judgement behind it, the sense of which alarms can safely be ignored and which ones mean trouble, or the instinct that a particular change is riskier than it looks. This is tacit knowledge, and it tends to survive in notes only as a faint outline of itself. You might read the notes a departing colleague leaves behind with great care and still miss what mattered most, simply because it was never on the page.

There is also something else going on, which I suspect we are inclined to overlook. The handover is one of the few moments at which a team formally marks the departure of one of its members, and it seems to me that it may matter more as a farewell ritual than as a transfer of information. To sit with a colleague and hear, perhaps for the first time, the full extent of what they have been quietly carrying is a form of acknowledgement—and one that deserves more respect than it usually receives when it is treated as an administrative task to be completed before the last day. Perhaps the handover passes on less knowledge than we hope, but more recognition than we notice.

## Draft B

When an engineer hands in their notice, the team usually responds the same way. A handover gets scheduled. The leaver books a few meetings with whoever inherits their work, walks through the systems they looked after, and writes a document that tries to capture everything they know. Everyone treats this as the moment when knowledge moves from one head to another.

In practice, very little moves. The recipient sits through the sessions, nods, and takes notes, but they cannot yet know what to ask. The questions that matter only appear months later, when something breaks at an awkward hour or a decision comes up that nobody remembers making. By then the person who could have answered is gone. The handover meeting happens at exactly the point when the new owner is least equipped to use it.

The written notes have a different problem. Most of what a departing engineer knows is not the kind of thing you can write down. It is tacit judgement: which alert can be ignored and which one means trouble, which part of the code is fragile, why a strange-looking workaround exists, who to call when a supplier goes quiet. [example needed] The documentation captures the procedures and misses the instincts, and the instincts are what mattered most.

None of this means the handover is pointless. It may simply be doing a different job from the one we assign it. A handover is also a farewell ritual. It is the moment when a team acknowledges that someone spent years looking after something, and that their care is being passed on rather than just dropped. The leaver gets to say what they built and what they worry about. The recipient gets to show they take it seriously.

Seen that way, the process deserves more respect, not less. Give it proper time instead of squeezing it into the final afternoon. Let the leaver tell the stories as well as the steps. And be honest that the notes will not be enough, so that nobody is surprised when the real questions arrive later.

## Draft C

When an engineer leaves a team, there is usually a handover meeting, and it is usually understood as the moment at which their knowledge passes to whoever will carry the work forward. In practice these meetings pass on remarkably little. The difficulty is not that the departing engineer is careless or the recipient inattentive; it is that the recipient cannot yet know what to ask, since the questions that matter will only become visible months later, when something behaves strangely and the person who might have explained it is no longer there to be asked.

Part of the reason is that most of what a leaver knows is not the kind of thing that can be written down. It is tacit judgement rather than recorded fact: a sense of which parts of a system are fragile, which warnings can safely be ignored, which colleagues to consult before changing something that looks harmless. Written notes capture the procedures well enough, and they are worth having, but they tend to miss precisely what matters most, because the person writing them no longer notices the judgements they make without thinking. We might ask them to try harder, and perhaps they would, yet I suspect the gap would remain much the same.

If that is so, it may be worth thinking about the handover differently. It seems to me that the process matters less as a transfer of knowledge than as a farewell ritual, one of the few moments at which a team formally acknowledges that someone's work has shaped the place and that their departure leaves a real absence. Rituals of this kind deserve a certain respect. They give the leaver an occasion to say what they are proud of and what they worry about, and they give those who remain an occasion to listen with some care, which is rather different from taking notes. Would we treat these meetings more seriously if we saw them this way? I think we would—and we might, as a consequence, also learn a little more from them than we currently do.

## Draft D

When an engineer leaves a team, the organisation usually responds with a handover: a series of meetings in which the departing person walks a colleague through the systems, the open tasks and the known problems, often supported by a document written in the final weeks. The intention is sensible enough, and I would not want to argue against it. But it is worth asking how much actually passes from one person to the other in these sessions. My impression is that it is rather less than we suppose, and for a reason that is difficult to avoid: the recipient does not yet know what to ask. They are being shown a landscape they have never had to navigate, and the questions that would draw out the useful knowledge will only occur to them months later, when something breaks and the person who could have answered is gone.

Part of the difficulty lies in the nature of what the leaver knows. Most of it is not a list of facts that could be written down but a kind of judgement, built up over years—a sense of which alerts can safely be ignored, which parts of the system are fragile, which requests from which people tend to conceal a larger problem. Written notes capture the facts reasonably well and the judgement hardly at all, which means they tend to record precisely the things that matter least. This is not a fault of the person writing them; it is simply that tacit knowledge resists being made explicit, and a few weeks of effort cannot change that.

It may be, then, that the handover matters for a different reason than the one we give it. Perhaps it is better understood as a farewell, a ritual by which a team acknowledges that someone has been part of its work and is now leaving it. Seen this way, the meetings deserve a certain respect even if they transfer little, because they mark something real: the end of a contribution, and the recognition that it was worth something. I suspect we would conduct them more gracefully if we admitted as much.
