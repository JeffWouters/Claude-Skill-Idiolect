# Judge packet synthetic-idris-06

## The passage (by the author)

Why do we find it so hard to name things? Most of us who have worked on software for any length of time will have sat through a conversation in which a group of capable people, faced with a new service, a new table or a new concept in the domain, spent considerably longer choosing what to call it than they had spent deciding what it should do. The conventional explanation is that naming is a technical matter of precision, and that a good name is simply one that describes its referent accurately enough for a stranger to guess what it holds. That explanation is correct as far as it goes; yet it does not quite account for the heat the question generates, or for the curious reluctance we feel about settling it. It may be that a name is less a description than a promise, a small public statement about what the thing is and, just as importantly, what it will never be allowed to become. I suspect that what we are really negotiating, when we argue about a name, is the future.

Consider what happens in the moment after a name has been agreed. For a little while it feels provisional, a placeholder that everyone privately assumes will, perhaps, be revisited once the shape of the work is clearer; then it appears in a ticket, on a dashboard and in a conversation with another team, and before long it has quietly hardened into something nobody quite remembers choosing. The name begins to do work of its own—it tells the newcomer where to look, it tells the reviewer what belongs and what does not, it tells the funders of the work what they are paying for. Is it any wonder that we hesitate? A team that calls something a scheduler has, in some sense, already decided that it will not also send messages, and a team that calls it a platform has invited every neighbouring group to bring it their problems. I suspect the hesitation, seen this way, is not indecision so much as an honest recognition that we are about to commit ourselves in public.

## Draft A

Anyone who has worked on a software team has seen it happen. A new service, module or feature needs a name, and the discussion runs longer than the one about what the thing should actually do. People who agreed quickly on the design argue at length about a single word. From the outside it looks like bikeshedding, and sometimes it is. But accuracy alone does not explain how intense these debates get. If the only goal were a correct label, most names would be settled quickly, because most candidates are roughly correct.

What the debate is really about is commitment. A name is a statement of what something is, and by the same stroke it is a statement of what it is not. Call a component a "scheduler" and you have implied it schedules things, and also that it does not do the other jobs someone might later want to add to it. Every name closes off some future directions. People sense this, even when they cannot put it into words, and the ones who care about those directions push back.

The usual escape is to pick a provisional name and move on. That feels sensible: nobody wants to hold up the work over a word, and you can always rename it later. In practice, "later" rarely comes. The provisional name ends up in code, in dashboards, in documentation and in the way people talk in meetings. Before long it has hardened, and renaming it now means touching all of those places. So the placeholder stays, and it starts to shape the scope of the thing it was only meant to label.

The clearest example is the word "platform". Calling something a platform tells everyone else in the organisation that it is meant to be built upon. That is an invitation, and people accept it. Requests start arriving from teams you never planned to serve, each reasonable on its own terms, each pulling the work in a new direction. The name did that, not the design.

So the long argument over a name is often a scope argument wearing a disguise. Treating it as one, and saying out loud what the name commits you to, tends to make it shorter and more useful.

## Draft B

Anyone who has worked in a software team for long enough will have sat through a discussion about a name that ran considerably longer than the discussion about what the thing should actually do. It is tempting to dismiss this as a kind of indulgence, the sort of argument people have when the harder questions feel too large, and there is perhaps some truth in that. The intensity of these debates, however, is difficult to explain by a concern for accuracy alone. People who would happily accept an imperfect function signature will resist a name with surprising conviction, and it seems to me that they are responding, often without quite saying so, to something real.

What a name does, I think, is commit us to a view of what something is. To call a component a scheduler is to say that it schedules, and by implication that it does not do the several other things it might one day have been asked to do; the name draws a boundary around the future as much as it describes the present. Those who argue over it are arguing, in effect, about which directions will remain open and which will quietly close. This would matter less if names were easily revised, but in practice they rarely are. A name chosen provisionally, in the expectation that something better will come along, tends to harden within weeks, as it spreads into code, documentation and conversation, until changing it would cost more than anyone is willing to spend. It then begins to shape the scope of the thing it names, since people reasonably assume that the thing is what it is called. Calling something a platform, for instance, is not a neutral description; it is an invitation, and other teams will accept it, arriving with requests that a more modest name might never have attracted. Perhaps the lesson is not that we should argue less about names but that we should recognise what the argument is really about—and give it the attention that a decision about scope deserves, rather than the impatience we usually reserve for questions of taste.

## Draft C

Anyone who has spent time on a software team will have noticed how much energy can go into choosing a name. A discussion about what a service should be called may run longer, and grow warmer, than the discussion about what it should actually do—and the participants are rarely unreasonable people. We tend to explain this as a concern for accuracy, and accuracy certainly matters; a misleading name is a small trap laid for every future reader. Yet accuracy alone does not seem sufficient to account for the intensity. People do not usually argue this hard over whether a description is precise. Something else, one feels, must be at stake.

What seems to be at stake is commitment. To name a thing is to say what it is, and by the same act to say what it is not. A name draws a boundary around the work, admitting some possibilities and quietly ruling out others, and those who care about the directions being ruled out are right to sense that a decision is being made on their behalf. The argument over the word is, in many cases, an argument over the future of the thing, conducted in the only vocabulary the moment allows.

We often try to defuse such arguments by choosing something provisional, a working title that we promise to revisit once the shape of the work is clearer. This is a sensible instinct, and it rarely works as intended. Provisional names have a way of hardening almost as soon as they are spoken: they appear in repository names and dashboards and meeting invitations, they are adopted by colleagues who were never told they were temporary, and before long they have begun to shape the scope of the very thing they were meant merely to label. Consider what happens when a team calls its work a platform. The word is modest enough on the page, but it carries an invitation, and other teams will reasonably take it up, arriving with requests the original authors never intended to serve. Is the name then wrong, or has it simply become true? It is, perhaps, a little of both—which is precisely why the conversation deserves the care it so often receives, even when that care looks excessive from the outside.

## Draft D

Why should a name provoke more argument than the thing it names? Anyone who has worked on a software team will have seen a discussion about what to call a service or a module run on far longer than the discussion about what it should actually do, and the usual explanation, that names matter because they must be accurate, never seems quite sufficient to account for the heat involved. Accuracy is a real concern, certainly, but people do not generally raise their voices over precision alone. It may be that the intensity comes from somewhere else—from a sense, rarely articulated, that choosing a name is a commitment about what the thing is and, by implication, about everything it will not be allowed to become.

A name, in other words, is not merely a label attached after the fact. It draws a boundary around the work, and whoever proposes one is also proposing, perhaps without realising it, which future directions will seem natural and which will seem like a departure. The people who object to a name are frequently objecting to that boundary; they can see a direction they care about being quietly excluded, and the argument over a word becomes the only available place to defend it.

There is also the matter of how quickly provisional names harden. We tend to reassure ourselves that a name is only temporary, something to be revisited once the design has settled, and yet within a few weeks it has appeared in documentation, in dashboards, in the way colleagues describe the work to one another, and changing it would require more effort than anyone is prepared to spend. By then the name has begun to shape the scope rather than describe it. Calling something a platform, for instance, is arguably an invitation: other teams hear the word and reasonably conclude that they may bring their requests, and a piece of work that began with a narrow purpose finds itself answering to people it was never designed to serve.

It seems to me that the lengthy debates over naming are therefore less wasteful than they appear, since they are often the first honest conversation a team has about what it is building and for whom.
