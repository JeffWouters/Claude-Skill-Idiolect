# Judge packet synthetic-idris-05

## The passage (by the author)

What is a backlog, really, once it has grown past the point at which anyone can read it from beginning to end? The official answer is that it is a queue of work waiting its turn, ordered by value and consumed from the top, and in a young team with a short list that is roughly accurate. Yet most backlogs do not behave like queues; items enter far more quickly than they leave, a considerable proportion are never touched again, and the list grows until it becomes a kind of archive that nobody consults and nobody feels able to close. It may be that the backlog survives in this form because it does something other than schedule work. I suspect it serves, in some sense, as the place where a team keeps its deferred promises—the request it could not refuse, the improvement it genuinely meant to make, the complaint it wished to acknowledge without having to answer—and that its real function is less to organise the future than to make the present bearable.

Consider what happens when a colleague from another team asks for something reasonable that there is simply no time to do. To decline outright feels unkind, and to agree feels dishonest, since everyone involved knows the coming months are already spoken for; the ticket offers a third way, in which the request is written down, given a label and a priority, and placed somewhere safe. The colleague leaves with the sense of having been heard, and the team leaves without having committed to anything in particular, which is, arguably, a small act of diplomacy rather than a failure of planning. Is it any wonder that the list keeps growing? Each item is a courtesy extended at the moment it was needed, and the cost of that courtesy is passed on to some later reader who will find, a long time afterwards, a request whose author has moved on and whose context has quietly evaporated.

## Draft A

What is a backlog actually for? We tend to describe it as a queue, a list of work waiting its turn, and for a small team with a modest list this description is accurate enough. It may be that past a certain size, however, the backlog quietly stops behaving like a queue at all, since many of the items in it will never be picked up, and most of the people who maintain it know this perfectly well even if nobody says so during planning. The list keeps growing, the top of it keeps shifting, and the bottom becomes a place where requests go rather than a place from which they return.

If it is not a queue, then what is it? I suspect it is better understood as a repository of postponed commitments, and of requests the team cannot fulfil but would prefer not to decline. Logging a ticket is a remarkably convenient gesture; it allows us to avoid refusing someone without obliging us to promise them anything, and it lets the conversation conclude politely on both sides. The person who asked leaves with the impression that their request has been heard, which in a narrow sense it has; the team leaves without having accepted a deadline it knows it could not meet. Perhaps this is the genuine function of the oversized backlog, which is not to organise work but to absorb the discomfort of saying no.

The difficulty is that the cost of this politeness is deferred rather than avoided. Whoever reads the backlog later, whether a newcomer or the same team several months on, inherits a collection of items that have gone stale and lost their context, written by people who may since have moved elsewhere, for reasons that were never recorded. It seems to me that a smaller backlog, honestly pruned, would demand a little more courage at the moment of asking. Perhaps that is a price worth paying, since the alternative is simply a refusal we have postponed and left for someone else to make.

## Draft B

Most teams talk about the backlog as if it were a queue. Work goes in at one end, gets prioritised, and comes out the other end as something shipped. That picture holds while the backlog is small. Past a certain size it stops being true, and the backlog starts doing a different job altogether.

The simplest sign is that many items are never picked up. They are not waiting their turn; there is no turn coming. New work arrives faster than old work leaves, and whatever sits near the bottom stays there until someone archives it in a clean-up or the tool itself is replaced. Calling that a queue is generous. It is closer to a storage room.

What gets stored there is worth looking at. Some of it is postponed commitment: things the team agreed were worth doing, just not now, and "not now" quietly became "not ever". The rest is requests the team cannot fulfil, because the capacity, the skills or the mandate are not there. Neither kind is going anywhere, but both are kept as though they might.

There is a reason for this, and it is not laziness. Logging a ticket is a way to avoid two uncomfortable conversations. You do not have to refuse the person asking, and you do not have to promise them a date you cannot meet. The ticket says "noted", which sounds like progress to the requester and costs the team nothing in the moment. As a social tool it works very well.

The cost comes later, and it lands on someone else. Whoever reads the item months on inherits a stale request with no context. The person who raised it may have moved on. The problem it described may have been solved, or may have changed shape. The reasoning behind the priority it was given is gone. So the reader has to reconstruct a decision nobody actually made, or ignore it and feel vaguely guilty.

An oversized backlog, then, is less a plan than a record of things a team chose not to say out loud. Seeing it that way makes it easier to decide what it should hold.

## Draft C

We tend to speak of a backlog as though it were a queue, a list of work waiting its turn, and for a small team with a short list this description is accurate enough. Past a certain size, however, the metaphor quietly stops working. Items are added far faster than they are removed, and a great many of them will never be picked up at all, not because anyone has decided against them but because no one ever reaches that far down. What the backlog holds at that point is perhaps better described as a store than as a queue: a place where postponed commitments accumulate alongside requests the team has no realistic prospect of fulfilling. It seems to me that this is not a failure of discipline so much as a sign that the backlog has taken on a different job from the one we assigned it.

That job, I suspect, is largely social. Logging a ticket allows us to avoid two uncomfortable conversations at once; we need neither refuse the person asking nor promise them something we cannot deliver. The request is acknowledged, given a number and set aside, and everyone may leave the exchange feeling that something has been done. This is an understandable kindness, and in the moment it might even be the wisest response available. The difficulty is that the cost does not disappear; it is merely deferred to whoever reads the ticket later. That reader inherits an item whose context has evaporated, whose author may have moved on, and whose original urgency can no longer be judged. They must decide whether it still matters without any of the information that would allow them to decide well. Would we log quite so freely if we pictured that reader clearly? Perhaps not, and perhaps the more honest course would be to treat a large backlog less as a plan than as an archive of things we were reluctant to say no to—and to read it, when we read it at all, with that in mind.

## Draft D

We usually speak of the backlog as though it were a queue, a line of work waiting patiently for its turn, and for a small team with a modest list this description is perfectly fair. Past a certain size, however, the metaphor quietly stops working. Items are added faster than they are taken away; the oldest ones sink out of sight; and a good many of them, if we are honest, will never be picked up at all. A list of that kind is no longer a queue in any useful sense. What, then, is it?

My own view is that an oversized backlog becomes something closer to an archive of deferrals. It holds the commitments we have postponed without quite abandoning, and the requests we have no realistic prospect of fulfilling but could not bring ourselves to turn down. Logging a ticket is, after all, a remarkably comfortable response to an awkward conversation. It allows us to avoid saying no, which feels unkind, and to avoid saying yes, which would be a promise we could not keep; the request is acknowledged, given a number, and placed somewhere safe, and everyone leaves the meeting with the sense that something has been done. Perhaps something has—though not, as a rule, the thing that was asked for.

The cost of this arrangement is borne later, and usually by someone else. Whoever eventually opens the backlog, whether a new team member, a product owner trying to plan a quarter, or simply the original author a year on, inherits a collection of items whose meaning has drained away. The ticket survives; the context that made it sensible does not. Who asked for this, and why? Is the problem still real, or was it solved long ago by some other route? Nobody can say, and so the item is neither done nor closed but merely carried forward, adding a little more weight to a list that already feels heavier than it should. It may be that the most honest thing a team can do with such a backlog is to admit what it has become, and to decide, deliberately, what it is prepared to say no to.
