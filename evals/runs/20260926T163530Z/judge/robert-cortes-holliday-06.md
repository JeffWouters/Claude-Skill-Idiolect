# Judge packet robert-cortes-holliday-06

## The passage (by the author)

Then one night there came a tinkle in the temperature as of sleigh bells. And the town, the world sank into a soft blanket of white. Were you out then? Ah! you should have been. You were not, I hope, in a parlor with a regulated temperature.

Well, anyhow, everybody else was out. The cross streets of the big city had "all to oncet" taken on the air of a small town "sociable." Shadowy multitudes seemed to sprout up out of the ground. The sidewalks, especially those usually so deserted at this hour, now ahum with dark busy bowing figures, rang and clanged gayly with the sound of scoop and shovel. In the democratic, jovial, village-like spirit of the occasion, many of the workers (those more staid and portly ones) removed their coats. Every here and there an areaway held, in a holiday effect, a cluster of bare-headed maid-servants—the "gallery" of the shovellers, whose presence tended to make of the task of clearing the sidewalk a night-hour lark.

Voices in the street, as you know, and laughter there, is never so musical as above snow-stilled pavements. Then, too, cheery echoes are abroad among the recesses between the houses, in the courts and down the ways where packages are delivered. The shovellers good-naturedly banter one another and pass a cordial jest with those who travel by. And every here and there the rich contralto of negro mirth is heard.

I do not know that the city's parks are not a finer spectacle under snow than in the summer—their dark glistening branches laden à la Christmas card, and, after dark, their festoons of lamps more twinkling and more yellow than at any other time.

Along Broadway what a whirl! The street like an arena, hordes of gladiators in doughty combat with the onslaught of the storm, snow-carts banging and backing about (horses seem to stomp and snort and rear more in a snowstorm than at any other time), new ridiculously miniature "caterpillar tractors" performing like toy tanks at war, traffic in a hilarious tangle, street cars crawling along looking more than ever before like prodigious cat-eyed bugs. Here with a terrific buzz comes one all dark furiously thrashing the snow from side to side by means of revolving brooms beneath. The crowds an animated silhouette against the whitened air. One wants to hop and shout one feels so much alive.

## Draft A

The first heavy snowfall came to the city at night, and the city, which ordinarily regards weather as something that happens to other people, went out of doors to see about it. I do not know that I have ever seen anything quite like it. From every stoop and area-way came householders with shovels, with brooms, with coal scuttles, with anything, apparently, that would move snow. They were not in the least put out. They called to one another across the street; they made jokes, some of them rather poor jokes, about the weather, about the city, about each other's technique with a shovel. Neighbours who ordinarily pass one another with a nod leaned on their shovels and conversed. And in the lighted doorways behind them stood the servants, watching with an expression of mild and superior amusement, as one might watch children at play. It was, one felt, a village gathering, a sort of husking bee, set down for an evening in the middle of a great metropolis.

The parks, I thought, were lovely. The snow lay softly on the lawns and the benches and along every branch of every tree, and the lamps, which on an ordinary night are merely lamps, stood each in a little globe of falling white, like something in a picture on a Christmas card. There was no one about. It was very still.

But the avenue was another matter altogether. Here civilisation had determined to fight back, and the result was magnificent confusion. Great plows came roaring down the middle of the street, throwing up the snow in long curving waves. Carts of every description were being loaded by men with shovels; and small tractors, rather absurd little machines, pushed about in the drifts as though they were enjoying themselves immensely. The streetcars came along with revolving brooms at their fronts, sweeping their own way before them in clouds of white. Meanwhile the ordinary traffic had simply given up. Motors stood where they had stopped, wagons were stalled across the crossings, and nothing moved but the machines that had come to clear the way. Horns sounded; somebody shouted; nobody went anywhere. Why it should be so I cannot say, but the whole spectacle was exhilarating. One walked up the avenue through the storm with the feeling that something was happening, that the city had, for once, been surprised—and was rather enjoying it.

## Draft B

The first real snow of the winter came in the night, and the great city, which ordinarily does not know its neighbours, went out of doors to meet it. Everybody, it appeared, had a shovel. Men who had not handled a shovel since they were boys were out in front of their houses, in their good overcoats, pushing at the sidewalk. They leaned on their shovels and called across the street to one another. One wished to know whether the other had ordered this weather. The other said that he had, and that he would send the bill later. Somebody threw a snowball, not very well. There was laughter up and down the block. And in the doorways and at the basement windows stood the servants, watching, with an air of mild amusement, their employers doing their work. It was like nothing so much as a village. It was like the evening of a fair in a small town, where all the people come out upon the one street and are glad to see each other.

The parks were very lovely. The snow lay along the branches of the trees, and on the benches, and on the tops of the railings, soft and thick, as though it had been laid there by hand. The lamps burned in it with a kind of gentle astonishment. Each lamp had a little room of light about it, full of falling flakes, and beyond the lamp the paths went off, white and silent, into the dark. You could hardly believe that this was the park you crossed every day of your life.

But the avenue—the avenue was another matter. Here the great city had not gone out to play; it had gone out to fight. Plows came pushing up the middle of the street. Carts stood about, and men were filling them. Little tractors, busy as beetles, ran here and there, and nobody seemed to know precisely what they were for. The streetcars came along with their great brushes whirling before them, flinging the snow in clouds to either side. And everything else stood still. Taxicabs stood still. Trucks stood still. A limousine stood still, with a gentleman in the back seat looking at his watch. Horns blew. Men shouted. A policeman waved his arms as though he were conducting an orchestra that would not follow him.

It was a great mess. It was a glorious mess. One walked through it with a sort of foolish lightness in the heart, like a boy let out of school.

## Draft C

The first heavy snow came in the night, and the great city, which ordinarily takes no notice of anything, went out of doors to look at it. It was a curious and charming thing to see. Householders who all the year round would not dream of speaking to one another appeared on their steps in overcoats and old hats, armed with shovels and brooms, and fell to clearing the sidewalks. They joked across the drifts. They lent each other shovels. Somebody was always ready with advice upon the proper way to throw snow, and was laughed at, quite rightly, by his neighbours. Meanwhile the servants stood in the areaways and the lighted doorways and watched their masters labour, with an expression of mild and not unkindly amusement. It was, for an hour or so, a village. The metropolis had forgotten itself.

The parks were lovely. There is perhaps nothing in a city so pretty as a row of park lamps in falling snow. Each lamp wore a little white cap, and each threw a soft yellow ring upon the white ground about it, and the paths ran off between them into a kind of luminous silence. The trees were all traced in white. A few people walked there, slowly, as people walk in a church. I don't know that I have ever seen the place look so much like a picture of itself.

The avenue was another matter altogether. Here the snow was not a spectacle but an enemy, and the whole mechanical civilisation of the town had turned out to fight it. Great plows came grinding along, throwing up waves on either side. Carts of every description lumbered by, piled high with snow, and small tractors, rather like busy insects, darted and backed and snorted among them. The streetcars went by with their sweepers whirling, flinging a white spray over everything within reach. Motors stalled. Trucks stalled. Drivers shouted, and horns complained, and nobody moved at all for minutes together. It was, I suppose, a perfect confusion. And yet one stood on the corner in the cold with one's collar turned up and felt, somehow, enormously exhilarated. There is something in a city at war with the weather that stirs the blood; one wants to cheer, as it were, for both sides.

## Draft D

The first heavy snow came at night, and the city changed its mind about itself. All day it had been the usual hard, hurried place. By the time the flakes had settled into a proper layer, it felt like somewhere else entirely, somewhere smaller and friendlier and a good deal more fun.

What surprised me most was how many people came outside. You would expect a big city to pull its curtains and wait for the morning, but instead residents poured out of their front doors with shovels and brooms and set about clearing the sidewalks themselves. Nobody seemed to mind. They called across the street to neighbours they probably never spoke to, made jokes about the weather, compared shovels and laughed at each other's technique. The servants of the grander houses stood in doorways and watched their employers at work, which added to the feeling that the usual order of things had been suspended for the evening. It looked less like a metropolis than a village gathering, the kind of scene where everybody knows everybody and a bit of shared trouble becomes a social occasion.

The parks were another matter, quieter and lovelier. Snow lay along every branch and bench, and the lamps threw soft circles of light onto the white ground. Walking past them felt like looking at a picture someone had taken great care over. Nothing moved much there. It was the calm part of the city's night, and I lingered longer than I meant to.

Then I turned onto the main avenue, and the calm was gone. Here the snow had become a problem to be solved all at once and by everyone. Plows pushed great ridges of it to the side. Carts tried to get through and mostly did not. Small tractors buzzed about with a kind of self-importance. The streetcars came along with their sweepers whirling, throwing snow off the rails in bright sprays. And in between all of this, ordinary traffic sat stalled, horns going, drivers leaning out to see what the hold-up was, though the hold-up was plainly everything.

It should have been maddening. Instead it was exhilarating. There is something about watching a whole city throw its machinery at a single problem, noisily and without much coordination, that lifts the spirits. I stood on the corner longer than was sensible, getting colder, grinning at the mess, and feeling that I had seen the place as it really was: busy, sociable and, for one night at least, entirely delighted with itself.
