Speaking of travel reminds me of the hotel bathroom.  I have in my time come into contact with a good many hotel bathrooms, and I have observed certain things about them which I do not recall having seen set down before.  The old-fashioned tub is, of course, no puzzle at all.  It stands upon its feet in the middle of the floor like an honest animal.  It has two taps, one marked "hot" and one marked "cold," and a plug upon a chain.  You turn the one a little and the other a little, and there you are.  A child could do it.

But the modern tub is built into the wall, as if it had something to hide.  It has no taps.  It has a single dial, handsomely nickelled, bearing no word of any kind.  You turn it gently to the left, and you are scalded.  You turn it gently to the right, and you are in the North Sea.  Somewhere between, I am told, there is a point of perfect comfort.  I have not found it.  I have turned that dial, early and late, with the patience of a safe-cracker, and the water has never once been what I had in mind.

Then there is the shower stall of bare concrete, a sort of small grey cell with a drain in it.  I will own that I rather enjoy it.  There is something bracing, something of the gymnasium and the barracks, in a bath of that kind.  Only, the door is heavy, and it shuts with a very final sound; and I have stood there dripping and wondered who, in the event, would think to look for me.

And there is the room without a hook.  A room without a clothes hook is--well, it is a room without a clothes hook.  Your coat goes on the chair, your trousers upon the coat, your towel upon the floor.  Just so.  Nothing more need be said of such a room, and I shall say nothing more.
