It seems to me that a more candid approach to interviewing would begin with an admission that most interviewers are reluctant to make: an hour, or even several, reveals remarkably little about how someone will actually work. We compress a relationship that may last years into a single afternoon and then speak of our conclusions with a confidence the evidence hardly supports. Acknowledging this does not make the interview useless, but it does suggest a different way of conducting it. Rather than puzzles and brain-teasers, which test mainly the ability to solve puzzles and brain-teasers, we might offer tasks that resemble the work itself; rather than treating the candidate's questions as a formality at the end, we might welcome the difficult ones, since they tell us something about what the person values and are, in any case, entirely fair.

Part of what makes interviews so unreliable is that both sides are performing. The candidate presents a version of themselves suited to the occasion, and the interviewer presents a version of the team and the organisation that is similarly polished, so that each ends up judging an act. This is understandable — both parties are nervous, and nervousness encourages performance — but it is worth remembering, if only so that we hold our judgements a little more loosely.

Perhaps the best measure of an interview, then, is not whether it produced the right decision, which we can rarely know, but how the candidate felt on leaving it. Did they feel they had been treated generously, given a fair chance to show what they could do and an honest picture of what they would be joining? If so, the interview has done something worthwhile whatever the outcome.
