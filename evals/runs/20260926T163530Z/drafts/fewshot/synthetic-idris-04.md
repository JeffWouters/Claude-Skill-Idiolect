There is an art to declining a colleague's request, and it seems to me that it is one we rarely discuss. Most of us learn it, if we learn it at all, by getting it wrong: by agreeing to things we cannot do, or by letting a request sit unanswered in the hope that it will quietly go away. A good refusal, by contrast, has a few recognisable qualities. It comes promptly, before the other person has begun to build their plans around our help. It names the real reason, which is usually some competing priority that genuinely matters more, rather than a vague reference to being busy. And where it can, it offers something smaller in place of what was asked — an hour instead of a week, perhaps, or the name of someone better placed to help.

None of this is especially difficult in principle. What makes it hard in practice is that declining requires us to admit that our time is limited, which is an uncomfortable thing to say aloud in a working culture that tends to prize availability. It is tempting to avoid the admission by saying yes and hoping for the best. But the disappointment of a refusal now is almost always smaller than the disappointment of a commitment broken later, when the other person has relied on us and has less room to find an alternative.

There is also, I think, a longer-term benefit that is easy to overlook. Someone who says no honestly and with care is someone whose yes can be believed. Their agreement carries weight precisely because it is not automatic, and colleagues learn that when they do commit, the commitment will be kept. Arguably this does more to strengthen working relationships than any amount of reluctant compliance, since it rests on a kind of trust that only candour can supply.
