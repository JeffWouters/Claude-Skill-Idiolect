Most of the advice about working across time zones treats asynchronous communication as a slower version of ordinary conversation. The recommendations follow naturally from that assumption: find the hours in which regions overlap, protect them carefully, schedule the important meetings there, and accept that everything else will simply take a little longer. There is nothing wrong with this advice as far as it goes. But it rests on a picture of the problem that seems to me incomplete, since it frames the difficulty almost entirely as one of timing.

Consider what actually happens when we write to a colleague who is asleep. In a live conversation we adjust constantly, often without noticing: a puzzled expression prompts us to rephrase, a hesitation tells us we have been too blunt, a question arrives before a misunderstanding has had time to settle. None of that feedback is available to a message sent into someone else's night. It will be read hours later, by a person who cannot ask what we meant, and who will fill the gaps with whatever interpretation their morning mood supplies. Seen this way, the challenge is less a matter of scheduling than of etiquette — and an etiquette, moreover, that most teams have never written down or even discussed.

The consequences are sharpest for those who wake to find that decisions have already been made. Over time a pattern can settle in almost unnoticed: one region deliberates and decides, while the other region's role shrinks to reviewing what has been decided in its absence. Is it any surprise that people in the later time zones begin to write differently in response? Their messages grow longer and more guarded, anticipating objections, hedging every proposal, trying to secure their position before the next handover leaves them once again with a settled outcome. This defensive writing is often read as a personality trait or a lack of confidence, when it may be a perfectly rational adaptation to the structure in which they work. If that is so, the remedy lies not in more overlap but in agreeing, explicitly, how decisions are to travel across the gap.
