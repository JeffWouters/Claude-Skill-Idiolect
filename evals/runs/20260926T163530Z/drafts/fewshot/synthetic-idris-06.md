Anyone who has spent time on a software team will have noticed how much energy can go into choosing a name. A discussion about what a service should be called may run longer, and grow warmer, than the discussion about what it should actually do — and the participants are rarely unreasonable people. We tend to explain this as a concern for accuracy, and accuracy certainly matters; a misleading name is a small trap laid for every future reader. Yet accuracy alone does not seem sufficient to account for the intensity. People do not usually argue this hard over whether a description is precise. Something else, one feels, must be at stake.

What seems to be at stake is commitment. To name a thing is to say what it is, and by the same act to say what it is not. A name draws a boundary around the work, admitting some possibilities and quietly ruling out others, and those who care about the directions being ruled out are right to sense that a decision is being made on their behalf. The argument over the word is, in many cases, an argument over the future of the thing, conducted in the only vocabulary the moment allows.

We often try to defuse such arguments by choosing something provisional, a working title that we promise to revisit once the shape of the work is clearer. This is a sensible instinct, and it rarely works as intended. Provisional names have a way of hardening almost as soon as they are spoken: they appear in repository names and dashboards and meeting invitations, they are adopted by colleagues who were never told they were temporary, and before long they have begun to shape the scope of the very thing they were meant merely to label. Consider what happens when a team calls its work a platform. The word is modest enough on the page, but it carries an invitation, and other teams will reasonably take it up, arriving with requests the original authors never intended to serve. Is the name then wrong, or has it simply become true? It is, perhaps, a little of both — which is precisely why the conversation deserves the care it so often receives, even when that care looks excessive from the outside.
