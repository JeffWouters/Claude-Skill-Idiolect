When an engineer leaves a team, the organisation usually responds with a handover: a series of meetings in which the departing person walks a colleague through the systems, the open tasks and the known problems, often supported by a document written in the final weeks. The intention is sensible enough, and I would not want to argue against it. But it is worth asking how much actually passes from one person to the other in these sessions. My impression is that it is rather less than we suppose, and for a reason that is difficult to avoid: the recipient does not yet know what to ask. They are being shown a landscape they have never had to navigate, and the questions that would draw out the useful knowledge will only occur to them months later, when something breaks and the person who could have answered is gone.

Part of the difficulty lies in the nature of what the leaver knows. Most of it is not a list of facts that could be written down but a kind of judgement, built up over years — a sense of which alerts can safely be ignored, which parts of the system are fragile, which requests from which people tend to conceal a larger problem. Written notes capture the facts reasonably well and the judgement hardly at all, which means they tend to record precisely the things that matter least. This is not a fault of the person writing them; it is simply that tacit knowledge resists being made explicit, and a few weeks of effort cannot change that.

It may be, then, that the handover matters for a different reason than the one we give it. Perhaps it is better understood as a farewell, a ritual by which a team acknowledges that someone has been part of its work and is now leaving it. Seen this way, the meetings deserve a certain respect even if they transfer little, because they mark something real: the end of a contribution, and the recognition that it was worth something. I suspect we would conduct them more gracefully if we admitted as much.
