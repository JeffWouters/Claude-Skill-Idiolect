It has been charged against us that we are given to exaggeration, and I do not see how the charge can be denied. The only question is whether it is so great a fault as our critics suppose. There is something to be said for a people who habitually think of themselves in large terms, if only that they are seldom bored with their own affairs.

The habit is an old one. The frontier settler, with a clearing of a few acres and a log house not yet finished, did not regard himself as an obscure person. He was conscious that he was making history, and he conducted himself accordingly. Ethan Allen, when he had a grievance against the Congress, did not put it in the modest form of a petition. He threatened that body in terms that would have been large for a sovereign power, [example needed: Allen's words]. It did not occur to him that the scale of his language was out of proportion to the size of his following. He was speaking for the future, which he assumed would be very large indeed.

The same spirit has survived the frontier. I once made a journey through a number of Midwestern towns, and I found that each of them was in possession of the largest something in the world. The article varied, but the claim did not. I came at last to a village which I thought too small to make any pretensions. I was mistaken. Its hotel, which was a modest building and made no particular show, was pointed out to me as the world's largest [example needed: what the hotel claimed]. I did not dispute it. One does not argue with a superlative; one only admires the courage of the man who holds it.
