There was a time when people wept over Dickens, and were not ashamed of it. They read his books aloud by the lamp, and when they came to the sad chapters the reader's voice broke and the listeners reached for their handkerchiefs. We do not defend those tears nowadays. We excuse them. We speak of them as we speak of the crinoline and the horsehair sofa, as things that belonged to a simpler age and are not to be too severely judged.

The modern critic has his explanation, and I am not disposed to quarrel with it. He tells us that Dickens is wholly obvious. His characters are all on the surface; his pathos is laid on with a trowel; his humor announces itself before it arrives. There is nothing left over for the reader to find out. He has only to open the book and receive what is handed to him.

It must be confessed that this is true, and that it takes a good deal of the credit out of our old enthusiasm. When I was fond of Sam Weller I thought it showed a certain breadth of sympathy in me. When I detested Pecksniff I took it as evidence of a fine moral discrimination. I now perceive that I was doing exactly what I was told. Dickens never trusted me to form an opinion of my own. He saw to it that I liked the people I was meant to like and hated the people I was meant to hate, and he did not leave the matter to chance. Every judgment was made for me before I came to it, and made so plainly that a child could not miss it.

There is a certain humiliation in discovering that one's virtues were supplied by the author. To love Sam Weller is no more a sign of insight than to feel warm in front of a fire. The fire does the work. And yet, now that the critics have taken away my merit, I find that I still sit by the fire.
