I was told the other day a little story--[example needed]--which amused me at the time, and which has not ceased to trouble me since. It is not, I think, a story about one man or one town. It is a story about who now has the say in American education; and that, frankly, is not a laughing matter.

Now it has been pointed out often enough, by people who count heads for a living, that the old settler families are no longer the majority among us. The census says so; [example needed]. The comic papers say so too, and the comic papers are seldom wrong about such things, for they must be understood or starve. The jokes have changed their butt. It is always the descendant of the Pilgrims (it is always he!) who is now the quaint survival, the figure of fun.

I do not mind the jokes. What I mind is what has gone with the old majority. Our grandfathers revered schooling. They were not all learned--not by any means--but they knew that learning was a thing to be looked up to, and they paid for it, and went without for it. Their grandsons, it seems, have handed it over to lawmakers who never had much of it themselves. One is ready to grant that a legislator need not read Greek. But one may ask that he should not stand in the way of standards; and he does stand in the way, because standards would shame him. No man votes willingly for the yardstick that proves him short.

Meanwhile the newly rich families rise, as they have always risen, without polish; that is no crime, and polish may come in a generation or two. But the colleges and libraries and museums, which are supposed to guard learning, must go to these families for money. And he who pays the piper, I have been given to understand, is apt to call the tune. So the little story is not, after all, so very little. It is the whole matter in small.
