Speaking of death reminds me of the undertaking business.  I have recently been struck by a curious fact concerning it which I have not seen commented upon before, namely, where in the great city it is to be found.  You may walk up the most fashionable of our avenues from one end to the other, past the great houses and the fine shops and the clubs, and you will not, so far as I have been able to observe, see an undertaker.  Or, if there is one, he is so discreet about it that he might as well not be there.  One is driven to the conclusion that in these parts people do not die.  They are too well off.  It would not, I suppose, be quite the thing.

I will make one exception.  There is uptown a funeral establishment of the most lavish kind.  It is filled with objects of art--vases, tapestries, bronzes, great carved chairs, pictures in heavy frames.  Everything is so dressed up that you wouldn't think anybody had died at all.  It looks like a museum, or a dealer's gallery, or the house of a rich man who collects.  And it seems, somehow, in the wrong place, like a gentleman in evening clothes who has got off at the wrong station.

For the undertaker, as a rule, lives among the poor.  He keeps a little shop, one room wide, in some street of tenements, between the grocer and the tailor.  There is a palm in the window, perhaps, and a card with his name in black letters, and inside a desk and a couple of chairs.  And about his door, when there is occasion, the neighbours gather.  They stand on the sidewalk in their shawls and aprons and shirt-sleeves, and talk.  They are in no hurry.  They talk of the departed, and of the weather, and of the price of things, and of the last funeral, and of the one before that.  There is no sense of the wrong place here.  Death, in these streets, is a neighbour too, and is received accordingly, at an easy pace.
