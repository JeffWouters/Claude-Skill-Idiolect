My dear sir, I have read your novel with the attention which a man in my line of business gives to any new enterprise, and I should like to offer a word of counsel from one who has paid rather dearly for his education. I deal in land. When I began, I supposed that land had a value of its own, as a man has a character. I soon learned otherwise. A lot is worth precisely what somebody wants it for, and not a penny more. Demand gives it value, and demand is a fickle tenant. I learned, too, that a building must suit its site. I once put up a handsome structure on ground where nobody cared to go, and I still have the privilege of looking at it. It was well built; the plumbing was excellent; the only thing wrong with it was that it stood where it stood. That lesson cost me more than I care to tell.

Now it seems to me that you have made the same mistake on a larger scale. You have put a great deal of costly work upon poor ground. Your characters are true to life; I do not dispute it. I have met every one of them, and I have taken some pains to avoid meeting them again. That they are true to life is no recommendation. So is the weather.

Consider the situation of your reader. He is a busy man with a limited amount of time, and he must choose his acquaintances with some care. When he opens a book he is not looking for more of the people he already knows; he has enough of them at home and at the office. He wants to meet somebody worth the trouble. If it is mere reality he craves, the newspapers deliver it every morning at a moderate price, and the historians will furnish it in bulk for those who like it seasoned with dates. You cannot hope to compete with them on their own ground.

My advice, therefore, is simply the advice of my own trade. Before you build again, look to your site, and ask whether anyone will want to come there.
