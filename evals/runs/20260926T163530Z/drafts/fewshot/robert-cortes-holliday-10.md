Speaking of streets, there is nothing, to my mind, quite like the streets that run along a harbour.  I do not know precisely why this should be so.  They are not, as a rule, handsome streets.  They are not clean.  Nobody who has any business elsewhere goes near them.  And yet I have never been in a port city without being drawn down to the water, as surely as if somebody had tied a string to my coat.

San Francisco's waterfront is a case in point.  Along the bay, one after another, stand the piers of the shipping companies, each with its name painted up large, as though it were afraid you might mistake it for some other company.  If you have walked along the Hudson shore of Manhattan you will know the sort of thing I mean; there, too, the steamship buildings stand in a row facing the river, and there, too, they have a great air of importance about them.  The two waterfronts are a continent apart, but I could not honestly tell you, with my eyes shut, which of them I was on.

Then there are the sailors' shops.  I have observed a certain outstanding fact about sailors' shops which I have not seen commented upon before.  They are all the same shop.  Whether you meet one in San Francisco or in New York or in [example needed], it is the same window, with the same things in it, and very likely the same man behind the counter.  Just so.  The sailor, it seems, goes round the world in order to find the store he left behind him.

And what is in the window?  Showy things, chiefly.  Nothing in a sailors' shop is modest.  Above all there are the plates: thick, heavy plates, painted over in the brightest colours, with a bit of ribbon through a hole at the top so that you may hang them upon the wall.  Why, you wouldn't think anybody would eat off them.  Nobody does.  They are not for eating; they are for having.
