Our acquaintance with the barbershop was--at first--merely literary.  We had read of it, as a great many people have, in the pages of a novel, and it had not occurred to us that it was a real place, with real chairs in it, until somebody told us the street.  So we went there one morning to be shaved.

The proprietor received us himself.  He was a Frenchman, and a very elegant Frenchman.  He had a beard--we find it difficult to denote that beard.  It was not a large beard merely; it was a beard of character, of great distinction, full and silken and spread upon his chest like an order of merit.  His hair was of a piece with it, abundant, lustrous, swept back from his brow in a manner not common among barbers.  "Good morning," he said, and bowed; and he said it in such a way, with such a grave and gracious air, that we felt for a moment that we had come to present our credentials and were being received at an embassy.  We were quite abashed.  It seemed hardly proper to ask a diplomat for a shave.

Presently, when he had learned that we knew the novel, his manner warmed.  He went to a cupboard and brought out, very carefully, a little stack of magazines.  These were the numbers, he explained, in which the story had first appeared, in parts.  He turned the pages for us himself.  Here was his shop.  Here, in so many words, was he.  He was very proud of them, and we could see that they were not often allowed out of the cupboard.

Then he told us the history of the place.  It had been a barbershop for [example needed] years.  And before that, he said, before ever it was a barbershop, a writer had lived in those very rooms--[example needed].  He said it as one names an ancestor.  We looked about us at the chairs and the mirrors and the bottles with a new respect.  We felt that we had been shaved, so to speak, in good society.
