It is not pleasant to say that a thing one loves is dying, and I should be glad to be told I am wrong. But the evidence accumulates, and I do not see how any one who looks at it honestly can escape the conclusion that the serious standards of culture are vanishing from American life. Take the schools first, since it is in the schools that a nation shows what it really values. Everywhere the older studies are giving way to the modern and the vocational; the boy is to be fitted for a trade, and the girl for an office, and whatever cannot be sold across a counter is put down as a luxury. I have before me a reading list, issued with every appearance of official gravity in Massachusetts--of all States!--in which popular fiction of the day is counted as literature [example needed: titles from the Massachusetts list]. I do not say the books are wicked. I say only that no one who framed such a list can have had any notion of what literature is.

Nor is it only the schools. Art, philosophy and fiction have one after another abandoned the ideals which gave them their dignity, and have not found anything to put in their place. The painter no longer tries to paint what is beautiful; the philosopher has ceased to ask what is good; the novelist is content to be amusing, and is not always that. And the public does not complain. Children, left to themselves, reach for the lightest thing on the shelf, which is natural enough in children. What is not natural is that their parents should do the same, and should be rather proud of it. [example needed: an instance of parents preferring light reading]

The truth is that real cultivation is a slow and thankless business. It is not got in a season, nor in a lifetime; it takes generations of patient effort, most of it unrewarded. It brings in no money. It wins no flattery--quite the contrary, for the cultivated man is suspected of thinking himself better than his neighbours, and his neighbours make him pay for it. Small wonder that people who have to choose between culture and comfort choose comfort. But I confess I have less patience with a newer habit, which is the boasting of humble origins. A man tells you, with the air of one producing a certificate of virtue, how little his people had [example needed: an instance of boasting of humble origins]. It is meant to be modest. It is not modest in the least; it is snobbery turned inside out, and quite as tiresome as the other kind. There is no merit in having come from nothing, as there is no merit in having come from something; the merit lies in what one does with the inheritance. And if the inheritance is to be thrown away--if every family is to begin again from the beginning, and be proud of it--then the standards will not merely vanish. They will not be missed.
