Every newspaper man I have ever known has told me, at one time or another, what he would accomplish if only he had a little leisure to himself.  He would write the book.  He has carried it about in his head for years; it is all there, he explains, excepting the mere mechanical business of putting it down.  What he requires is a month, or a summer, away from the city desk and the telephone and the managing editor and the continual demand for another paragraph before the paper goes to press.  Well, now and again somebody presents him with the month.  Nothing whatever comes of it.  He sits in an agreeable room with a considerable quantity of clean paper, and he looks out of the window, and he sharpens his pencils, and he goes out for a walk, and the book does not arrive.  The thing he needed, it appears, was not freedom at all, but somebody standing over him.

The instruments of the trade are another curious matter.  One author will tell you that he cannot think except with a pen in his hand; the ink, he believes, is somehow connected with his intelligence.  Another has never produced a line that was not pounded out upon a typewriter.  A third walks up and down the room and dictates, which strikes me as rather like proposing marriage through an interpreter.  And there are those who employ everything by turns: the pen for the first draft, the pencil for the corrections, the machine for the fair copy.  I don't know that it makes a particle of difference to the reader.

Then there is the question of where.  Some writers, I am informed, must live in the very middle of whatever it is they are describing, and cannot compose a paragraph about a place they have not breathed and eaten and slept in.  Others, quite as distinguished, can't see their own town at all until they have put a considerable distance between themselves and it.
