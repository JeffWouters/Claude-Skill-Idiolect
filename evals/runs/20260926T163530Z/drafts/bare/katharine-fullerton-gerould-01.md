I was told the other day a small story which I cannot get out of my head: [example needed: the anecdote about declining learning]. It is a trifle, and I should not repeat it if it did not seem to me to explain a good deal, for the story is not really about one family, or one town, but about who is now in charge of American education. We need not go to the census for the answer, though the census will give it to any one who asks [number needed: population figures on the older settler stock]. The comic papers have known it longer. Their jokes about the old families, the thin Puritan households with their dwindling nurseries and their faded portraits, are not jokes at all; they are the plain news that the stock which built the schools has ceased to be the majority, and is fast ceasing to be even a large minority.

Now that older generation, whatever its faults, revered schooling. It went without things in order to send its sons to college, and it thought a man who could not spell had something to be ashamed of. To-day the schools are governed, very largely, by men who were poorly taught themselves. I do not say this to sneer. But it is natural enough that a legislator who never learned his grammar should not care to vote for standards which would show him up before the whole State; no one likes to pass a law that shames him. So the standards go, quietly, one by one. Meanwhile a new class has come into money--a great deal of money, and very suddenly--without having had time to come into manners as well. I have nothing against new money; all money was new once. The trouble is that the colleges and libraries and museums, which ought to be guarding learning against every one who would cheapen it, must go to these very families, hat in hand, for their endowments, and they can hardly bite the hand that feeds them. Perhaps that is the real meaning of my little story. I am afraid it is.
