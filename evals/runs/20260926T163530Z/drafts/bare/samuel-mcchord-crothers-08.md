It has become the fashion to speak of the leading minds of our time as though they had fallen silent in despair. The old faiths are gone, we are told, and the new ones are not yet born, and between the two the thinker sits with folded hands and broods. Matthew Arnold has put this mood into a poem, and he has done it so well that many readers have taken the poem for a description of the facts. [example needed: a line or image from the poem] The modern mind, as he presents it, is a melancholy spectator. It watches the procession of events and is too sorrowful to interfere.

I do not believe it. The mood is genuine enough, but it is temporary, and it is not the characteristic mood of the people who count. A man who really thinks cannot brood for very long. Sooner or later his thinking gets the better of his melancholy, and he begins, almost without meaning to, to shape the thing that is coming next. The despair is an interval between two kinds of work.

Arnold himself knew this better than his poem admits. In the verses he wrote about his father he drew quite another picture, and to my mind a truer one. There the leader is not a spectator at all. He is a man in whom intellect and moral force are united, who sees clearly and acts upon what he sees, and who carries others along with him because he does not stop to wonder whether the journey is worth while. That is the kind of leadership the world has always had when it needed it; the brooding figure is only what we see in the pause.

And if the leaders themselves lose heart, the situation is still not so desperate as it appears. They are replaced. An Irish officer after the Boyne understood this perfectly and said as much. [example needed: the officer's words] His point was not that leaders never fail, but that their failure is not the end of the enterprise. When one company of captains grows discouraged, another is always waiting, impatient to take its turn.
