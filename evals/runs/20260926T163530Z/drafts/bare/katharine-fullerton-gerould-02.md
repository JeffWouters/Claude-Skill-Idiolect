Is it ever right to lie? The moralists have always found the question easy, and the rest of us have always found it hard. I do not think we are merely weaker than the moralists; I think we are more honest about what we actually do. For the plain fact is that the whole machinery of polite life runs upon small departures from the literal truth, and runs, on the whole, smoothly. When the maid tells a caller that her mistress is not at home, nobody is deceived, and nobody is meant to be. It is a convention, as well understood on the doorstep as in the drawing-room. Yet if we are to be strict, it is not true. And silence, which the strict would have us take refuge in, is no safer. One may deceive quite as thoroughly by holding one's tongue as by wagging it; any one who has ever let a false impression stand, because it was convenient, knows that.

Nor is it only the drawing-room that bends. The courts themselves, which exist to find out the truth, do not expect it of every one. A man on trial is not required to tell against himself, and the law does not look very hard at him if he tells something less than the whole story. A wife is not asked to condemn her husband. We allow, in short, that there are loyalties which may outweigh the oath--or at any rate that it would be cruel, and useless, to demand the contrary.

But I would go further than this. There are times when deceit is not merely permitted but required of us. If a burglar asks me where the silver is kept, I shall not tell him, and I shall feel no scruple whatever in sending him to the wrong cupboard. If a hostess has given me a dinner I did not enjoy, I shall thank her warmly; to do otherwise would be a brutality, not a virtue. I dare say the moralists would call this weakness. It seems to me a recognition that truth is not the only good in the world, and that a man who sacrifices every other good to it--kindness, safety, the peace of the household--has not so much served the truth as made an idol of it. Honesty is complicated because life is complicated. The difficulty is not in knowing that we must sometimes lie; it is in knowing when.
