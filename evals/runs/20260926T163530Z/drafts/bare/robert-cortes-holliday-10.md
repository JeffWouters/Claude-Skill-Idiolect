I have always had a weakness for the streets that run along a harbour, and I am not certain that any of them pleases me more than the waterfront of San Francisco.  There the piers of the shipping companies stand in a long row against the bay, each with its name painted large across the front, each looking out upon the water with a kind of dignified expectancy.  A New Yorker, strolling there, is reminded at once of the steamship buildings along the Hudson shore of Manhattan, and he feels, I suppose, rather more at home than he had anticipated.  The resemblance is not an accident.  Ships are ships, and the business of receiving them seems to produce the same architecture wherever it is conducted.

The same is true, and even more remarkably, of the sailors' shops.  You may walk into one of these establishments in any port city you please, and you will find it precisely like the one you visited in the last.  I don't pretend to understand why this should be; it is simply one of those things.  The window, the counter, the proprietor, the general atmosphere of an old sea chest recently opened: all are identical.  [example needed: another port city visited]

What particularly distinguishes these places is their taste in merchandise.  Nothing modest is admitted.  The sailor, it appears, when he goes ashore with money in his pocket, desires something showy, something that will be recognised as a present from a considerable distance.  And the shops have accommodated him.  Above everything else they offer plates: thick, heavy, decorative plates, painted in the most enthusiastic colours, with a hole bored through the rim and a ribbon passed through the hole so that the thing may be hung upon a wall.  They are not intended to be eaten from.  I doubt whether anybody has ever eaten from one.  They hang in rows about the walls of the shop, glowing, and they are, in their way, entirely irresistible.
