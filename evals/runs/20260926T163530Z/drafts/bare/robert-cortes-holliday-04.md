I had long wished to see the barbershop which figures so prominently in [title needed: the novel], and one afternoon I went.  I had expected, I suppose, an ordinary shop with a little literary dust upon it.  I was not prepared for the proprietor.  He was a Frenchman, and a very elegant one, and he possessed a beard and a head of hair such as I have seldom seen upon any living man; they were not so much grown as cultivated.  He came forward to receive me with so grand a courtesy, with such an inclination of the head and such a gracious movement of the hand, that I felt for a moment I had strayed into an embassy.  One would have said that he was about to present his credentials.  I had merely come, as it were, to look.

He understood at once, however, what I was about, and he was delighted.  From somewhere at the back of the shop he produced, with evident pride, the numbers of the magazine in which the novel had first appeared as a serial.  He handled them tenderly; he turned the pages for me himself, and he pointed out the passages in which his establishment was described.  It was plain that he regarded these magazines as something between a family Bible and a patent of nobility.

Then, settling himself comfortably, he gave me the history of the place.  He told it well, and at some length, as a man tells a story he has told many times and has come to enjoy.  The shop, it appeared, had not always been a shop.  Before his time, a writer had lived in these very rooms, [example needed: who the writer was], and had done his work here, perhaps in the corner where the chair now stands.  My host seemed to feel that this earlier tenant had, in a manner, prepared the ground for the novel.  I did not dispute it.  In the presence of that beard one does not dispute anything.
