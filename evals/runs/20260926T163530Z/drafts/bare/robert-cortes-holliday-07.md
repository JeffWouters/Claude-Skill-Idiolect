To the New Yorker who goes over to Philadelphia for the day the first agreeable thing about the place is the journey there.  I do not say this unkindly.  I mean that a man who is accustomed, morning and evening, to being packed into the subway with several hundred of his fellow citizens, all of them apparently standing upon his feet, finds a seat by a window in a railway coach a positively luxurious experience.  He may read his paper.  He may look at the landscape, or not, as he chooses.  Nobody pushes him.  He arrives rested, which is more than he is accustomed to do at his office, and in a frame of mind to notice things.

And there are, I found, a good many things to notice.  There is, for instance, the manner in which rooms are advertised. Where I come from, one thinks of an undertaker as keeping a shop, more or less like other people; in Philadelphia, it appears, he is quite likely to keep a house, a residence with curtains at the windows, looking for all the world as though a family lived there--which, in a manner of speaking, perhaps one does.  The fare system on the cars is a study in itself.  [example needed: how the fares work]  And I have never, anywhere, seen so many different kinds of street lamps; it is as if every generation of the city's history had put up its own and none had ever been taken down.

One is impressed, too, by the extraordinary number of financial firms.  Banks, trust companies, brokers, investment houses: they seem to occupy every other doorway in the older part of town, and to have occupied it for a very long time.  The architecture is, to put it mildly, mixed.  Buildings of every period and every taste stand shoulder to shoulder, rather like guests at a large reception who have not been introduced.

But what I liked best were the porches.
