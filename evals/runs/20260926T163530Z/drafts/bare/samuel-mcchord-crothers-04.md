It has often been remarked that Americans are fond of exaggeration, and I do not see much use in denying it. We like large statements. We like to be told that a thing is the biggest of its kind, and if it is not the biggest we are inclined to think there has been some mistake in the measuring.

The habit is an old one. The settlers on the frontier did not regard themselves as obscure persons engaged in clearing land. They thought of themselves as historic figures, and they talked accordingly. When Ethan Allen had a grievance against Congress, he did not petition it in the language of a humble subject; he threatened it in terms that would have been appropriate to a sovereign power addressing another. [example needed: the substance of Allen's threat] There was nothing modest about the frontier imagination. A man who had built a cabin in the wilderness felt that he had founded a state.

The same spirit survives in more peaceful forms. I once travelled through a number of Midwestern towns and found that each of them was able to boast of possessing the largest something in the world. The object of pride varied from town to town; the superlative did not. In one modest village the claim was made on behalf of the hotel, which did not appear to me to be very large at all. But the proprietor believed in it, and so, I think, did the villagers, and it would have been a churlish thing to dispute the matter.

When we come to estimate the national character, however, it is perhaps wiser not to begin with our own claims. We are too much interested in them. A better starting point is to be found in the complaints of foreigners, who have no reason to flatter us. Kipling wrote a poem about the American which I have always thought instructive in this respect.
