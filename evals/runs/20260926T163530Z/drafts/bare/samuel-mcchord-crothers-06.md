It is a curious circumstance that a forest so extensive and so ancient should have only one story to tell, and that the story should be a killing which nobody has ever solved. Everything else that happened in the New Forest seems to have been forgotten. What is remembered is that King William Rufus went out hunting there and did not come back alive, and that no one has ever been able to say with certainty who was responsible. The woods have kept the secret. They have kept it so long that the secret has become, in a manner, the whole of their history.

We did not go looking for the spot. There was a road that led in that direction, but it was crowded with automobiles, each carrying its load of travellers toward the place where history had happened, and we had no particular desire to join the procession. So we turned aside and wandered among the old trees. They were venerable and unconcerned, and they did not seem to care in the least who had been killed beneath them. I was grateful that the Crown still owned them. There is something to be said for a landlord who is too dignified to improve his property; the forest has remained wild because nobody with authority has ever considered it worth while to make it otherwise.

We had our picnic in the shade. [example needed: a detail of the picnic] While we sat there we noticed some women gathering firewood, as they were entitled to do by old right. They did not look like people who were taking anything. They looked like people who were collecting what was theirs.

It occurred to me that this was a better kind of history than the other. The king was killed once, and the question has been debated ever since. But the women had been exercising their privilege quietly all along, and nobody debated that at all.
