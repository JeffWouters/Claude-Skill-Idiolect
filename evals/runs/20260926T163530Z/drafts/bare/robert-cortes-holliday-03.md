It is a curious thing, and one which I do not remember to have seen remarked upon, that in the wealthier quarters of the city there are practically no undertakers.  You may walk the length of the fashionable avenues, past the great houses and the costly shops, and you will look in vain for the modest sign of the funeral director.  One is almost driven to conclude that in these neighbourhoods nobody ever dies.  Perhaps the rich have arranged that too.  Or perhaps, when the inevitable does occur, the matter is attended to somewhere else, discreetly, as one sends out the laundry.

There is, to be sure, one conspicuous exception.  Uptown there stands a funeral establishment of the most lavish description, and I confess that I have never passed it without a certain astonishment.  It is filled with art objects, [example needed: the kinds of objects], collected, one would say, with more zeal than discrimination.  It has less the air of a place of mourning than of an antique dealer's, or of a small museum which has somehow lost its way.  It seems, in short, entirely out of place; and I have wondered whether the bereaved are expected to grieve or to bid.

The undertaker, for the most part, is a humbler person, and he lives among humbler people.  His shop is small, and it is found in the poorer districts, among the other small shops of the street.  Here he is very much a part of the neighbourhood.  The neighbours gather before his door, and they talk, and they go on talking, about whatever neighbours talk about.  Nobody is in any hurry.  There is none of the sombre hush which the word undertaker calls to mind; there is only the ordinary, comfortable conversation of people who know one another well.  Death, in these streets, is not a stranger to be kept out of sight; it is a neighbour, and it is taken, like everything else, at an easy pace.
