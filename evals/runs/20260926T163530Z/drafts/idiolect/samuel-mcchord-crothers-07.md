There was a time when people wept over Dickens and were not ashamed of it. We do not defend them now. We excuse them. They lived, we say, in a simpler generation, when a sorrowful chapter was an event in the household and nobody had yet been instructed to distrust a lump in the throat, and we extend to their tears the same indulgence we extend to their furniture. We do not share the taste, but we are willing to believe it was sincere.

The modern critic has gone further, and he is right to. He observes that everything in Dickens lies on the surface. The good people are good and the wicked people are wicked, and the author takes particular care that we shall not mistake one for the other. The pathos is laid on where pathos is wanted and the laughter where laughter is wanted, and there is nothing left over for the reader to discover by himself. A character in Dickens has no secrets from us, for Dickens has no secrets from us. There is nothing to interpret.

This is a just criticism, and it takes away rather a good deal of the credit we used to give ourselves. It is no proof of insight to be fond of Sam Weller. Everybody is fond of Sam Weller; that is what he is for. Nor does it show any remarkable moral penetration to detest Pecksniff, since the author has done the detesting beforehand, thoroughly and with evident enjoyment, and merely invites us to join in. The judgment is handed to us ready made, like a parcel from the shop. All we have to do is carry it home.

But perhaps the pleasure need not be taken away along with the credit. We might still laugh at Sam Weller and still enjoy disliking Pecksniff, so long as we do not mistake the enjoyment for discernment. The reader of Dickens is a guest who has been conducted through every room of the house by an attentive host, and he should not afterwards boast of having explored it.
