It has often been observed that Americans are fond of exaggeration. The charge is true, and I see no reason to deny it. But it is worth asking where the habit came from, and whether it is quite so bad as our critics suppose.

The frontier settler did not think of himself as a private person. He thought of himself as a historic figure, and he was not altogether wrong. When a man is laying the foundations of a commonwealth in the wilderness, with nothing between him and the future but his own axe and his own opinion of himself, it is natural that he should speak as if posterity were listening. Ethan Allen, when he had a grievance against Congress, did not write a modest letter of complaint. He threatened that body in sweeping terms, as one power addressing another. The habit has survived the frontier. I once made a trip through a number of Midwestern towns, and I found that each of them was prepared to show me the largest something in the world. The objects varied a good deal; the spirit did not. In one modest village the claim was made on behalf of the hotel, which to my untutored eye seemed a very ordinary hotel indeed, but I was assured that in some particular it surpassed every other on the globe, and I had no evidence to the contrary. It is a pleasant thing to live in a place that leads the world, even if the lead is a narrow one.

How, then, are we to judge the national character? I would not begin with what we say of ourselves, for that is apt to be generous. I would rather begin with the complaints of foreigners. They are not always fair, but they are seldom without foundation, and a wise man will take his enemies' view of him as the starting point for self-knowledge.
