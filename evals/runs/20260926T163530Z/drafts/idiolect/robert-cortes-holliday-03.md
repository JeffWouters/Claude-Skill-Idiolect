It is a curious circumstance, and I do not know that anybody has remarked upon it, that you may walk the length of the handsomest avenues in the city and scarcely pass an undertaker. There are jewellers there, and florists, and dealers in expensive carpets. There are establishments which sell nothing whatever, so far as one can determine, except an atmosphere. Of undertakers, however, there are practically none. One is driven to conclude that the wealthy, in this city, do not die. Or, if they do, they arrange it somewhere else, discreetly, as they arrange most things. It would be entirely consistent with their habits.

There is, to be sure, one exception, and a very magnificent one. Uptown there stands a funeral establishment of the most lavish description, crowded with objects of art. It contains bronzes, tapestries, carved chests and enormous vases on pedestals. These are the furnishings you would expect of a museum, or of the drawing-room of a collector. The interior is hushed and opulent; and yet there is something about the place that does not quite belong. It is as if a palace had been deposited by mistake to perform the business of a shop. One feels that a person would need to be exceedingly well connected to be buried from it.

The undertaker proper, the neighbourhood undertaker, is quite another affair. You discover him in the poorer districts, in a small shop among other small shops, with a modest sign over the door. His premises are generally open. People drop in; they do not appear to regard it as a melancholy place at all. The neighbours gather on the pavement in front of it and converse, of the weather and of prices and of who is ailing and who is recovering, at the unhurried pace of people who have lived a long time on the same street. Death, here, is a neighbour too. It is not concealed. It keeps a shop on the corner, and passes the time of day.
