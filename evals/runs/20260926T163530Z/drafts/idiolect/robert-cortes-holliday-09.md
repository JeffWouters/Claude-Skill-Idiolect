Every newspaper man of my acquaintance longs for leisure.  He will tell you so at any hour of the day or night, and particularly at the hour when the city editor sends him out again on some errand of no consequence.  If only he had a little time to himself, he says, he would produce the thing he has always intended to produce.  Well, give it to him.  Give him a month, a summer, an entire year of unoccupied days.  What happens?  Nothing whatever happens.  He sits down before a clean sheet of paper, and the clean sheet of paper, so to speak, looks back at him; and presently he gets up and goes out for a walk.  It seems that a considerable number of men do their best work only when they have not the time to do it.  [example needed: a real instance of a journalist given free time who produced nothing]

Then there is the question of implements.  One author will accept nothing that does not come from his own hand, slowly, in ink.  Another cannot think at all except at the typewriter, and he pounds away at the machine as though it owed him money.  A third walks up and down the room and dictates to a stenographer.  And I suppose there are some (I suspect they are the majority) who employ a little of everything: a pen for the beginning, a pencil for the middle, and the machine when the manuscript has got to be finished and sent off.  Which method is the correct one?  I don't know that anybody knows.

Place is altogether another matter.  Certain writers, it appears, must live in the very thick of whatever they describe.  They must hear the conversation in the street and smell the cooking in the hallways, or they cannot write a line about any of it.  Others do precisely the reverse.  They travel as far away as they can manage, to some foreign country perhaps, and there, with the whole width of the ocean between themselves and home, they write about home better than they ever did while they lived in it.  It is a curious business, literature.
