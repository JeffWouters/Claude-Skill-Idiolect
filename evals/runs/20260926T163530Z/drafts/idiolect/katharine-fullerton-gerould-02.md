Is it ever right to lie? The moralists have always said no, and most of us, I think, would say no too if we were asked in church. But we are not often asked in church; we are asked at the front door, in the witness-box, at the dinner-table, and there the answer is not nearly so simple. One has only to watch a well-ordered household for a morning to see how much of its peace rests upon polite untruth. The servant who tells a caller that her mistress is not at home, when her mistress is upstairs all the while, deceives nobody and offends nobody. It is a convention, and every one understands it. Yet it is not, in the strictest sense, the truth. Nor is silence always innocent. A man may never utter a false word and still leave his hearers believing what is false--which is, I suppose, the most respectable way of lying there is.

The law, which ought to know better than any of us, is curiously tolerant here. A defendant is not required to condemn himself out of his own mouth; he may deny what he did, and no one thinks the worse of the court for letting him. A wife is not asked to testify against her husband. The courts do not, of course, call this lying. They call it something longer. But the principle is plain enough: there are loyalties which the law itself sets above the bare recital of fact.

And then there are the cases where deceit is not merely tolerated but required. If a burglar asks me where the silver is kept, I am under no obligation whatever to tell him; I am under some obligation, rather, to send him to the wrong cupboard. If a hostess has done her best and her dinner is a failure, the guest who tells her so has not served truth--he has only served himself. Some one will object that this is the thin end of the wedge. Granted; but the wedge was in long before we came. The point is not whether we shall ever lie, since we plainly shall, but whether we know why we are doing it. Honesty is not a rule. It is a discipline, and a harder one than the moralists allow.
