What can an interviewer honestly expect to learn about a person in an hour? Very little, I suspect, and it may be that the most useful thing an interview process can do is admit as much. We tend to treat the conversation as a reliable instrument, capable of revealing how someone will think and behave over years of working together, when it is at best a brief and heavily staged glimpse. A more candid approach would begin from that admission and design around it: fewer brain-teasers, which measure mainly a talent for brain-teasers, and more realistic tasks that resemble the work the person would actually be asked to do.

There is also the matter of performance, which runs in both directions. The candidate presents a confident, well-rehearsed version of themselves; the interviewer presents a team that is calm, organised and pleasant to work in. Each side is therefore judging an act, and each knows, at some level, that the other is doing the same. This is understandable rather than cynical, since both parties are nervous and both want the meeting to go well. It seems to me that the sensible response is to invite the candidate to ask difficult questions and to welcome them when they do, rather than treating a sharp question as a sign of awkwardness. You might learn more from how someone probes your weaknesses than from how they describe their own.

Perhaps the fairest test of an interview is how the candidate feels afterwards, whatever the result. The best ones leave people with the sense that they were treated generously, that their time was respected and their answers taken seriously, even when the answer they receive at the end is no.
