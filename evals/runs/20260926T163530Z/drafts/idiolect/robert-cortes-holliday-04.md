We had read the novel, of course, and it was in something of the spirit of a pilgrim that we went to look at the barbershop which figures in it. One does not ordinarily approach a barbershop with reverence. This one, however, had been in literature, and that makes a difference. We opened the door rather timidly.

The proprietor came forward to receive us. He was a Frenchman, and a very elegant one; and he possessed a beard, and a head of hair, of a magnificence I shall not attempt to describe, for I should not be believed. He bowed. He inquired, in the most courteous terms, how he might be of service. His manner was so grand, so finished, so altogether distinguished, that for a moment we had the impression of having wandered by mistake into an embassy, and of being received by the ambassador himself. We explained, somewhat apologetically, that we had come on account of the book.

Nothing could have pleased him more. He retired to a back room and returned carrying, with great care, a pile of magazines, the very issues in which the novel had first appeared as a serial. These he laid before us one by one, turning the pages to the passages concerning his establishment, and watching our faces while we read. He was, it was evident, exceedingly proud of them, as another man might be proud of a decoration. Then, having put the magazines away again, he gave us the history of the shop from its beginning: who had kept it, and when, and what alterations had been made. He mentioned, among other things, that the premises had at one time been occupied by a writer, [name needed], who had lived there before ever it became a barbershop. He spoke of this as one speaks of an ancestor. It seemed to us that the place had a literary destiny, so to speak, and that it knew it.
