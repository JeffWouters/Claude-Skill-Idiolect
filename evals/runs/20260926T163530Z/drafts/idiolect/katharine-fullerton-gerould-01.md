It is a trifle, I grant; but trifles are often the most honest witnesses we have. One need not be a statistician to see what lies behind it. The population figures tell us that the old settler families no longer make up the majority of the country [number needed: their present share], and the comic papers, which are perhaps rather wiser than the census, have been telling us the same thing for years [example needed: the popular joke]. The people who once shaped the nation's mind do not shape it any more. This is not a lament. It is simply a fact, and we had better look at it plainly.

Our grandfathers revered schooling--foolishly, now and then, I fancy, but always sincerely. They thought a learned man a person to be looked up to, and they did not much mind if he was poor. To-day the schools are governed by men who were poorly taught themselves, and who sit in legislatures deciding, with a confidence that might be touching if it were not so costly, what other people's children shall learn and how little of it they shall be expected to remember. It is not surprising that they resist standards. A standard is a measuring-rod, after all; and no one cares to be measured and found short, least of all in public. The newly rich, meanwhile, rise without polish (and do not appear to miss it!), while the colleges and libraries that guard learning must go to them, hat in hand, for money. But he who pays the piper has always called the tune. Learning has not been abolished in America; it has merely been put in the keeping of people who do not know what it is worth.
