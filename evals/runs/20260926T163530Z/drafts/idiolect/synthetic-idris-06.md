Why should a name provoke more argument than the thing it names? Anyone who has worked on a software team will have seen a discussion about what to call a service or a module run on far longer than the discussion about what it should actually do, and the usual explanation, that names matter because they must be accurate, never seems quite sufficient to account for the heat involved. Accuracy is a real concern, certainly, but people do not generally raise their voices over precision alone. It may be that the intensity comes from somewhere else — from a sense, rarely articulated, that choosing a name is a commitment about what the thing is and, by implication, about everything it will not be allowed to become.

A name, in other words, is not merely a label attached after the fact. It draws a boundary around the work, and whoever proposes one is also proposing, perhaps without realising it, which future directions will seem natural and which will seem like a departure. The people who object to a name are frequently objecting to that boundary; they can see a direction they care about being quietly excluded, and the argument over a word becomes the only available place to defend it.

There is also the matter of how quickly provisional names harden. We tend to reassure ourselves that a name is only temporary, something to be revisited once the design has settled, and yet within a few weeks it has appeared in documentation, in dashboards, in the way colleagues describe the work to one another, and changing it would require more effort than anyone is prepared to spend. By then the name has begun to shape the scope rather than describe it. Calling something a platform, for instance, is arguably an invitation: other teams hear the word and reasonably conclude that they may bring their requests, and a piece of work that began with a narrow purpose finds itself answering to people it was never designed to serve.

It seems to me that the lengthy debates over naming are therefore less wasteful than they appear, since they are often the first honest conversation a team has about what it is building and for whom.
