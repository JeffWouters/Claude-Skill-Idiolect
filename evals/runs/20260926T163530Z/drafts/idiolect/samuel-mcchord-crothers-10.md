My conservative friend is troubled about the country, and I should like to state his complaint as fairly as I can, for it deserves a better hearing than it usually gets. As he sees it, the nation has lately enjoyed a prosperity such as few peoples have ever known. One might suppose that citizens so favored would be content. They are not. Instead of going quietly about their business, they have taken to following agitators, experimenting with reforms, and generally hampering the very captains of industry to whom, in his opinion, the prosperity was chiefly due.

Nor is this all. The courts, which used to be a refuge, have become uncertain. Professors who ought to be teaching their classes are busily revising the tariff, and officials whose zeal outruns their discretion are poking into affairs that were once considered private. New issues arise faster than anyone can understand them. The old parties, which a man could count upon as he counted upon the multiplication table, no longer mean what they meant; a voter may find himself in agreement with his lifelong opponents and at odds with his own leaders. Party loyalty, which he regards as a kind of civic virtue, is fast becoming a matter of temporary convenience.

But it is the consequences that really alarm him. Business, he says, cannot go on in an atmosphere of perpetual suspicion. Capital is timid, and it will not venture while the rules of the game are being changed in the middle of the play. Industry will be paralyzed, and while we are quarrelling among ourselves our foreign rivals, who have no such scruples, will quietly overtake us. And when the confusion has gone far enough, the people, weary of debate, will perhaps turn to some military strongman who promises order. That, as he reads history, is how such experiments have generally ended.
