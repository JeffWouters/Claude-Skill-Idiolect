What should we say when an engineer proposes to rewrite a legacy system from scratch? The instinct of most managers is to argue about cost and risk, and these arguments are sound as far as they go, but they rarely persuade, because the proposal is seldom really about the system. It may be that the more useful response is to ask which particular frustrations lie behind it, and then to ease them one at a time: by renaming what is misleadingly named, by deleting what nobody uses, by documenting what nobody understands, and by treating incremental improvement as work worth recognising rather than as maintenance to be tolerated.

There is also something more personal in the wish to start again. A rewrite carries the hope of becoming a better engineer, of finally building the thing properly, without the compromises that seem to disfigure the existing code. I suspect this hope is sincere, and I suspect it is also misplaced, since the pressures that shaped the old system — deadlines, shifting requirements, incomplete knowledge — have not gone anywhere; they will return in time, and the new system will begin to acquire the same compromises for the same reasons.

It seems to me that the most helpful thing to remember is that the earlier authors were rarely careless. They worked under limits much like our own, and many of the decisions that now look strange were reasonable given what they knew at the time. Improving the code we have inherited is less glamorous than replacing it, and it offers no clean beginning; it is, I think, considerably more likely to leave the system better than we found it. Perhaps the wiser ambition is not to write the code our predecessors should have written but to become the predecessors our successors will be glad to have had.
