I have a weakness, I confess, for streets that run along the water.  Put me down in any town that has a harbour and I will find my way to it, sooner or later, without anybody's assistance.  There is something about the edge of a city, where the pavement stops and the shipping begins, that I have never been able to resist.  And of all such streets the San Francisco waterfront is, to my mind, one of the most agreeable.  [detail needed: a first-hand observation of the waterfront]

Along the bay stand the piers of the various shipping companies, one after another, each with its name across the front in letters large enough to be read from the deck of a passing vessel.  They put one, naturally, in mind of the steamship buildings which line the Hudson shore of Manhattan.  The resemblance is considerable.  There is the same procession of great sheds, the same traffic of drays and trucks at the entrances, the same air of important business being done by somebody else.

But what I particularly enjoy are the sailors' shops.  Now, it is a remarkable thing about sailors' shops that they are exactly alike in every port in the world.  I have not, it is true, visited every port in the world; but I am quite prepared to believe it.  You could be set down blindfolded before one of these establishments, have the bandage removed, and not know whether you were on the Pacific or the Atlantic.  The merchandise, so to say, speaks one universal language.

It is not, I should explain, a language of restraint.  The sailor, when he comes ashore with money in his pocket, does not want anything quiet.  He wants something that will show.  And so the windows are crowded with articles of the most conspicuous description.  Chief among these, and most characteristic, is the decorative plate: a heavy, thick affair, painted in the brightest colours obtainable, and hung up by a ribbon.  Why a sailor should want a plate to hang on the wall I cannot tell you.  Perhaps that is the charm of it.
