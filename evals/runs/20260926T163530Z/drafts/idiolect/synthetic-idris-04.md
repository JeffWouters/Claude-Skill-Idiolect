Why do we find it so difficult to say no to a colleague? The request is usually reasonable, the person asking is usually someone we like, and the cost of agreeing seems small at the moment of agreement. It may be that the difficulty lies less in the refusal itself than in what it obliges us to admit: that our time is limited, that we cannot do everything we are asked to do, and that some of the things we have already promised matter more than this one.

A good refusal, it seems to me, has a few recognisable qualities. It comes promptly, before the person asking has built any plans around our help; it names the real competing priority rather than a vague appeal to being busy; and it offers something smaller in place of what was requested, perhaps a more modest piece of help, or the name of someone better placed to give it. What this asks of us is mostly the willingness to disappoint someone now rather than later, and the recognition that a disappointment delivered early is considerably kinder than a commitment that quietly collapses later, when the colleague has been depending on it.

There is also a less obvious benefit, which I suspect we tend to underestimate. A person who refuses honestly gives their agreement a weight it would not otherwise have; when they do say yes, the colleague can trust that the yes is meant. You may have worked with someone whose agreement was so automatic that it had stopped carrying any information at all, and noticed how little you came to rely on it. Perhaps the respectful refusal is not a small failure of generosity but one of its more demanding forms, since relationships are strengthened less by how often we agree than by how much our agreement can be believed.
