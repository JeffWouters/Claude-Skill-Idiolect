Ask any overworked journalist what he wants most, and he will tell you: time. Time to think, time to read, time above all to write the book he has been carrying around in his head for years. He pictures the empty weeks ahead of him, the quiet desk, the long mornings with nothing to interrupt him. Then, now and then, somebody gives him exactly that. And very often nothing comes of it. The weeks pass, the desk stays quiet, and the book stays in his head. It seems that for some of us the pressure of a deadline is not the enemy of the work but the thing that makes it happen at all. Take the pressure away and the machinery stops.

The tools a writer uses are just as personal as the conditions he works under. Some authors will write only by hand, trusting the slow movement of the pen to keep pace with their thinking. Others cannot imagine working without a typewriter and the steady rattle of its keys. Some dictate, walking up and down the room while another person takes down the words. And a good many use a mix of all of these: a first draft in pencil, corrections in ink, a clean copy on the machine. Place matters too, and here writers split into two camps. There are those who must live in the middle of what they describe. They need the streets outside the window, the voices in the next room, the smell and noise of the world they are putting on paper. Without that, the writing goes thin. And then there are those who have to go far away before they can write about home at all. Distance gives them the clarity that closeness takes away; only from a foreign room can they see their own town plainly. [example needed] Neither camp is wrong. Each writer has to find out, usually the hard way, which one he belongs to.
