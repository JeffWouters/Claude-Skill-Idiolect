There is something about a harbor street that draws people in, even people who have no business with ships. The streets that run along the water have a character of their own, rougher and livelier than the rest of the city, and they seem to belong less to the town behind them than to the sea in front. San Francisco's waterfront is one of the best of them.

Along the bay stand the piers of the shipping companies, one after another, each with its name painted large across the front. Anyone who knows New York will be reminded of the steamship buildings that line Manhattan's Hudson shore, the same row of broad sheds facing the water, the same sense that the whole world is only a voyage away. [example needed] Walking past them, you feel the pull of departure, whether or not you have any intention of going anywhere.

Across the street from the piers are the shops that serve the sailors, and here is a curious thing: they look the same in every port city. The window you pass in San Francisco could have been lifted from any other harbor town and set down here without anyone noticing. The same goods, arranged in the same way, wait for the same kind of customer, a man just ashore with money in his pocket and a few days to spend it.

What those shops sell leans heavily toward the showy. Nothing is quiet or plain if it can be made bright instead. Above all there are the plates: thick, heavy decorative plates, painted in strong colors, each one hung up by a length of ribbon so that it can go straight onto a wall. [example needed] They are not meant for eating from. They are meant to be carried home across an ocean and hung up as proof that the sailor has been somewhere and remembered someone. There is a whole philosophy of the sea in those plates, and in the streets that sell them: a taste for color, for keepsakes, and for something to show when the voyage is over.
