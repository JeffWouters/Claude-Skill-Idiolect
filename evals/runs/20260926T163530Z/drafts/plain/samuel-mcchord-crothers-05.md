There is an old English habit, older than most of our institutions and a good deal hardier, of meeting a bad law not with argument alone but with open refusal. It is worth understanding what that habit assumes, because it assumes something about law itself that the textbooks tend to leave out.

A statute, once passed, looks finished. It has been debated, voted, signed and printed. Yet in a real sense it is not secure until the public obeys it. Until that moment it is a claim rather than a settled fact, and the claim can be contested. Those who think it unjust have a choice that is not quite rebellion and not quite submission: they may decline to comply, say plainly that they decline, and take whatever punishment the law provides. They do not hide, and they do not fight the constable at the door. They simply let the state discover how much it will cost to make the law real.

The seventeenth century is full of such people. The dissenters of that age were told how to worship, where to preach and whom to acknowledge, and a great many of them would not do it. Bunyan is the familiar case, and Fox beside him. Both could have bought their freedom with a little conformity. Both chose prison instead, and not as a gesture made once and then regretted, but as a settled course. The prison became, in effect, their reply to the statute. Every month they spent there was a public statement that the law had not yet won.

Fox gives the tradition its most characteristic flavour, because the thing he refused to do was so small. He would not take off his hat. To the magistrates this looked like mere insolence, and they treated it so. Fox did not answer them with a theory of civil liberty. He answered with Scripture, pointing to the three Hebrews in Daniel, who stood before the king's command and would not bend to it. The appeal was shrewd. It placed his stubbornness inside a story every listener knew and most of them admired, and it made the bench look rather less like guardians of order and rather more like the king in that story.

That, I think, is the heart of the thing. Open disobedience does not deny the authority of law. It puts that authority on trial, in public, and lets the country decide.
