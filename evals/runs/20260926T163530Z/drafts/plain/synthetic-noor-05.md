When the full one has had its time, it gets turned into the empty one, and the cycle starts again.

What goes in changes with the year. In spring it is mostly [example needed]; through summer, grass clippings and kitchen peelings; in autumn, the leaves and the spent stalks from the beds; in winter, not much beyond scraps from the kitchen. None of it is measured. The heap takes what the garden and the house give it.

Turning is a job for the right day. Too wet and the material clumps and weighs twice what it should; too hot and dry and it is dusty, hard work for little gain. I wait for a mild, overcast spell after a dry few days, when the heap is damp but not sodden.

Opening up the full bay is the best part. Pull away the front and the year is there in layers: the top still recognisable as what it was, the middle darker and softer, and the bottom close to crumbly soil that smells of woods rather than rubbish. You can read the seasons in it, if you look.

The fork I use is old, [details of the fork needed], with a handle worn smooth where hands have held it for years. It is not the lightest tool in the shed, but it is the one that feels right for this.

The method is simple. The least-rotted material from the top goes in first, to the bottom of the empty bay, where it will sit longest and be buried deepest. The further down the full bay I dig, the more finished the compost, and the higher it lands in the new heap. By the end, the order has been turned upside down, and the slow work can carry on.
