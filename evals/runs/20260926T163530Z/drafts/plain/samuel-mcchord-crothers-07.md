Anyone who comes back to Dickens now has to get past his earlier readers first. They wept over him, openly and in great numbers, and they said so. The modern reader is inclined to treat those tears with a kind of embarrassed tolerance. We do not defend them; we excuse them, as one excuses the enthusiasms of a relative who was young in a different decade. They did not know better, we suggest, and it did them no harm.

The critics of our own time have gone further, and on the main point I think they are right. Dickens's characters are wholly obvious. His emotional effects are wholly obvious too. When he wants us to grieve, the grief is prepared, announced and delivered, and there is no mistaking the moment it arrives. When he wants us to laugh, the joke is labelled before it is told. Nothing is left in shadow. There is, in the strict sense, nothing to interpret, because the author has already done all the interpreting and handed us the result.

This has a curious consequence for the reader who enjoys him. Suppose you are delighted by Sam Weller, as nearly everyone has been. Or suppose you detest Pecksniff, as you are plainly meant to. Neither response tells anyone anything about you. It shows no discernment, no special insight into character, because Dickens has spelled out every judgment in advance. You have not noticed Pecksniff's hypocrisy; you have been told about it, repeatedly and with gusto, and you have agreed. Liking Sam Weller is not a critical opinion. It is closer to accepting an invitation.

That is a real limitation, and it explains a good deal about why rereading him can feel strange. The pleasures are all still there, but they are pleasures of recognition rather than discovery. One goes back not to find something new but to be given the same thing again.

Whether that settles the question of his worth is another matter. It does settle the question of the tears. They were not a sign of shallow readers. They were the response the books were built to produce.
