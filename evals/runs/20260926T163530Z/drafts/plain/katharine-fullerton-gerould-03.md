Anyone who wants evidence that serious cultural standards are disappearing in America does not have to look very hard. The signs are everywhere, and most of them are presented to us not as losses but as progress.

Begin with the schools. The subjects that once formed the core of an education, the old languages, the classics, the long reading of history and literature, have been pushed to the margins. In their place come modern subjects and vocational courses, which promise something practical and immediate. There is nothing wrong with learning a trade. What is wrong is the assumption that this is all education is for. The clearest example I know is a reading list issued in Massachusetts, of all places, which counted popular fiction as literature. The same drift shows up well beyond the classroom. Art, philosophy and fiction have each walked away from the ideals that used to steady them. Art no longer aims much at beauty, philosophy no longer aims much at wisdom, and fiction no longer aims much at showing us how to live. Each has found it easier to entertain, to shock or to comment on the passing moment. And the public has gone along. Children prefer light reading, which is natural enough in children. But their parents prefer it too, and that is harder to excuse. A household where nobody reaches for a difficult book is a household where the habit will not be passed on.

This matters because real cultivation is slow. It is not something a person acquires in a semester or a family acquires in one lifetime. It takes generations of steady effort, most of it unrewarded. A father reads to his children things they will not appreciate for many years. A mother insists on standards of speech and manners that win her no thanks. None of this earns money, and very little of it earns flattery. Cultivation is, in the plainest commercial sense, a bad investment. That is exactly why it is rare, and why it is so easily lost when a society begins to judge everything by what it pays.

There is one more symptom worth naming. It has become fashionable to boast of humble origins, to announce that one grew up with no books in the house and made one's way without any of that old-fashioned polish. The boast is meant as modesty, but it is really a kind of reverse snobbery. It turns the absence of cultivation into a badge of honor and quietly suggests that those who did inherit it should apologize. A society that sneers at inherited learning will not have much of it to inherit.

None of these signs is decisive on its own. Taken together, they point in one direction, and it is not upward. If we want serious standards to survive, someone will have to keep them without expecting to be paid or praised for it.
