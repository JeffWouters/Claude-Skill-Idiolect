Americans have always liked a large claim. It is one of the first things a visitor notices and one of the last things a native thinks to question. We do not merely have a town; we have the finest town. We do not simply build; we build the biggest. The habit is old, and it seems to have grown up with the country.

It goes back at least to the frontier. The men and women who settled the edges of the continent did not think of themselves as ordinary people doing ordinary things. They saw themselves as historic figures, actors in a drama that later generations would study. When Ethan Allen fell out with Congress, he did not lodge a modest complaint; he threatened it in sweeping terms, as though he were one sovereign power addressing another. I was reminded of this on a trip through a string of Midwestern towns. Each had its boast, and each boast was the same shape: the world's largest something. [examples needed.] The claims grew more ambitious as the towns grew smaller, until I reached a modest village whose hotel, a plain enough building, announced itself as the world's largest of its kind. Nobody seemed embarrassed. The claim was not really meant to be checked; it was a way of saying that this place, too, mattered, and that it would not be overlooked.
