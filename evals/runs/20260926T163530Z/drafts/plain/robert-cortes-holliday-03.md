If you were a stranger to New York and judged the city only by where its undertakers keep shop, you would come to a strange conclusion about who dies here.

Walk the wealthy avenues and you will look a long while for a funeral business. There are jewelers, there are florists, there are shops that sell one kind of glove and nothing else, but of undertakers there are almost none. The impression it leaves is that rich people simply do not die, or at least not in the neighborhood. Perhaps they are too busy. Perhaps it is considered a lapse of taste, the sort of thing one's family would rather handle discreetly, somewhere else, the way one handles a divorce.

There is an exception uptown, a funeral hall so lavish it seems to have wandered in from some other trade. It is filled with art objects, pieces set out carefully in the rooms, and the whole place has the hushed, expensive air of a gallery where nothing has a price tag because anyone who needs to ask should not be there. [example needed] It is handsome, in its way, but it feels out of place, as if the building had been meant for something else and had only lately and reluctantly taken up the dead.

Most of the city's funeral businesses are nothing like that. They are small shops in the poorer districts, set in among the grocers and the shoe repairers and the laundries, with a modest window and a plain name over the door. They are not hidden and they are not grand. They are simply part of the street.

And what struck me most about them is the company they keep. Neighbors gather outside, or just inside the door, and talk. Nobody hurries. They lean against the wall and trade news and ask after each other's families, and the conversation moves at an easy pace, the way conversations do in a place where people expect to see one another again tomorrow. Death, in these districts, has a storefront and regular hours, and nobody seems to find that especially remarkable.

It may be the most sensible arrangement in the city.
