What is a backlog, really, once it has grown past the point at which anyone can read it from beginning to end? The official answer is that it is a queue of work waiting its turn, ordered by value and consumed from the top, and in a young team with a short list that is roughly accurate. Yet most backlogs do not behave like queues; items enter far more quickly than they leave, a considerable proportion are never touched again, and the list grows until it becomes a kind of archive that nobody consults and nobody feels able to close. It may be that the backlog survives in this form because it does something other than schedule work. I suspect it serves, in some sense, as the place where a team keeps its deferred promises — the request it could not refuse, the improvement it genuinely meant to make, the complaint it wished to acknowledge without having to answer — and that its real function is less to organise the future than to make the present bearable.

Consider what happens when a colleague from another team asks for something reasonable that there is simply no time to do. To decline outright feels unkind, and to agree feels dishonest, since everyone involved knows the coming months are already spoken for; the ticket offers a third way, in which the request is written down, given a label and a priority, and placed somewhere safe. The colleague leaves with the sense of having been heard, and the team leaves without having committed to anything in particular, which is, arguably, a small act of diplomacy rather than a failure of planning. Is it any wonder that the list keeps growing? Each item is a courtesy extended at the moment it was needed, and the cost of that courtesy is passed on to some later reader who will find, a long time afterwards, a request whose author has moved on and whose context has quietly evaporated.
