What might it look like to say no in a way that honours the colleague rather than merely protecting ourselves? It seems to me that the most generous refusals share a few quiet features: they are given early rather than after a long silence, they explain the competing commitment rather than gesturing vaguely at being busy, and they offer something smaller in place of the whole — a later hour, a partial reading, the name of someone better placed to help. None of this requires a policy or a new way of tracking work; it asks only that we treat the refusal as information our colleague is entitled to, rather than as a verdict on them. It is, admittedly, harder than saying yes, since it requires us to admit in plain words that our time is finite and that we have chosen, however reluctantly, to spend it elsewhere. That admission can feel faintly exposing, and the colleague may well be disappointed; yet disappointment honestly delivered tends to heal far more quickly than a promise quietly broken.

There is something to be said, finally, for the trust that a well-made refusal can build. A colleague who has heard us say no, and who has seen that our reasons were real and our regret was genuine, learns that our yes means something; the word acquires a weight it never had when it was given to everyone and everything. In this sense saying no is less a withdrawal from a relationship than a contribution to it, since it tells the other person where we actually stand and lets them plan accordingly. Perhaps the deepest courtesy we can offer one another at work is not endless availability but an honest account of what we can carry, given kindly and in good time, so that the help we do give is help that can be relied upon.
