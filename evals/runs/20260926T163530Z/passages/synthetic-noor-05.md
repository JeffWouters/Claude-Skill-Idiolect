The compost heap is at the end of the garden, behind the shed. It is two bays made of old pallets, each about a metre square. I turn it twice a year. Once in April, once in October.

The heap has been there longer than the shed. My father built the first bays from floorboards. Those rotted years ago, which seems fair. The pallets came from a builder's skip on the corner. They will rot too.

All year the left bay fills. Peelings, eggshells, tea leaves, coffee grounds. Grass cuttings in summer. Leaves in autumn. Torn cardboard from the boxes the post brings. I keep a green caddy by the back door and empty it most days. It holds four litres.

The right bay waits. It stands empty for most of the year, apart from a few old stalks. On turning day everything goes from left to right.

Here is how.

I wait for a dry day with no wind. Wet compost is twice the weight and sticks to the fork. Wind blows the dry top layer across the lawn. So a still morning, after a night without rain.

I take the front boards off the left bay first. They slide up out of two grooves. Then the heap stands open like a cut cake. You can see the year in it. Dark at the bottom, brown in the middle, green and pale at the top.

The tool is a garden fork with four tines. It was my grandfather's. The handle is ash and has gone grey and smooth. One tine is bent a little inwards. I have meant to straighten it for three winters.

So then the work. I lift the top layer first and throw it into the bottom of the right bay. The unrotted stuff goes to the bottom, where it is warm. That is the rule: the top goes under. Otherwise it just sits there and dries out.
