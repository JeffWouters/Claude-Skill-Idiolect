Why are we so easily persuaded that the next tool will solve our problems? There is a pattern, familiar in almost every technical organisation, in which dissatisfaction with current ways of working attaches itself to a particular piece of software — the ticketing system, the build pipeline, the chat platform — and the conviction grows that replacing it will relieve the underlying frustration. The migration is planned, sometimes with considerable enthusiasm; the new tool arrives; there is a brief period of optimism, during which everything feels fresher and more promising. Gradually, however, the old frustrations return in slightly altered form, and the team begins to suspect that the problem may not have been the tool after all. I do not wish to suggest that tools never matter, since some are genuinely better than others; it may be, however, that we systematically overestimate how much of our difficulty they are responsible for.

Part of the appeal, I suspect, is that tools offer a tangible object on which to focus a diffuse unhappiness. It is difficult to articulate the problem when the real issue is a lack of shared priorities, or an unclear division of responsibility, or a culture in which people are reluctant to raise concerns; these things are abstract, uncomfortable, and implicate the people discussing them. A tool, by contrast, can be criticised without implicating anyone; it is external, replaceable, and conveniently unable to defend itself. Is it any wonder that organisational discontent so often expresses itself as a desire for new software? The migration becomes a kind of displacement activity — an enormous amount of effort directed at a problem that is real but secondary, while the primary problem remains untouched and, in some cases, grows worse for having been ignored.

There is also the matter of what a tool encodes. Every piece of software embodies certain assumptions about how work ought to be done; a ticketing system implies a particular model of how tasks are defined and tracked, a deployment platform implies a particular relationship between development and operations, and so on. When we adopt a new tool, we are therefore adopting, perhaps without noticing, a set of practices that were designed by someone else for some other organisation. Occasionally those practices fit us well, and the tool genuinely improves our work; more often, arguably, we find ourselves either bending our habits to fit the tool or bending the tool until it resembles the one we abandoned. You may have witnessed this second outcome, in which a carefully selected new system is configured, over time, into an almost exact replica of its predecessor.

What would it mean to approach tools more thoughtfully? It seems to me that the essential step is to separate the conversation about tools from the conversation about practices, and to have the second conversation first. Before asking which system we should use, we might ask what we are actually trying to do, what is preventing us from doing it, and whether the obstacle lies in the software or in ourselves. Sometimes the answer will genuinely point to a tool, in which case the migration can proceed with a clearer sense of purpose; sometimes it will point elsewhere, to decisions that need to be made or conversations that need to be had, and no amount of new software will substitute for them. This is slower and less satisfying than choosing something new, since it denies us the pleasure of a fresh start. It is also, I suspect, considerably more likely to change anything.

There is perhaps a broader temptation at work here, one that extends well beyond software. We are inclined to believe that our difficulties are technical because technical difficulties are the kind we know how to address; they can be specified, evaluated, purchased, and installed. The difficulties that really constrain us are more often human — questions of trust, attention, communication and purpose that no product can resolve on our behalf. Tools can support good practices, and they can certainly obstruct them, but they cannot supply what we have failed to cultivate among ourselves. Perhaps the wisest relationship to our tools is one of gratitude without illusion: an appreciation of what they make possible, joined to a clear recognition that the harder work has always been, and will always remain, the work we do with one another.
