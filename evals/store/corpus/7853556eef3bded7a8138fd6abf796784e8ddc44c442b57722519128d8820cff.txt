What does a team actually communicate to someone in their first weeks? The formal answer is usually a collection of documents, access requests, introductory meetings and perhaps a small starter task designed to familiarise the newcomer with the codebase; these things matter, and teams that neglect them tend to pay for it later. Yet I suspect that the more important communication happens informally, in the tone of the first conversations, in the way questions are received, in the unspoken signals about what is valued and what is merely tolerated. The newcomer is, in some sense, an anthropologist arriving in an unfamiliar culture, attending closely to every gesture in an attempt to understand the rules that nobody has written down. It may be that the lessons learned in those first weeks shape a person's relationship with a team for a long time afterwards, long after the formal onboarding materials have been forgotten.

Consider the experience of asking a question when you are new. You do not yet know which questions are considered reasonable and which are considered naive; you do not know whose time is precious and whose is available; you do not know whether the answer you need is written down somewhere you ought to have found it. Each question therefore carries a small risk, and the newcomer must decide, repeatedly and with incomplete information, whether that risk is worth taking. How the team responds to those early questions determines, arguably, how many more will be asked. A patient and generous answer encourages further inquiry; a hurried or faintly impatient one teaches the newcomer to struggle alone, and struggling alone is a habit that can persist for a considerable time, well beyond the period in which anyone would still describe the person as new.

There is a particular difficulty for teams that have worked together for a long time, since they have accumulated a vast store of shared context that no longer feels like context at all. The history of past decisions, the reasons behind current conventions, the private vocabulary that has developed around certain parts of the system — all of this is so familiar to established members that they can hardly perceive it, much less explain it. You might say that the newcomer is the only person on the team who can actually see the team's culture, because they are the only one who has not yet been absorbed into it. Is this not a valuable perspective, and one that teams might deliberately seek out rather than simply waiting for it to fade? I suspect that newcomers notice a great many things that established members would benefit from hearing about, if only they were asked and if only they felt safe answering.

It seems to me that the best onboarding treats the newcomer not merely as someone to be taught but as someone who can teach the team something about itself. This means inviting their observations early, before they have learned to stop noticing; it means treating their confusion as information about where the team's knowledge is poorly recorded; it means, perhaps, appointing someone whose explicit responsibility is to make the newcomer feel welcome rather than leaving this to whoever happens to have time. None of these practices is especially elaborate. They require only a shift in perspective, from seeing onboarding as a transfer of knowledge in one direction to seeing it as an exchange from which both parties can learn. The consequence, in my experience, is not only faster integration but a team that remains more aware of its own assumptions.

There is something to be said, finally, for remembering what it was like to be new ourselves. Most of us have forgotten the uncertainty of those first weeks, the careful calibration of every question, the quiet relief when a colleague offered help without being asked; the memory fades as we become established, and with it fades our capacity to extend the same kindness to others. Perhaps the most useful thing an experienced team member can do is to recover that memory deliberately, and to let it shape how they receive the next person who arrives. Teams are not static entities but continuous processes of arrival and departure, and the way we welcome people is, in the end, one of the truest expressions of what kind of community we have chosen to become.
