Who, exactly, are we writing for when we write documentation? The question tends to be answered too quickly, usually with some reference to "the team" or "future maintainers", as though these were stable and knowable audiences rather than abstractions that allow us to avoid imagining an actual reader. It may be that much of the documentation we produce is unhelpful not because it is inaccurate but because it was never addressed to anyone in particular; it records what the author knew at the moment of writing, in the order in which the author happened to think of it, with the assumptions that made sense to the author left carefully unstated. I suspect we have all encountered such documents — thorough, well-intentioned, and almost entirely impenetrable to someone arriving without the context the author took for granted. The failure is not one of effort but of imagination, and imagination is precisely the faculty that our working routines tend to leave undeveloped.

I have come to think of documentation as a form of hospitality, in the old sense of the word: an obligation to the stranger who arrives at your door without warning, tired, uncertain of the customs, and in need of orientation. The host does not know who this stranger will be, but can nevertheless anticipate certain needs — somewhere to rest, some explanation of the household, some indication of where things are kept and which doors should not be opened. Good documentation performs a similar function; it assumes the reader is intelligent but unfamiliar, anticipates the questions they are likely to ask, and offers answers before those questions have to be spoken aloud. What would change if we wrote with that stranger in mind? Arguably a great deal, since the stranger forces us to articulate the context we have long since stopped noticing.

There are institutional reasons why this kind of writing is rare. Documentation is seldom rewarded in the way that new features are rewarded; its benefits accrue slowly, to people who may not yet have joined the organisation, and its absence is felt only indirectly, in the form of repeated questions and prolonged onboarding and a general atmosphere of dependence on whoever happens to remember how things work. It is, in some sense, the purest form of work done for others, and our incentive structures are rarely designed to recognise such work. Is it surprising, then, that it tends to be written hurriedly, at the end of a project, by people who are already thinking about the next one? The consequence is a body of documentation that reflects the priorities of its authors rather than the needs of its readers — a record of what was built rather than a guide to how it might be understood.

If you have ever tried to write for the stranger, you will know that it is considerably harder than writing for yourself. It requires a kind of deliberate forgetting, in which we set aside what we know and try to reconstruct the experience of not knowing it; it requires us to notice the jargon we use without thinking, the steps we skip because they seem obvious, the history we assume because we lived through it. Perhaps the most useful practice is simply to watch someone else attempt to follow what we have written, resisting the urge to intervene, and to observe with some humility where they hesitate and where they go astray. The experience is rarely flattering; it reveals how much of our understanding was never on the page at all. It is also, I suspect, the only reliable way to discover what our documentation actually communicates, as distinct from what we believed it communicated.

There is something quietly moral about this work, though we rarely describe it in those terms. To document well is to acknowledge that our knowledge is not our private property, that the systems we build will outlive our involvement in them, and that the people who inherit them deserve more than a collection of hurried notes. It is to extend a courtesy to someone we will probably never meet, whose gratitude we will never hear, and whose difficulties we can only imagine. Perhaps that is why it matters so much: because so much of working life is organised around visible contribution and immediate recognition, and documentation offers us one of the few remaining occasions to be generous without expecting anything in return.
