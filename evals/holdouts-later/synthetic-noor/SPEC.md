# Noor Vale — style specification

Noor Vale is an invented author (synthetic test data, CC0). This spec describes the voice closely
enough to write new essays without seeing the originals. Figures below were measured across the 12
essays (about 8,050 words, 241 paragraphs, about 1,160 sentences).

## Persona and stance

- A first-person narrator of middle years who lives alone or nearly so in a house with a garden, a
  shed, an allotment plot, and a neighbour over the fence. A household that was once fuller (children
  are implied in the past, never named or dwelt on). Gender is never stated; keep it unstated.
- Practical, patient, handy. Fixes, maintains, cooks, grows, mends. Owns old tools, some inherited
  from a parent or grandparent, and keeps spares in labelled jars.
- Calm and exact. Measures, times and counts things for the pleasure of it, and reports the numbers
  plainly. Admits small failures and repeated resolutions without fuss (the tool never bought, the
  job carried over, the vow to do it differently next year).
- Towards the reader: companionable and instructive, never preachy. Addresses the reader as "you"
  when stating a general rule or a sensory fact the reader will recognise ("you" as the generic
  person doing the job). Never asks the reader anything, never tells the reader how to live.
- Feeling is shown through objects and routine, understated to the edge of dryness. Warmth toward
  other people (a neighbour, a grandparent, strangers seen daily) comes through what they do, not
  what the narrator says about them. Small dry humour, never jokes.
- Other people appear briefly and are rarely named; at most one first name per essay (a neighbour).
  Family appears as roles: my father, my grandmother, my grandfather, a daughter.

## Subjects

- One small domestic or near-domestic task, object or routine per essay: a household repair, a
  seasonal maintenance job, a recipe cooked habitually, a crop grown in the garden, a tool or
  workspace, a daily journey, a household system, a craft of mending, a neighbourly arrangement.
- Everyday, low stakes, repeatable. Nothing exotic, nothing public, no news, no work life beyond a
  passing mention of an office.
- Setting: a temperate, northern European-feeling place with cold springs, a west wind, rain, frost,
  swallows, herons, blackbirds. Money in euros and cents. Metric throughout.

## How an essay is built

- **Title.** Plain and concrete, two to five words, title case: "The [object]", "[Gerund] the
  [object]", or "[thing] on/in a [time]". No colon, no pun.
- **Opening.** Starts in the thing itself with a flat declarative statement of fact, usually in the
  first sentence: what it is, how often, how big, how long owned. Often a second short sentence
  qualifies or measures it at once (a count, a duration, a limit such as "not every one, most").
  No scene-setting weather, no anecdote about someone else, no general reflection first.
- **Body.** Mostly chronological process: preparation, then the steps in order, each step a short
  paragraph. Signposts are plain: a one-line paragraph announcing the method or the order ("here is
  how", "here is the method"). Explains the why of each step in one short
  clause-sized sentence (a rule of the trade: what goes wrong if you skip it). Includes one or two
  small past mishaps that taught a rule. Frequent tallies: an essay often has a paragraph that
  begins with a "here is the count" style signpost followed by fragments of numbers.
- **Turn.** About two thirds through, the essay steps back for one or two paragraphs: why the
  narrator keeps doing it this way, what the routine gives them, a quiet admission (the cheaper
  alternative is acknowledged, then set aside). The turn stays concrete and short; the reflection is
  one or two plain sentences, never a thesis. Sometimes introduced by a one-line paragraph that
  starts with "But" or "And".
- **Ending.** The last paragraph moves to the present: "Tonight", "This morning", "In the morning",
  "Last week" then now. It is a still image of the object after the job, two to four sentences,
  often ending on a verbless fragment or a noun phrase with a small concrete detail (a mark, a
  leftover, a light, a single remaining item). No moral, no summary, no line addressed to the
  reader. Often an echo of the opening object or of a detail set up earlier (the spare part, the
  unfinished job, the neighbour doing the same thing again).

## Sentence movement

- Very short sentences. Mean about 7 words, median 6. About 40 percent of sentences are five words
  or fewer; about 17 percent are three words or fewer. Fewer than 4 percent reach 15 words; almost
  none exceed 20; the longest is about 24.
- Fragments are normal and frequent: verbless noun phrases for measurements, colours, sounds and
  lists ("Two trays. Hot on the left."), echoing negatives ("Not more." "Not brown."), and
  one-word-rhythm sequences ("Over, under, over, under." "Hook, drawer, box."). Use runs of two to
  four fragments to describe a thing or a moment, then return to a full sentence.
- Sentence openers, in rough order of frequency: "I" (by far the most), "The", "It", "Then", "A",
  "So", "And", "But", "You", "That", "There is/are", "This", "Not", "By [time]", "If", "When",
  "Some". Starting sentences with "So", "And", "But", "Then" and "Or" is a signature; "So" often
  marks a decision or the start of the next phase, and "So then" is used for the next step.
- Clauses join mainly by full stops. Commas join short lists and occasional pairs; "and" chains
  adjectives ("worn and hard and split" pattern). Subordination is light: "if", "when", "because", "until",
  "while", "before". No long relative-clause chains. Repetition across adjacent sentences for
  rhythm is common (a short claim, then the same verb again with "always"; a colour word said twice).
- Tense: simple past for the particular occasion, simple present for habits, rules and how things
  work, switching freely inside an essay. The ending moves to the present or recent past.
- Similes are rare (one or two per essay), short and homely, built with "like" and a common object
  (a road, soap, combed hair, a harp, a light coming on).

## Paragraph size

- Short paragraphs: mean about 5 sentences, most between 3 and 7. Words per paragraph about 25 to 50.
- About one paragraph in eight is a single sentence or fragment, used for signposts, a result, or a
  pause (for example, a one-line report that the stuck part finally came loose). Rarely more than 9 sentences in a paragraph.
- A typical essay has 18 to 24 paragraphs.

## Punctuation

- Full stops dominate: roughly one per 7 words.
- Commas: about 20 per essay (roughly 3 per 100 words); in lists, after short introductory phrases
  used sparingly, and before a trailing detail.
- Colons: about one per essay, only to introduce a rule, a result or a short list after a
  statement (a pattern like: That is the rule: dry it first.), or in a reported tally.
- Apostrophes only for possessives and "o'clock". Hyphens very rare (a compound adjective at most
  once in an essay).
- Never: semicolons, dashes of any kind, parentheses, exclamation marks, question marks (a
  rhetorical question is written as a statement ending in a full stop), ellipses, quotation marks.
  Speech is reported without quotation marks, introduced by "said," with a lower-case clause
  (a pattern like: He nodded and said, fine by me.), or paraphrased.
- Written notes or labels are given in capitals or after a colon without quotes (a label in capitals on a jar;
  a one-word note after a colon).

## Register and vocabulary

- No contractions at all: "do not", "I have", "it is", "cannot", "will not".
- British spelling and usage: colour, grey, litres, centimetres, millilitres, neighbour,
  aluminium, anticlockwise, spanner, tap, plughole, jug, car boot sale, crisps, allotment, tin,
  sieve, "at the weekend" register. Metric units spelled out in full (centimetres, grams, degrees,
  kilometres an hour, bar); euros and cents for money.
- Numbers: digits for measurements, quantities of ten or more, prices, temperatures, dates' years
  and times; digits may open a sentence ("24 cells each." pattern). Small counts under ten are
  usually words (two trays, three stones, nine radiators), and a word is used for one-off small
  quantities or approximate amounts ("about ten", "four times in five"). Clock times use a full
  stop: 7.42, 17.51; round times as words ("at nine", "at seven o'clock", "half past").
- Vocabulary is plain, concrete, mostly one or two syllables. Nouns of tools, materials, parts and
  household things named precisely (washer, headgear, burr, bevel, warp and weft, filling loop,
  side shoots). Technical terms are introduced then glossed in one short sentence ("That is the
  part that...").
- Recurring kinds of word: measurement (centimetres, minutes, degrees), counting ("by my count",
  "I have kept count"), sensory detail (hiss, knock, tick, smell of metal, the feel under a thumb or
  flat palm), light and weather, colour words (grey most of all, green, brown, gold, pale), and
  time markers (Saturday, October, every morning, three winters ago).
- Adverbs few: slowly, at once, just, still, always, most days. Intensifiers almost absent.
- Hedges in the narrator's honest, small way: "most days", "about", "I think", "or so it looked".

## Length

- 650 to 720 words per essay (measured range 658 to 715, mean about 690). Aim for 680 to 700.
- Title in front matter; body has no headings, no lists, no bold or italics.

## Never

- Never use contractions, semicolons, dashes, parentheses, exclamation marks, question marks,
  ellipses or quotation marks.
- Never use headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold, italics or emoji inside an essay.
- Never open with a question, a quotation, a definition, a general truth about life or a weather
  description unrelated to the job.
- Never end on a moral, a lesson spelled out, an address to the reader or a summary of the essay.
- Never write a sentence longer than about 25 words; never stack subordinate clauses.
- Never use abstract or elevated vocabulary (journey, mindfulness, meaningful, profound, cherish,
  embrace, testament), marketing tone, slang, or irony at anyone's expense.
- Never use imperial units or dollars; never write clock times with a colon.
- Never describe the narrator's own emotions directly at length; never mention therapy, politics,
  religion, social media or current events.
- Never give people full names or physical portraits; never write dialogue scenes.
- Never let an essay cover two separate subjects or drift into a list of tips.
