The bicycle gets a puncture about twice a year. Almost always the back tyre. I mend it at the kitchen door, on the step, with the door open behind me. The light is good there until four.

The kit lives in a tin that once held throat sweets. It was my father's. Inside are six patches, a tube of glue, a stub of chalk, a square of sandpaper and three plastic levers. One lever is cracked. I have meant to replace it for two winters.

This time it was a thorn. I felt it on the way home on Tuesday. A soft thud through the saddle. Then the slow sag. I walked the last kilometre.

Here is the order.

First the wheel comes off. I turn the bicycle over and stand it on the saddle and the bars. I undo the brake. I loosen the nuts with the 15 millimetre spanner. Then the chain has to be lifted off the small cog. That is the fiddly part. Your fingers go black at once.

So then the tyre. I let out what air is left and push the valve up into the rim. Two levers go in, a hand's width apart. You hook them under the bead. That is the stiff edge that holds the tyre to the rim. Then you run the third lever round. The tyre comes away like a lid off a jar.

The tube comes out last. Valve first, or it tears.

Finding the hole is the slow part. I pump the tube up hard and hold it by my ear. Nothing. So I fill the washing-up bowl at the sink and carry it out to the step. I feed the tube through the water a hand's length at a time. Bubbles. A thin line of them, very fine. I mark the place with chalk. A big cross.

Then I check the tyre. This is the step I skipped once. I patched a tube, fitted it, pumped it and was flat again by morning. The thorn was still in the tyre. Now I run my hand slowly round the inside. Flat palm, then fingertips. This time I found it at once. A black spike, about 4 millimetres. I pulled it out with the tweezers from the bathroom.

The tube has to be dry. I wipe it on the tea towel and leave it in the sun for five minutes. Then the sandpaper. You rough up a patch of rubber a little larger than the patch. Glue does not hold on a shiny surface. That is the rule: dry and dull before anything.

The glue goes on thin. One layer. Then you wait. The tin says one minute. I give it three. The glue should go from wet to dull. If you press the patch on while it shines, it slides. So I wait and look at the garden. A blackbird on the fence. The swallows not back yet.

The patch goes on from the middle out. I press it with both thumbs and count to sixty. Then I roll the handle of the screwdriver over it, hard. The foil comes off. The thin plastic stays.

Here is the count since I started keeping it. 11 punctures in six years. Nine on the back. Seven thorns, two bits of glass, one pinched tube that was my own fault, one I never found. Six patches used from this tin. A new tube costs about 7 euros at the shop by the station.

But I do not buy the tube.

The shop would change it in ten minutes. Jan next door says so every time. He leans on the fence and says, just bring it in and have done with it. I think he is right about the time. The patch takes most of an hour. But the tube is fine apart from one small hole. And I like the step, and the bowl, and the bubbles coming up in a line. It is a job with an end to it. Not many are.

Putting it back is the same job in reverse. A little air in the tube first, so it keeps its shape. Valve in. Then the tube into the tyre, and the tyre onto the rim with my thumbs. The last part is hard. I do not use a lever for it. A lever pinches the tube. That was the pinched one in the count.

Then the pump. 4 bar for the back. I check it with my thumb and then with the gauge. The thumb is usually right. Then the wheel goes back in. Chain on, nuts tight, brake closed. I spin the wheel. A soft tick from the valve each time round.

This evening the bicycle is back in the shed. The bowl is upside down on the step to dry. The tin is on the shelf by the door with five patches left. One cracked lever, still.
