I wind the mantel clock every Sunday night. Not most Sundays. Every one, for 11 years now. It came from my grandfather's front room. It stands on the shelf above the stove in a case of dark oak, 31 centimetres high. White face, black numerals. A brass bezel with a small dent at the top.

It is an eight-day clock. That is the rule of it: one winding, eight days. So a week leaves a day spare. The spare day is for forgetting. I have used it four times.

There are two holes in the face. The right one winds the going train. That is the part that turns the hands. The left one winds the strike. The strike rings the hours and one note at the half.

Here is the method.

At nine I turn off the radio. The kitchen goes quiet. Then the tick comes forward, as it does every night once the room is still. I open the bezel with a thumbnail under the catch. The glass swings out to the left.

The key lives in a jar on the same shelf. The jar is labelled KEY in my father's capitals. There are two keys in it. One is brass and worn smooth at the bow. The other is steel and came in a box of odds from a car boot sale. 2 euros 50 for the box. The steel one is a size too big. I have kept it anyway.

I hold the case with my left hand. You steady the clock when you wind it. If you do not, it creeps along the shelf a millimetre a week. By spring it is out of beat and leaning.

So then the going side first. Clockwise. Slow, even turns, half a turn at a time. The spring tightens under the key. You feel it in the wrist before you hear it. A dry click at each tooth of the ratchet.

I stop when the key stops. Not before. I do not force the last half turn. My grandfather said, a spring is a patient thing, do not bully it. I took that as a rule about clocks. I think it was also about my grandfather.

Then the strike side. It takes more turns. By my count, 14 on the going side and 16 on the strike. Most weeks. A cold week takes one fewer. The oil is thicker then, or so it looks.

Here is the count for a year. 52 Sundays. About 1,500 half turns. Two new mainsprings in 11 years, 38 euros each. One cracked glass.

The glass was my doing. Three winters ago I wound it with a tea towel over my shoulder. The towel caught the bezel. The bezel swung shut on the key. A crack ran from the four to the eight. I left it for a month. Then I had a new one cut in town for 22 euros. The man at the counter measured it with a steel rule and said, you will not do that twice. I have not.

I check the time against the radio pips. On Monday morning, not on Sunday. The clock loses. About 40 seconds a week in winter, less in summer. Under the pendulum bob is a small nut. That is the part that sets the rate. A turn to the right shortens the rod and speeds it up. I have turned it twice this year. Each time an eighth of a turn.

Then I close the glass. I set the key back in the jar. I put my hand flat on the top of the case for a moment. It is warm from the stove.

But there are easier clocks.

There is a battery clock in the hall. It keeps better time than this one. It cost 9 euros. It does not need me on a Sunday night or any other night. I change its battery once a year and forget it. I could put that one on the mantel. I have thought about it.

The mantel clock needs me every week. I think that is why I keep it. The week has a hinge in it now. Sunday night, the key, the clicks, the quiet after. Then Monday starts at the next tick.

When the house was fuller the clock struck through everything. Nobody heard it. Plates, doors, the television. Now I hear every hour. The half notes too. At three in the morning, if I am awake, I count the strikes and know where I am.

Tonight I wound it at nine. 14 and 16. The pendulum swings behind its little window, brass and slow. In the jar the steel key, still a size too big.
