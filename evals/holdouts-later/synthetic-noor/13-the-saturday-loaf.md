I bake one loaf every Saturday. The same loaf, for 11 years now. Not every Saturday. About 48 in a year, by my count.

It is a plain white loaf with a little wholemeal in it. 500 grams of flour. 350 millilitres of water. 10 grams of salt, 7 of dried yeast. Nothing else. No sugar, no oil.

The flour comes from the mill shop on the ring road. A 25 kilogram sack costs 31 euros. I keep it in a metal bin with a tight lid in the shed. The mice found an open bag once. They do not get a second chance.

Here is the order.

I start at seven. The kitchen is cold at that hour. So I fill the kettle first and warm the bowl with hot water from the tap. A cold bowl slows the yeast. You lose half an hour and do not know why.

Then the water. I mix it from the kettle and the cold tap until it feels like nothing on the inside of my wrist. That is about 37 degrees. I checked it once with a thermometer. I have not needed one since.

The flour goes in the bowl first. That is the rule: salt on one side, yeast on the other. Salt kills yeast if they sit together. My grandmother never measured anything. She always kept those two apart.

I pour in the water and stir with the handle of a wooden spoon. The handle works better than the bowl of the spoon. Less dough sticks to it. Then I tip it all out onto the table.

The kneading takes 10 minutes. I use the clock on the cooker. Push away with the heel of the hand. Fold it back. Turn it a quarter. Push, fold, turn. Push, fold, turn.

At first it is rough and wet and clings to everything. I do not add flour. That was my mistake for the first two years. Extra flour makes a dry, close loaf. You have to keep going until the dough takes up the water on its own.

By minute six it changes. It pulls together. It stops sticking to the table and then to my fingers. By minute ten it is smooth and cool, like a bar of soap left in the dish. I press it with a thumb. The dent fills back slowly.

So then it rests. It goes back in the bowl under a damp tea towel, on the shelf above the radiator. One hour. A little more in winter. It doubles.

Then I knock it back, shape it and drop it in the tin. The tin was my grandmother's. Black, dented at one corner, 23 centimetres long. Another 45 minutes. The oven goes on at 220 degrees.

Here is the count for one loaf. 10 minutes of kneading. About three hours from kettle to rack. 1.40 euros in flour and yeast. Two bowls, one tin, one spoon to wash.

The loaf bakes for 35 minutes. I tip it out and knock on the base. A hollow sound means it is done. A dull one means five more minutes upside down, without the tin.

Once I forgot it completely. I was at the allotment and came back to a black crust and a kitchen full of smoke. Els had opened her window next door. She said, I thought you had moved out.

But I still do it by hand.

There is a machine in the loft that kneads for you. A daughter gave it to me some years ago. It makes a good loaf. I used it for one winter and then put it back in its box.

I think it is the 10 minutes. The kitchen is quiet and the radio is off. My hands know the dough better than my eyes do. I feel it is ready before I see it.

The bread lasts until Wednesday. Thursday it is toast. Friday the crusts go out for the blackbirds, and Els puts hers out too.

This morning the tin is drying on the rack. There is flour on the table still, a pale square where the dough was. The loaf is cooling by the window. One end already gone.
