The tiles around the bath are white and 15 centimetres square. There are 86 of them, not counting the cut ones along the top. I have regrouted them twice in 19 years. This spring was the third time.

The old grout had gone grey at the bottom. Not all of it. The two rows nearest the water, and the corner under the taps. It was soft there. I could press a thumbnail into it and leave a mark. That is the sign. Grout should be hard like stone. It should not give.

I put the job off for a winter. Then a tile by the soap dish rang hollow when I tapped it. So I bought the grout.

Here is what it takes.

A grout rake with a carbide blade. A bag of white grout, five kilos, 11 euros 40. A rubber float. A bucket, a sponge, a clean cloth. Masking tape for the edge of the bath. An old towel in the bottom of it. The rake was my father's. The blade is new.

The towel matters. The first time, I did not use one. A chip of old grout went down the plughole. A tile corner fell and marked the enamel. The mark is still there. Small and grey. A pencil dot.

Raking out is the slow part. You draw the blade along each joint, not across it. Short strokes. Two or three millimetres deep, no more. If you go deeper you loosen the tile. If you go shallower the new grout has nothing to hold. It comes out as dust and small white crumbs. The sound is dry and steady. Scrape, scrape, tap.

I did the bottom three rows and the corner. That is 41 tiles. It took two evenings, about three hours in all. My wrist knew about it on the Wednesday.

Then the hollow tile. It came off with a putty knife and a little care. The adhesive behind it had gone to powder. I scraped the back, buttered it fresh and pressed it in. Tape across it overnight to hold it level.

So then the cleaning. Every joint vacuumed, then wiped with a damp sponge. Grout will not stick to dust. It sits on top of it and lifts in a month.

The mixing is the part people rush. Water first, then powder. Stir until it is like thick yoghurt. Not runny. Not stiff. Then leave it five minutes and stir again. That is the rule: let it slake. Slaking is the time the powder takes to drink the water. I mix small amounts, about a cup at a time. More than that goes hard in the bucket before I reach it.

The float goes on at an angle. You push the grout across the joints, not along them. Diagonal one way, then the other. It fills the gaps and leaves a grey film on the face of the tiles. The film does not matter yet.

Twenty minutes later I come back with the sponge. Wrung out almost dry. One pass, then turn the sponge. One pass, then rinse. A wet sponge drags the grout out of the joints. I learned that the first time too. The joints went hollow and pale.

Then the handle of a teaspoon. I run it along each line to shape it. A shallow curve, just below the tile edge. Water runs off a curve. It sits on a flat line.

Here is the count. 41 tiles. About 11 metres of joint. Four cups of grout. Six rinses of the bucket. One tile reset. One broken fingernail.

The last step is the haze. An hour after the sponge, a dry cloth. The film comes off like chalk from a board. The tiles looked whiter than they have in years. They are not. The new grout makes them look so.

There is a cheaper way. You can buy a pen that paints old grout white. My neighbour did his last year. It looked fine for a month. Then he stood at the fence and said, it wants doing again already.

But I do not mind the raking. I like to know what is behind a surface. Paint tells you nothing. The rake told me about the loose tile, and the powder behind it. I would rather know.

I still have not sealed it. The bottle says to wait 72 hours. I have waited nine days.

This morning the light came in low through the frosted glass. The new grout was bright against the old. A clean white line three rows high, then grey above it. The top rows can wait another year. My father's rake on the windowsill, still white with dust.
