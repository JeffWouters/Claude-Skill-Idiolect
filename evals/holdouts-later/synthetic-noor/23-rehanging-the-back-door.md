The back door is the oldest door in the house. It is pine, painted green, and it opens onto the garden. It has hung on the same three hinges for as long as I have lived here. 19 years. It gave me no trouble until last October.

Then it began to stick. Only in wet weather. On a dry day it swung shut with a click. After two days of rain it caught at the top corner and I had to lean on it. You know the sound. A dry scrape, then a thump.

I did what most people do. I ignored it for a month. Then I rubbed a candle stub along the top edge. That helped for a week. Wax is a patch, not a fix.

So in November I looked at it properly. I held a torch against the gap from outside while the kitchen was dark. Light all down the hinge side. Light along the bottom. None at the top corner by the lock. The door had dropped. Not much. About three millimetres at the far end.

A door drops when the top hinge works loose. The weight pulls on it every time it opens. The screws go into the frame, and over the years the holes wear wide. The door had hardly swollen at all. It had sagged, and the rain made the last millimetre.

Here is what I used.

My grandfather's screwdriver with the wooden handle. A block plane. A bradawl. Six new brass screws, 50 millimetres long, from the jar marked HINGE. A handful of matchsticks. Wood glue. Two wedges cut from an old shelf.

Here is the method.

First the wedges go under the door, one each side, so it is held at the right height. You do not take a door off its hinges without something to catch it. I learned that 11 years ago with a shed door. It fell on my foot and split along the bottom rail. I still have the shed door. The foot mended quicker.

Then the pins. These hinges have loose pins. I tapped each one up from below with a nail and a hammer. The bottom one came easily. The middle one came slowly. The top one would not move at all.

So then some oil, and a cup of tea, and a second try.

It came out at 10.20.

With the pins out the door lifted away, and I laid it on two trestles on the grass. It is heavier than it looks. About 30 kilograms, I think. Old pine is dense and there are many coats of paint on it. Green over cream over brown.

The top hinge holes in the frame were worn round and soft. A screw would go in and turn and turn and never bite. I dipped the matchsticks in glue and pushed four or five into each hole. Then I snapped them off flush. That is the old trick: fresh wood for the new screw to cut into.

While the glue set I planed the top edge of the door. Only a little. Three long strokes at the lock end, then a check, then two more. You plane toward the corner, never off it, or the corner splits. I painted the bare wood at once. Bare wood takes on water. That is how it started in the first place.

By one o'clock the glue was hard. I made pilot holes with the bradawl and drove in the new screws. They pulled the hinge leaf tight against the frame. Brass is soft, so you go slowly and stop when it is firm. A stripped head in brass is there for good.

Then the door went back on. That is a job for two people, and I did it alone. Wedges again, and a lot of small lifting with the toe of a boot. The pins dropped in. Bottom, middle, top.

I shut it.

Click.

Here is the count. One afternoon. Six screws at 40 cents each. About twenty matchsticks. One cup of tea gone cold on the step.

A joiner would have done it in an hour. Marit next door said as much when she looked over the fence. She said, you could have rung someone. I could have. But I like to know why a thing goes wrong. A door that sticks is telling you about its top hinge. Once I know that, I can hear it.

And it will drop again. Not for ten years or so. The new screws will wear their holes the way the old ones did. That is what doors do.

Tonight it has rained since four. I opened the back door at six to put out the peelings. It swung shut behind me without a sound. On the step, a curl of green shaving I did not sweep up.
