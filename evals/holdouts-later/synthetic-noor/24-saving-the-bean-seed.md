I save seed from the runner beans every year. Not from every plant. From six of them, the best six, marked in July with a twist of red wool.

The beans grow up a row of hazel poles at the allotment. Twelve poles, tied at the top in pairs. The variety is an old one with scarlet flowers. My grandfather grew it, and his seed came from a man two plots down. I have never bought a packet. I do not know its proper name.

So the choosing happens early. In July I walk the row and look for the plants that set first and climb cleanest. Then I tie the wool. After that I do not pick from those six. It is hard in August. The pods hang there, long and green and straight, and I walk past them to the others.

That is the rule: you cannot eat the beans you save.

By late September the marked pods change. They go pale, then yellow, then a dry brown like a paper bag. The seeds inside swell and harden. You can hear them if you shake a pod. A dry tick against the shell.

I leave them on the plant as long as the weather allows. The longer they stay, the better they keep. But frost ruins them. A bean that freezes while still soft will rot in the envelope by March. I learned that four winters ago. I waited one week too long and lost most of a year's seed to a single cold night. Since then I watch the forecast from the middle of October.

So I pick on the first dry Saturday after the frost warnings start. This year it was 18 October. Grey sky, a west wind, the ground still wet.

Here is how.

I cut each pod with scissors. Pulling tears the stem and shakes the pole. The pods go into a shallow wooden tray, one layer only. Then the tray goes into the shed and sits on the top shelf for two weeks. The shed is dry and cold. Seed dries slowly there. That is what it wants.

After two weeks the pods crack open under a thumb. I shell them at the kitchen table on newspaper. The beans come out loose and hard. Purple and black, mottled, each one a little different. Like pebbles from the same beach.

Then the sorting. I throw out any bean that is wrinkled, soft, split or pale. A pale bean did not finish. A split one lets in damp. I keep only the full, glossy ones.

Here is this year's count. Six plants. 41 pods. 187 beans shelled. 162 kept. 25 to the compost.

The beans then dry for another week on a plate on the windowsill. I test them with a fingernail. If the nail leaves a mark, they are not ready. If it does not, they go into envelopes.

The envelopes are brown paper, the small kind meant for coins. I buy a box of 50 for 3 euros 20. A box lasts years. Paper lets the seed breathe. I tried a glass jar once, the first year after my grandfather died. The beans sweated and grew a grey fur by Christmas.

So then the labels. I write on each envelope in pencil, because ink runs if the shed gets damp. Three lines. RUNNER BEAN. The year. The count inside. Twenty beans an envelope, more or less.

The labelling is the slow part. I do it at the table after supper with the radio on low. My handwriting is not good, so I print. Capitals only. Eight envelopes take most of an hour.

I keep one envelope to sow, two for spares, and give the rest away. Ellen next door takes one every year. She grows hers along her fence. In August she brings me a bag of beans, as if I had none. I always take it.

But I could buy seed. A packet costs about 3 euros and holds 30 beans. It would save me a Saturday and a shelf in the shed. The beans would be fine. They would not be these.

I think it is the line that keeps me at it. My grandfather to me, one year to the next, with nothing bought in between. Each envelope is one more year of it.

Tonight the envelopes stand in a biscuit tin on the shed shelf. Three of them, upright, in front of last year's spares. At the back, one from 2019, never opened. Pencil on brown paper, gone faint.
