The compost heap is at the end of the garden, behind the shed. It is two bays made of old pallets, each about a metre square. I turn it twice a year. Once in April, once in October.

The heap has been there longer than the shed. My father built the first bays from floorboards. Those rotted years ago, which seems fair. The pallets came from a builder's skip on the corner. They will rot too.

All year the left bay fills. Peelings, eggshells, tea leaves, coffee grounds. Grass cuttings in summer. Leaves in autumn. Torn cardboard from the boxes the post brings. I keep a green caddy by the back door and empty it most days. It holds four litres.

The right bay waits. It stands empty for most of the year, apart from a few old stalks. On turning day everything goes from left to right.

Here is how.

I wait for a dry day with no wind. Wet compost is twice the weight and sticks to the fork. Wind blows the dry top layer across the lawn. So a still morning, after a night without rain.

I take the front boards off the left bay first. They slide up out of two grooves. Then the heap stands open like a cut cake. You can see the year in it. Dark at the bottom, brown in the middle, green and pale at the top.

The tool is a garden fork with four tines. It was my grandfather's. The handle is ash and has gone grey and smooth. One tine is bent a little inwards. I have meant to straighten it for three winters.

So then the work. I lift the top layer first and throw it into the bottom of the right bay. The unrotted stuff goes to the bottom, where it is warm. That is the rule: the top goes under. Otherwise it just sits there and dries out.

Every forkful I shake a little. Clumps of grass break up. Air gets in. The heap needs air as much as it needs food. Without it the middle goes wet and sour and grey. It smells like a drain.

I learned that the year I left the heap alone too long. 18 months. The bottom was a black paste. The smell stayed on my hands for two days. I turned it three times that year to put it right.

Halfway down it changes. The compost is dark and loose and crumbles in the hand. There are worms, red and thin, hundreds of them. There is steam on a cold morning. I once pushed a thermometer into the middle. 58 degrees. Hot enough to hurt if you left your hand there.

Here is the count from this October.

Two hours and ten minutes. About 140 forkfuls. Three barrow loads of finished compost from the bottom. One teaspoon, one fork from the kitchen drawer, one sock. No sign of the caddy lid, which went missing in May.

The finished compost is sieved into the barrow. The sieve is a square of wire mesh on a wooden frame. A centimetre gap. What passes through goes on the beds. What does not goes back in the heap. Eggshells mostly, and avocado stones. They never rot.

Then I water the new heap. Two cans, about 20 litres. I put the boards back on the right bay and cover the top with an old carpet. The carpet keeps the heat in and the rain off.

The left bay is empty now. It starts again the same evening, with the peelings from dinner.

But it is heavy work, and slow. A plastic bin with a lid costs 35 euros at the garden centre. You tip in your scraps and wait. Ruud over the fence has one. He does not turn it. He says it gets there in the end. It does, in about two years.

I keep the bays. I like knowing what is in there and how far along it is. I like the heat. Twice a year I see the whole of the year before, in layers. Then I mix it up and start it again.

This morning the carpet on the right bay was steaming in the cold. A thin grey line of it along the edge. On the path, the fork, still with a clod on the bent tine.
