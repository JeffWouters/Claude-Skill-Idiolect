I sow broad beans at the allotment in the second week of November. Not every year. Most years.

The variety is Aquadulce. It is the old one for autumn sowing. It sits in the cold ground all winter and comes up short and dark and stubborn. The spring sowings grow taller. They are also later, and the blackfly find them first.

I keep the seed in a tin on the shed shelf. The label says BEANS in capitals, in my grandfather's hand. The tin held tobacco once. It still smells of it a little when the lid comes off.

The packet costs 3.20 euros. It holds about 60 seeds. I sow 48 and keep the rest in the tin for gaps.

Here is the method.

First the bed. I cleared it in October, after the last of the courgettes. So by November it is bare and brown and flat. I fork it over once more on the day. Not deep. Just enough to break the crust the rain has left. You want it loose on top and firm below. Beans do not like to sit in a hollow of wet soil. They rot there before they start.

Then the line. I use two pegs and a length of green string. The pegs are old tent pegs, bent at the top. I set the string tight and walk the length of it twice. The bed is three metres long and about a metre across.

The rows are double. Two lines 20 centimetres apart, then a path, then two more. In each line the seeds go 20 centimetres apart as well. I stagger them. That way each plant has a little more room to lean.

So then the holes. I use a dibber my father turned from a broom handle. It has a ring cut into it at five centimetres. I push it in to the ring, twist, pull. Push, twist, pull. The soil in November is cold under the palm. It holds the shape of the hole well.

One seed per hole. The scar faces down. I read that somewhere years ago and have done it since. I do not know if it matters. It costs nothing.

Then I close each hole with the side of my boot. Then I walk the rows once more and press down with a flat hand. Firm soil keeps the seed in touch with the damp. Loose soil lets the frost lift it.

Two winters ago I did not firm them. By January half of them had been heaved up. They lay on the surface like pale stones. The pigeons had the rest. That is the rule now: firm every seed.

Here is the count from last year. 48 sown. 41 up by Christmas. Three lost to mice. Two to frost. 36 plants in May. About five kilos of beans in the pod.

The mice are the main trouble. They know where a bean is. I used to soak the seed first to speed it up. That only made it smell stronger. Now I sow it dry. I lay a strip of fine mesh over the rows until the shoots show. The mesh is held down with half bricks.

It takes about an hour and a half, start to finish. The light goes by half past four. I aim to be at the plot by two.

Hanna on the next plot sows hers the same week. She uses a different variety and sows them closer. We compare in April. Hers are usually taller. Mine are usually earlier. Neither of us changes.

And then the matter of why I bother.

Broad beans are cheap in June. You can buy a bag of them at the market for less than the packet of seed. Frozen, cheaper still. I know that. But the plot is empty in winter otherwise, and bare soil in winter only washes away. The beans hold it. They put something back into it too. The roots carry small pink nodules. That is where the nitrogen goes in.

And there is the first picking. Late May, the pods still thin. The beans inside no bigger than a thumbnail. I eat the first ones raw at the plot. Grey green and sweet. Not starchy yet.

This afternoon I finished the fourth row at ten past four. The mesh is down and the bricks are on. The string is wound back on its peg. In the tin on the shelf, twelve beans rattle when I lift it.
