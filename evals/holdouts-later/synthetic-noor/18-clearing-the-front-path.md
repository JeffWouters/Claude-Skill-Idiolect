The front path is 14 metres long. It runs from the step to the gate. Then the pavement takes over for another nine metres to the corner. Not mine, strictly. I clear it anyway.

It snows properly here perhaps twice a winter. Some years, three times. The rest is sleet and grey slush that is gone by noon. A heavy fall is different. By my count it is 20 centimetres or more, and it always comes in the night.

Last Tuesday it came at about two. I woke at six to the quiet. Snow makes the street go still. No tyres, no bus. Just the tick of the boiler and a blackbird somewhere in the hedge.

The shovel lives in the shed with the handle up. It is a wide aluminium one, 50 centimetres across the blade. I bought it at a car boot sale for eight euros. Before that I used my grandfather's coal shovel. It is narrow and heavy and good for nothing but coal.

Here is the method.

First, boots and two pairs of socks. Then gloves with a grip on the palm. Wool gloves are wet in ten minutes. Your hands go stiff before the path is half done.

I start at the step and not at the gate. That way I am always standing on cleared ground. If you start at the gate you tread the snow flat on the way down. Flat snow turns to ice by evening.

So then the first cut. I push the blade straight down the middle, one width, all the way to the gate. That opens a line. Then I work outwards from it, left side first, then right. Push, lift, turn, throw. Push, lift, turn, throw.

You throw to the side the wind is going. Here that is east, mostly. I learned this the first winter in the house. I threw everything onto the west border. The wind brought half of it back across the path by lunch.

I never lift a full blade. A full blade of wet snow weighs about seven kilos. I weighed it once on the kitchen scales, a bucket at a time. Half a blade is enough. Your back knows the difference in the morning.

The last centimetre is the part that matters. The shovel leaves a thin skin that packs down under feet. So I go over the whole path again with the stiff yard broom. Then grit, a handful every metre or so, from the tin by the back door. The tin says SALT on the lid in my father's hand. It has held grit for years.

Here is the count for Tuesday. Front path, 14 metres. Pavement, nine metres. Two passes of the broom. One tin of grit, most of it. 55 minutes, start to finish.

And then the other path.

Ilse lives next door. Her path is the same length as mine and runs beside it, with a low fence between. She is past eighty. She still goes out for bread every morning at eight. So I do her path as well. I have done it for six winters now.

I do hers the same way. Step to gate, middle line first, then the sides. Her step is steeper than mine. It has a crack along the front edge where the frost got in. I clear half a metre in front of it and grit it twice. That is the rule: the step before the path.

She has never asked. The first winter she came to the window and lifted a hand. The next morning there was a jar of plum jam on my step. The lid was tied in cloth. The jam comes every winter now. One jar for each fall of snow.

But I would clear it without the jam. The council runs a snow service for older people. It costs nothing and it comes by about ten, most days. I told her about it once. She said, the council comes too late for the bread. So it is me, at seven.

I think I keep doing both paths for the order of it. A street under snow has no marks at all. By eight there are two clean lines to two front doors. And one set of small boots going out to the corner and back.

The shovel goes back in the shed wet. I stand it blade down on a sack so the edge does not rust. The broom goes beside it. Gloves on the radiator, boots on newspaper by the door.

It snowed again last night, not much. Three centimetres or so. This morning I swept both paths with the broom and left the shovel where it was. On my step, a jar of plum jam. The lid tied in blue cloth.
