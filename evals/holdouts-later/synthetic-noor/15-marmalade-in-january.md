I make marmalade once a year. It is always the third week of January, or near it. That is when the bitter oranges come into the shops. They stay about eight days. Then they are gone until next winter.

The greengrocer on the corner orders two boxes. Most of them go to me and to my neighbour, who keeps bees. I buy 2 kilos. They cost 6.40 euros this year. Last year they cost 5.90.

They are not pretty oranges. The skins are thick and rough and pitted. Some have a green patch near the stalk. The smell is sharp, not sweet. You notice it on your hands for a day.

The pan was my grandmother's. It is aluminium, wide and heavy, with a dent on one side. It holds nine litres. I use it for nothing else.

Here is the method.

I scrub the oranges in cold water with a nail brush. I cut them in half and squeeze out the juice. The pips go into a square of muslin. So does the pith I scrape out with a spoon. That is where the set comes from. Without it the marmalade stays runny.

Then I cut the peel. This is the long part. I cut it thin, about 3 millimetres, on the old board by the window. It takes two hours. I listen to the radio and do not hurry. Thick peel stays chewy. Thin peel goes soft and clear.

The peel goes into the pan with the juice and 4 litres of water. The muslin bag is tied to the handle with string. It hangs down into the water like a tea bag. Then it all sits overnight.

In the morning I simmer it. Two hours, lid off, low heat. The peel is done when it squashes flat between finger and thumb. If it is still firm, the sugar will harden it for good. I learned that in the second winter. 19 jars of something like toffee with bits in.

So then I squeeze the bag. I let it cool first. I press it against the side of the pan with a wooden spoon, then with my hands. A thick grey jelly comes out. It looks like nothing. It does all the work.

Then the sugar. 4 kilos, warmed in the oven at 100 degrees. Warm sugar melts faster. I stir until there are no crystals on the spoon. Not one. Then I turn the heat up high.

This is the part I watch. The boil rises up the pan and stays there. It spits. It smells of burnt sugar and orange oil. I stand back a little. I keep three saucers in the freezer.

Here is the test. After 10 minutes I drop a spoonful on a cold saucer. I wait one minute. I push it with a fingertip. If it wrinkles, it is ready. If it runs, I boil it for five more and try the next saucer. Most years it is the second saucer. Once it was the fourth.

The jars wait in a low oven. I let the marmalade stand in the pan for ten minutes first. That way the peel does not float to the top. I ladle it through a jam funnel and fill each jar to a centimetre from the rim. Then the lids go on hot.

Here is the count. 2 kilos of oranges. 4 kilos of sugar. 19 jars, most years. 16 the year I let it catch on the bottom.

But it would be cheaper to buy it. I know that. A good jar in the supermarket is 3.50 euros and it tastes fine. I bought one once, in a March when mine had run out. It was sweet and even. The peel was all the same size.

I keep making it because of the one week. The oranges come and I have to be ready. The pan comes down from the top shelf. The board comes out. The kitchen smells of it for days. It is the one job in the year that will not wait for me.

I give some away. Four jars go over the fence, and a jar of honey comes back. One goes to a daughter. The rest go on the larder shelf in two rows.

Each lid gets a label in capitals: MARMALADE, JAN, and the year. I have never needed to write more than that.

This morning I had the first of it on toast. It was dark and bitter and a little loose. The pan is drying upside down on the draining board. One saucer still in the freezer.
