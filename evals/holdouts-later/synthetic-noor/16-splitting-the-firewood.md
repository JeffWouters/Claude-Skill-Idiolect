The wood comes in October. Two cubic metres of beech, tipped on the drive in one grey heap. It has been the same load from the same man for eleven years.

He backs the trailer to the kerb and lifts the bed. The logs slide out with a long knock, like stones down a chute. He takes 180 euros in notes and does not count them. Then he is gone, and the heap is mine.

Here is how I do it.

First the tools. A splitting maul, 2.5 kilograms, with a new ash handle. The head was my grandfather's. A small axe for kindling. A steel wedge, a sledge, and a pair of leather gloves gone hard at the fingers. The chopping block is a round of elm, 40 centimetres high. It has stood in the same place by the shed for nine winters.

So then the block. It has to sit level on bare earth. If it rocks, the log jumps and the maul glances off. I learned that on a wet Saturday years ago. The blade came off the side and went into the grass a hand from my boot. I have checked the block with a spirit level ever since.

I stand a log on end. Cut face up, always. Bark side down is no good. I look for the checks first. Those are the small cracks that run out from the centre as the wood dries. They show you where it wants to go. You aim along one and let the weight do the work.

Feet apart. Hands slide together on the swing. Up, over, down. Not hard. Straight.

A good log opens at once. There is a crack and a clean smell, sweet and a little sour. The two halves fall either side of the block. Pale inside. Nearly pink.

A bad log does not. Knots, twisted grain, a branch that grew back on itself. The maul sticks and you have to work it free. So I tap the wedge into the split with the sledge. Three blows, four. Then a long creak, and it goes.

Most logs I split in four. Some in six. Nothing wider than my hand. That is the rule: small pieces dry faster and burn cleaner.

Then the stack. It goes against the side wall of the house, under the eave, on two old pallets. The pallets keep it off the ground. Without them the bottom row turns soft and grey by February. I found that out the first year, when I stacked straight onto the paving.

Bark up on the top row. Bark down lower. At each end the pieces go crossways, one row on the next, like a tower. The ends hold the middle. The middle leans a little in toward the wall. Not much. Two fingers.

I leave a gap of about ten centimetres from the bricks. The air has to move behind it. The west wind does most of the drying. It comes down the side passage all winter.

Here is the count for this year. Two cubic metres. Nine hours of splitting over three weekends. One wheelbarrow, 61 loads. A stack 3.2 metres long and 1.4 high. One blister, left palm.

Next door, Maren does the same job with a hydraulic splitter she hires for a day. 45 euros. She is done by lunch. She waves over the fence while I am still on the first pallet.

And I have thought about it.

It would be quicker. It would be easier on the back. But the maul is quiet, and it was my grandfather's, and I like the weight of it coming down. I like to know each log once before I burn it. That is most of the reason. The rest is habit.

By the end of the third Sunday the drive is bare again. Just bark and chips and a few pale splinters in the gravel. I sweep them into a sack for kindling. Nothing wasted.

This evening I stood at the side wall with the torch. The stack is square and dry on top. A wren has already found the gap behind it. On the block by the shed, the maul, head down, and one last log I could not split.
