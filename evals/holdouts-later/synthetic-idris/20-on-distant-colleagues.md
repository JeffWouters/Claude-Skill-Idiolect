What is a conversation when the people having it are never awake at the same moment? The conventional answer is that it is simply a slower version of the ordinary kind, in which a question is left in the evening and an answer is found in the morning, and the only real cost is a little delay. Most of the advice written about distributed teams follows from that assumption; it concerns overlap hours, handover notes and the careful scheduling of the few meetings that everyone can reasonably attend. Yet anyone who has worked this way for long knows that something more than speed is altered, since a message written to a colleague who is asleep is not quite a message at all but a small letter, composed without the correcting glance of a listener. I suspect that working across time zones is less a problem of logistics than a problem of etiquette, and that the difficulties we attribute to distance are, in some sense, difficulties of manners that nobody has yet written down.

Consider the experience of opening your laptop in the morning to find that the working day on the other side of the world has already happened without you. There is a long thread of messages, a decision that seems to have been reached, a question addressed to you that was asked in hope and has since been answered, less satisfactorily, by someone else. You may recognise the faint sensation that follows, which is perhaps part relief and part exclusion; the work has moved on, and your contribution, if it comes now, will arrive as a reopening rather than a reply. The colleagues who wrote those messages were not trying to leave anyone out — they had a deadline, a customer, a deployment that would not wait — and yet the effect is that one group quietly becomes the place where things are decided and the other becomes the place where things are reviewed. Is it any wonder that people in the later time zone often begin to write defensively, anticipating objections they will not be awake to hear?

There is also the matter of tone, which suffers considerably when every exchange must carry its whole meaning on its own. In a room, a blunt remark is softened by a smile or corrected at once by a look of puzzlement; in writing, left overnight, it has hours in which to be misread, and the reader has no one to ask. The standard remedy is to write more carefully and more completely, and I do not wish to suggest that this is wrong, since a well-constructed message that anticipates the obvious questions is a genuine kindness to a reader who cannot interrupt. The difficulty is that thoroughness has a cost of its own. A long, careful message can feel less like an invitation than a verdict, arriving fully formed with its reasoning already settled, and the colleague who receives it may reasonably wonder whether there is still room to disagree or only room to comply. Arguably, the more polished our asynchronous writing becomes, the more it conceals the uncertainty that would, in a shared room, have drawn other people in.

What might a more humane etiquette of distance look like? It seems to me that it would begin with a willingness to leave some things deliberately unfinished, to write not only what we have concluded but what we are unsure of and where we would welcome a different view. It might mean that a decision reached while half the team is asleep is treated as provisional until they have had a working day to respond, which is slower and occasionally frustrating but which acknowledges that their judgement is not a formality to be collected afterwards. It might also mean sharing the discomfort of the inconvenient meeting, so that the same people are not always the ones speaking at the edge of their evening, tired and a little less articulate than their colleagues. I suspect none of this requires a new tool or an elaborate policy; it asks only that we remember, each time we write, that the reader is a person who will meet our words alone.

There is something quietly moral in the discipline of writing to someone who cannot answer you yet. It asks us to imagine a colleague's morning as clearly as our own evening, to anticipate their confusion without presuming their conclusions and to leave them room to be more than a reviewer of what we have already done. In my experience, the teams that work well across distance are not those with the cleverest schedules but those in which people have learned, slowly, to trust one another's silence. Perhaps that is the real lesson of the conversation that never happens at once: that a working relationship can be sustained by letters as well as by talk, provided each letter is written with the patience and generosity we would hope to find waiting for us when we wake.
