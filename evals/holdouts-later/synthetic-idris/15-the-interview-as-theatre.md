What is a technical interview really for? The official answer is simple enough: an organisation needs to know whether a stranger can do the work, and the interview is the most efficient instrument it has found for finding out. Yet those of us who have sat on either side of the table know that the hour rarely feels like a measurement, and that both parties leave it with a curious sense of having taken part in something neither of them quite designed. The candidate performs competence for people who are, in some sense, performing judgement; the interviewers, for their part, perform a confidence in their own process that most of them privately doubt. It may be that the interview survives, despite a long and rather weary literature on its shortcomings, precisely because it does something other than predict who will do the work well. I suspect it is less an examination than a ritual of mutual performance, in which two groups of strangers agree to pretend, for an hour, that they already understand one another.

Consider the experience from the candidate's chair. You are asked to solve a problem you will almost certainly not meet in the job, on a whiteboard or a shared screen, while several people you have just met watch you think and take notes you cannot read. The skills the situation rewards — composure under observation, fluency in thinking aloud, the knack of seeming unhurried when you are not — are real skills, perhaps, but they are not obviously the ones the work will ask of you, which is mostly to sit quietly with a difficulty for a long time and then ask a colleague for help. Is it any wonder that so many capable engineers come away feeling that they have been assessed on their nerve rather than their judgement? The interviewers, meanwhile, are having an experience of their own; they have been pulled from their ordinary work, handed a set of questions they did not write and asked to reach a verdict about a person on the strength of a single, artificial afternoon.

There is also the matter of what the interview asks of the people conducting it. It is tempting to conclude that the answer lies in structure — standard questions, agreed rubrics, calibrated scoring — and I do not wish to suggest that such measures are without value, since they plainly reduce some of the arbitrariness that makes interviews feel like a lottery. Yet structure introduces a quieter distortion of its own. The more precisely the process is specified, the more the interviewers become custodians of a procedure rather than colleagues meeting a possible colleague, and the more the candidate learns to perform for the rubric instead of for the people. There is a status dynamic here, too, which is rarely acknowledged: the interviewer holds a considerable and temporary power over a stranger, and I suspect most decent people find that power faintly uncomfortable, so they retreat into the safety of the script, which, perhaps, relieves them of the need to exercise a judgement they might later have to defend.

What might a more honest version of the interview look like? It seems to me that it would begin by admitting, to ourselves and to the candidate, that an hour is too short to know anyone, and that what we are really doing is trying to form a reasonable guess about what it would be like to work together. We might, then, choose problems that resemble the work as it is lived — reading someone else's code, asking questions about an unclear requirement, explaining a decision to a sceptical colleague — rather than puzzles chosen for their difficulty. We could invite candidates to ask us the hard questions as well, and answer them without the polish we reserve for recruitment pages, since they are deciding about us as surely as we are deciding about them. None of this needs an elaborate process or new tools; it needs only a willingness to treat the interview as a conversation between equals, which is slower, less tidy and considerably harder to score.

The interview, like so many rituals of working life, persists because everyone involved understands the pretence and nobody quite wishes to be the first to stop observing it. Arguably, it is one of the few occasions on which an organisation meets a stranger with its guard up and its best face on; the stranger, of course, does the same, so that each side ends up judging a performance rather than a person. There is something quietly forgivable in that, since both parties are acting out of the same ordinary anxiety about being found wanting. Perhaps the kindest interview is not the one that reveals the most about a candidate, but the one that leaves them, whatever the outcome, with the sense of having been met with generosity by people who were just as nervous as they were.
