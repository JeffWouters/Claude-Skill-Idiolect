What is a backlog, really, once it has grown past the point at which anyone can read it from beginning to end? The official answer is that it is a queue of work waiting its turn, ordered by value and consumed from the top, and in a young team with a short list that is roughly accurate. Yet most backlogs do not behave like queues; items enter far more quickly than they leave, a considerable proportion are never touched again, and the list grows until it becomes a kind of archive that nobody consults and nobody feels able to close. It may be that the backlog survives in this form because it does something other than schedule work. I suspect it serves, in some sense, as the place where a team keeps its deferred promises — the request it could not refuse, the improvement it genuinely meant to make, the complaint it wished to acknowledge without having to answer — and that its real function is less to organise the future than to make the present bearable.

Consider what happens when a colleague from another team asks for something reasonable that there is simply no time to do. To decline outright feels unkind, and to agree feels dishonest, since everyone involved knows the coming months are already spoken for; the ticket offers a third way, in which the request is written down, given a label and a priority, and placed somewhere safe. The colleague leaves with the sense of having been heard, and the team leaves without having committed to anything in particular, which is, arguably, a small act of diplomacy rather than a failure of planning. Is it any wonder that the list keeps growing? Each item is a courtesy extended at the moment it was needed, and the cost of that courtesy is passed on to some later reader who will find, a long time afterwards, a request whose author has moved on and whose context has quietly evaporated.

There is also the matter of what the backlog does for those of us who maintain it. A long list is a curious kind of reassurance, since it suggests that nothing has been forgotten, that every concern has a home and that the organisation, whatever its difficulties, is at least keeping track. If you have ever read to the bottom of an old backlog, you may have felt something closer to the atmosphere of a churchyard than a plan: the periodic "grooming" sessions resemble the tending of graves, in which names are reread, the grass is trimmed, a few stones are straightened and everyone departs with the sense of a duty discharged. I do not wish to dismiss the habit of writing things down, which is genuinely better than relying on memory, and the standard advice to prune ruthlessly is correct as far as it goes. The difficulty is that pruning feels like breaking a promise, and so it rarely happens, since nobody wants to be the one who closes the ticket that someone once cared about.

What might a more honest backlog look like? It seems to me that it would begin by distinguishing the work we intend to do from the work we merely wish to acknowledge, and by being willing to say so aloud to the person who asked. A team might, perhaps, keep a short list of genuine commitments alongside a separate record of things heard but not promised — the pleasant idea, the half-formed request, the improvement nobody has yet found time for — and might close old items with a sentence of explanation rather than letting them sink without comment. None of this requires new tools or an elaborate process; it requires only the willingness to tell a colleague, kindly and early, that their request is unlikely to be met. That is harder than creating a ticket, and considerably less comfortable, since it exchanges a vague and pleasant deferral for a clear and faintly awkward refusal. Yet a clear refusal can be planned around, whereas a deferred promise can only be waited upon.

The backlog, like so many of the artefacts of working life, reveals how much of what we do rests on a reluctance to disappoint one another. We keep lists partly to remember and partly to postpone the conversations that remembering would require, and the two purposes are so intertwined that we rarely notice when the second has overtaken the first. I am not convinced that the answer is to abolish the list, which does real and useful work; the answer may simply be to read it more honestly, as a record of our good intentions rather than a plan. Perhaps the kindest thing a team can do with its backlog is not to empty it but to let it go gracefully, releasing the promises it never meant to keep so that the ones it does make can be believed.
