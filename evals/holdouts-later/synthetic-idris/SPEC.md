# Idris Holloway: style specification

Idris Holloway is an invented author (synthetic test data, CC0). This spec describes the voice closely enough that a writer who has never seen the twelve fixture essays can produce new ones that pass as his. Figures below are measured across all twelve essays unless marked as approximate.

## 1. Persona and stance towards the reader

- A reflective, experienced practitioner of software and team work, writing as a humane observer of working life. He is never a guru, a salesman or a campaigner. He sounds like someone who has done the work for years and now wants to think about what it means.
- The stance is tentative and inquiring. He puts forward a view, qualifies it, grants what is true in the opposing view, and ends on a hope rather than an order. He is gently critical of institutions and incentive structures, and never of individual people.
- He always gives people the benefit of the doubt. Failure gets put down to systems, incentives, habit, fear or missing context, and never to stupidity or bad faith. When he names a recognisable bad behaviour he explains it sympathetically before he suggests anything else.
- The first person plural ("we", "us", "our") is the main voice, about 8 to 9 uses per essay. It covers the writer, the reader and the profession together. The first person singular appears about 3 times per essay, almost always in a hedging frame (I suspect; I have come to...; in my experience; I am not convinced; a disclaimer that he does not wish to suggest something). He tells no personal anecdotes: "I" is a thinking voice, never a narrator of events.
- He addresses the reader as "you" about twice per essay, usually to invite recognition of an experience the reader has probably had ("you may have seen...", "if you have ever...", "you might say/think of this as..."). Second person is also used for the generic protagonist in a scenario.
- He carries a quiet moral seriousness. Late in an essay, work questions are reframed as questions of kindness, courage, honesty, generosity, humility or what colleagues owe one another. He never preaches, and the moral note is raised with a "perhaps".

## 2. Subjects and essay construction

### Range of subjects
Every essay takes one familiar, ordinary institution or practice from working life in software and technical teams and examines what it really does for people, as opposed to its official purpose. The existing subjects are: the recurring meeting, estimates, documentation, on-call, code review, inheriting old code, deadlines, relearning as an expert, onboarding newcomers, adopting new tools, postmortems and status updates. The subject is always a practice, ritual or experience shared by many teams. It is never a technology, a product, a named company, a news event or a technique tutorial.

### Titles
Short, in title case, with no subtitle. The patterns are "The [Noun Phrase]", "On [Adjective] [Plural Noun]", "[Noun] as [Abstract Noun]" (a metaphor title), "What [X] Is For", "The Uses of a [X]", "The [X] as Fiction", "The Seduction of the [X]" and "[Gerund] in Public/a [Noun]".

### Shape: always exactly five paragraphs
1. **Opening (about 150 to 175 words).** In ten of the twelve essays the first sentence is a direct question that goes to the nature or purpose of the subject (What is X really?, Why do we keep doing X?, Who are we doing X for?, Where does X come from?, How do you describe X?). The other two open on a scene-setting sentence beginning "There is a..." that evokes a recognisable moment or sensation. The paragraph then states the conventional view, suggests that the practice survives because it serves some other, unacknowledged purpose, and offers a thesis with a hedge ("It may be that...", "Arguably...", "I suspect..."). A sentence beginning "I suspect" usually appears in or near the last two sentences of the paragraph.
2. **Second paragraph: examining the experience.** It very often begins with the imperative "Consider..." (Consider the way / the experience of / what happens when...). Otherwise it opens with a follow-up question or "Part of the answer/appeal...". It walks through what the practice looks or feels like from the inside, often as a small generic scenario with no names, times or numbers. It contains a rhetorical question, frequently "Is it any wonder...?" or "Who among us...?".
3. **Third paragraph: a complication.** Typical openings are "There is also..." (followed by the question, the matter, a tendency), "There is a curious/particular...", "There are institutional reasons..." and an opening that grants how tempting a simple conclusion is. It adds a second, subtler angle: an institutional incentive, a psychological or status dynamic, or a distortion the format creates. It fairly concedes what the standard remedy gets right before showing its limits.
4. **Fourth paragraph: the constructive turn.** It opens with a question about doing better (what a more honest or truthful version might look like; what it would mean to approach the thing differently; whether there is a better way; what, then, we might change) or with "It seems to me...". It proposes modest, humane practices, framed as possibilities (might, could, perhaps) and never as steps. It insists these need no elaborate process or new tools, only a change of attitude or perspective. It admits the cost: slower, less comfortable, harder to write or to hear.
5. **Closing paragraph (about 120 to 140 words, usually the shortest).** It widens the lens: the subject becomes one instance of something larger about institutions, inheritance, uncertainty, human fallibility or community. Typical openings are "There is" followed by a broader lesson, something quietly moral or something to be said; "Perhaps the deepest difficulty..."; the subject followed by "like so many" and a gesture at working life in general; and the subject followed by "in the end" and a claim that it is a rare occasion of its kind. The **final sentence** usually begins with "Perhaps" (about 7 of 12). Otherwise it is a balanced aphorism of the "not X but Y" type, or a sentence built on "whether we... or...". It ends on a quiet, slightly lyrical image or a moral quality (courage, generosity, gratitude, curiosity, kindness). It never ends on a call to action, a question left hanging, or a joke.

### Recurring moves inside the essays
- The practice persists because it secretly serves another function (a ritual, a negotiation, a performance, a translation, a displacement activity, social infrastructure).
- An extended metaphor drawn from outside technology, used once and developed across a few sentences: anthropology and ritual, hospitality to a stranger, an abandoned house, archaeology, theatre, translation.
- Fair-minded concession: he disclaims any wish to dismiss the standard remedy, calls the official answer correct but incomplete, or splits an effect into a part that is healthy and a part that, arguably, is not.
- A contrast between the formal description of a practice (policies, templates, guidelines) and the lived experience of it.
- The observation that everyone privately understands the pretence, but nobody wants to go first in stopping it.

## 3. Sentence movement

- **Length:** long. The mean is about 30 words per sentence (per-essay means run from 28 to 31.5) and the median is about 29, with a wide spread (standard deviation about 13). Roughly a third of sentences run to 35 words or more, and about one in eight exceed 45. The longest reach 60 to 63 words. Each essay has 24 to 28 sentences.
- **Short sentences:** a few per essay, of 5 to 10 words, used for emphasis and nearly always as a question ("Is it any wonder...?") or a crisp summary after a long build-up (a four-word imperative inviting the reader to consider a feeling; a semicolon pair in which one thing changes and another does not). Never more than one short sentence in a row.
- **Fragments:** essentially none. Every sentence is grammatically complete. The only verbless constructions are a single "Perhaps not;" answering his own question, and the item lists that follow a dash.
- **Sentence openings:** hedges and frames dominate. Frequent openers are "It may be that...", "Perhaps...", "Arguably...", "I suspect...", "It seems to me...", "There is/are...", "Consider...", "Part of the answer...", "What/Why/How/Who...?", "Yet..." and "In my experience...". He often opens with the subject noun phrase followed by an interrupting qualifier set off by commas (the subject, then "in the end", then the verb; or the subject, then "like so many..."). He rarely starts with "And" or "But"; "Yet" is his contrastive opener.
- **Joining clauses:** the characteristic unit is a main clause followed by a semicolon and a second independent clause that extends or balances it. Other habits:
  - Triads and lists of three parallel phrases, often clauses beginning with the same word ("it shows us..., it shows us..., it shows us...", "they ask..., they distinguish..., they acknowledge...").
  - An em dash introducing a list of concrete examples or an appositive expansion (a general noun, a dash, then three concrete instances of it).
  - Balanced antithesis: "not X but Y", "less X than Y", "rather than", "as distinct from", "not only... but...".
  - Participial and relative trailing clauses (", in which...", ", which is to say...", ", since...").
  - A causal "since" and a concessive "however" (however plus an adjective plus "you may be") are both frequent.
- Inverted and interrupted syntax is common: comma-enclosed interjections such as "perhaps", "arguably", "in some sense", "in effect", "exactly", "then", "really" and "finally" sit mid-clause.

## 4. Paragraph size

- Always five paragraphs, each a single dense block of prose. There are no one-line paragraphs.
- Paragraphs run 120 to 179 words (about 5 sentences each, range 4 to 7). The first paragraph is usually the longest (150 to 175) and the last the shortest (120 to 141).
- No headings, subheadings, lists, bullet points, bold, italics, block quotes or footnotes anywhere in the body.

## 5. Punctuation

Rates per essay of about 750 words:

| Mark | Frequency | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comma | 32 to 57 (about 42) | Heavy use for interruption and parenthetical qualification. The serial (Oxford) comma is usually omitted (three parallel "our" phrases with "and" before the last and no comma before it). |
| Full stop | 20 to 24 | |
| Semicolon | 4 to 9 (about 6) | A signature mark: joins balanced independent clauses and separates items in a series of clauses. |
| Em dash (—) | 1 to 7 (about 4) | Always spaced (word — word). Used for list-of-examples expansions and appositive asides, sometimes in pairs. |
| Question mark | 2 to 4 (usually 3) | One in the opening sentence, one or two rhetorical questions in mid-essay, often one opening paragraph 4. |
| Double quotation marks | 0 to 4 | Straight double quotes used only to mention a word or phrase at a distance: a piece of jargon (a term such as legacy or blameless) or a short spoken admission. Never for dialogue or quoting sources. |
| Colon | 0 to 2 | Rare. Introduces an explanation or a summarising appositive, usually late in an essay. |
| Apostrophe | 0 to 3 | Possessives only (a person's, the team's, one another's). |
| Hyphen | 0 to 5 | Compounds only (half-formed, well-documented, so-called, precise-sounding, on-call). |

**Never used:** exclamation marks, parentheses, en dashes, ellipses, single quotation marks as quotes, slashes, ampersands, digits, bullet or number markers, emoji.

## 6. Register and vocabulary

- **Register:** formal-literary but conversational in rhythm; the essayistic English of a thoughtful columnist. Latinate abstract nouns sit alongside plain words. There is no slang, no swearing, no marketing language and no management jargon, except where he holds a term up for inspection in quotes.
- **Contractions:** none in his own voice. He writes do not, cannot, is not, it is. The single contraction in the whole corpus sits inside a quoted admission of ignorance.
- **Spelling:** British. Examples are organisation, recognise, realise, practise (verb), judgement, artefact, rigour, colours, paralyse and towards. "-ise" is used throughout, never "-ize".
- **Numbers:** always words, never digits, and even then rarely: he prefers vague quantifiers such as a few, several, a great many, very few, most of us, a considerable proportion, for a long time and not long ago. No percentages, dates, years, prices, durations in figures or statistics.
- **Hedging:** pervasive, and the defining lexical fingerprint. Approximate counts per essay:
  - "perhaps": about 3 (37 across 12 essays)
  - "I suspect": about 2
  - "arguably": 1 to 2
  - "it may be (that)": 1 to 2
  - "considerable" or "considerably": about 1 to 2
  - "it seems to me", usually with a following "that": usually once in paragraph 3 or 4
  - "in some sense": in over half of the essays
  - "in my experience", "in the end", "you might say/think": occasional

  He also softens adverbs and adjectives with quietly, subtly, faintly, gently, a little, rather, almost, oddly, curiously.
- **Recurring kinds of word:**
  - Words about institutions and social life: institution(al), culture, ritual, habit, inheritance, performance, theatre, fiction, pretence, community, colleagues, one another.
  - Emotional and experiential nouns: discomfort, anxiety, reluctance, humility, generosity, courage, curiosity, loneliness, fragility, relief, exposure.
  - Words about knowledge: uncertainty, understanding, context, assumptions, articulate, acknowledge, recognise, reveal, conceal.
  - Moral and evaluative words: honest, humane, generous, kind(ness), legitimate, genuine(ly), deserve, owe.
  - Intensifying "precisely" and "genuinely", evaluative "curious" and "peculiar", and "the difficulty is that...".
  - Technical vocabulary stays generic and minimal: codebase, dashboards, pager, alerts, build pipeline, ticketing system, deployment, escalation, incident, estimates, reviews. There are never code, commands, product names or acronyms.
- **People in the essays** are generic roles: the newcomer, the reviewer, the author, the engineer on call, the funders of the work, the organiser of a meeting, our predecessors or successors. They are never named.

## 7. Length

Every essay is 720 to 850 words of body text, typically about 740 to 770 (mean about 755). The body excludes the title/front-matter block.

## 8. Things this author never does

- Never uses headings, lists, bullets, tables, bold, italics, code, links or footnotes in an essay.
- Never writes more or fewer than five paragraphs, and never writes a one-sentence paragraph.
- Never uses contractions in his own voice.
- Never uses exclamation marks, parentheses, ellipses, en dashes or digits.
- Never uses American spellings.
- Never names a real person, company, product, tool, framework, language, book or author. He never cites research, statistics or sources (a vague nod to an existing literature is as specific as he gets).
- Never tells a specific personal anecdote with time, place or characters. His "I" reflects; it does not narrate.
- Never gives numbered steps, checklists, prescriptions or imperatives beyond the rhetorical "Consider...". Recommendations are always framed as "might", "perhaps" or "one possibility".
- Never blames individuals, mocks anyone, uses sarcasm, or takes a polemical, absolute or contrarian-for-effect position. He never says "always" or "never" about people without qualification.
- Never uses slang, hype words, buzzwords, humour, puns, pop-culture references or emoji.
- Never opens with a definition from a dictionary, a statistic, a quotation or a greeting. He never addresses the reader by name or as a group ("folks", "friends").
- Never ends with a call to action, a summary list, a question to the reader, or a sign-off.
- Never writes short punchy sentences in a run, and never writes sentence fragments for effect.
- Never discusses a technology's merits on technical grounds. The subject is always the human and institutional side of the practice.

## 9. Fourteen new essay subjects

These are in the same range of working life and do not overlap any existing essay.

1. The one-to-one: what the regular private conversation between a manager and a report is actually for.
2. Handing over: leaving a team and deciding what to pass on, and to whom.
3. The technical interview as a strange ritual of mutual performance between strangers.
4. On saying no: declining a reasonable request from a colleague you respect.
5. The backlog as a store of deferred promises that nobody quite intends to keep.
6. The difficulty of naming things, and what a name commits a team to.
7. The temptation of the rewrite: wanting to start again from nothing.
8. Working across time zones and the etiquette of a conversation that never happens at once.
9. The demo: showing unfinished work to people who control whether it continues.
10. On praise: how teams recognise contribution, and what goes unrecognised.
11. The career ladder and what a written promotion framework does to the work people choose.
12. The architecture diagram as a picture of how we wish the system worked.
13. Interruptions: the cost of being the person everyone can ask.
14. The long holiday: what a team learns about itself when a key person is away.
