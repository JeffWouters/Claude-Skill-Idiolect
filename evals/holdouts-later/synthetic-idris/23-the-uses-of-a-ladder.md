What is a career ladder really for? The official answer is that it makes progression fair and legible, replacing the private judgement of a single manager with a written account of what each level expects, so that people in one corner of an organisation are measured by the same standard as people in another. That answer is not wrong, and anyone who has watched promotions decided by proximity and charm will recognise what the written framework was meant to cure. Yet a document that describes what good work looks like does not stay a description for long; it becomes a map, and people begin to walk towards the places it marks. It may be that the ladder is less a record of how contribution is recognised than a quiet instruction about which contributions are worth making. I suspect most of us have felt this without quite saying it, and I suspect that these frameworks shape the work of a team considerably more than their authors ever intended.

Consider what happens when you are told, kindly and with every good intention, that the next level asks for evidence of influence beyond your own team. You begin to notice which pieces of work would produce that evidence — the cross-team proposal, the well-attended talk, the design document circulated widely — and which would not, however useful they might be. The patient work of fixing an unreliable build, answering a newcomer's questions or tidying a module nobody else will touch does not disappear from your week, but it begins to feel faintly like a private indulgence, something done at the expense of the case you are supposed to be assembling. Is it any wonder that the visible work gets chosen? Nobody has asked for this trade, and nobody would defend it if it were stated plainly; it simply follows from the fact that the framework can see some kinds of contribution and not others, and people are sensible enough to notice what is seen.

There is also the matter of the language in which these frameworks are written. Phrases such as "drives alignment" or "operates with ambiguity" are meant to be generous, broad enough to cover many kinds of excellence, and in some sense they are; yet their breadth means that they must be interpreted, and interpretation falls to the people in the calibration meetings, who reward whatever they find easiest to recognise. I do not wish to suggest that the remedy is to abandon the written ladder and return to the old arrangement of patronage and impression, which was arguably worse for precisely the people the frameworks were meant to protect. The framework gets something important right: it makes the terms of advancement discussable, and a thing that can be discussed can at least be argued with. The difficulty is that a standard written to be fair to everyone tends, perhaps inevitably, to become a standard that everyone performs towards, and a performance, however sincere you may be in giving it, is not quite the same as the work.

What would it mean to hold the ladder more lightly? It seems to me that the most useful change is not a better document but a different conversation around the one we already have — a manager who asks what work a person finds genuinely valuable before asking how it maps to the next level, a promotion case that is allowed to include the unglamorous and the unseen, a calibration discussion that names the kinds of contribution the framework habitually misses. None of this needs a new template or a revised rubric; it needs only the willingness to treat the framework as a guide to judgement rather than a substitute for it. Perhaps we might also be more honest with one another about the trade the ladder invites, saying aloud that some of the most valuable work on a team will never make a compelling case. This is harder than it sounds, since it asks managers to defend judgements they cannot point to in writing, and it is considerably less comfortable than simply matching evidence to a list.

The career ladder, like so many of the instruments by which organisations try to be fair, reveals as much about what they can measure as about what they value. There is something quietly moral in noticing the gap between the two, since every team depends on work that no framework describes — the colleague who remembers why a decision was made, the one who notices that a newcomer has gone silent, the one who keeps the old system running while everyone else builds the new one. We owe these people more than a line in a review. Perhaps the best we can do is to write our ladders with humility, read them with generosity and remember, when we climb them, that the rungs were never the whole of the wall.
