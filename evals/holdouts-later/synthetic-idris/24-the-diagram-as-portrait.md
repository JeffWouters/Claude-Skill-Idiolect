What is an architecture diagram really a picture of? The conventional answer is that it describes the system as it stands — its services, its stores of data and the lines along which requests travel — so that a newcomer can find their way and an experienced engineer can reason about a change before making it. Yet if you have ever compared such a diagram with the codebase it claims to depict, you will know that the two rarely agree, and that the disagreement is seldom a matter of carelessness; the boxes are tidier than the modules, the arrows run in fewer directions than the calls, and certain awkward components have simply been left out. It may be that the diagram survives, despite its inaccuracy, because accuracy was never quite its purpose. I suspect it is less a map than a portrait, and a portrait of a particular kind: the picture a team draws of the system it wishes it had built, or still means to build.

Consider the way such a diagram is usually made. Someone is asked to explain the system to a new colleague, a review board or the funders of the work, and they open a blank canvas and begin, quite naturally, with the parts they understand best and are most proud of; the clean separation between two services goes in first, drawn with care, while the shared table that both of them quietly write to is either omitted or reduced to a faint dotted line near the margin. None of this is dishonest in any ordinary sense, since the author is describing the design as it was intended and as, in some sense, it still exists in the minds of the people who hold it. Is it any wonder that the result looks calmer than the thing it represents? The diagram shows the system on a good day, from a flattering angle, with the untidy corners turned towards the wall, much as a family portrait once showed a household in its best clothes rather than in the ordinary disorder of a weekday morning.

There is also the matter of who the diagram is for, and what it is asked to do on their behalf. Within the team it serves as a shared statement of intent, a reminder of the shape everyone has agreed to move towards, and I do not wish to suggest that this is a small thing; a team that can draw its aspirations together has, arguably, already done some of the harder work of agreeing on them. The difficulty is that the same picture travels outward, into planning documents and slides for people who will never read the code, and there it is taken not as a hope but as a description. Decisions about staffing, about risk and about how long a change should take get made against the portrait rather than the sitter, and the engineers who know the difference find themselves in a curious position, since correcting the picture in public feels a little like confessing to a failure that nobody individually committed. Everyone privately understands that the diagram flatters; very few want to be the first to say so.

What might a more truthful diagram look like? It seems to me that it need not abandon the aspiration at all, only acknowledge it, perhaps by drawing two pictures rather than one — the system we intend, the system we have and the distance between them — and letting the distance be the most interesting part of the conversation. One could imagine marking the places where the design and the implementation have drifted apart, naming the shared table and the old synchronous call that nobody has yet found time to remove, and treating those marks not as embarrassments but as the honest record of a team that has been busy doing other necessary things. You might think of this as annotating the portrait rather than repainting it, and it requires no new notation or better drawing tool; it requires only a willingness to let the picture be a little less beautiful. That willingness has a cost, since an accurate diagram is harder to present, slower to agree on and considerably less comfortable to show to the people who decide whether the work continues.

The architecture diagram, like so many of the artefacts of working life, carries our hopes as faithfully as it carries our knowledge, and there is nothing shameful in that. We draw what we meant, what we still mean and what we would like others to believe of us, and the lines on the canvas are as much a promise as a record. Perhaps the deepest difficulty is not that the picture is idealised but that we forget, after a while, which parts of it were wishes. A diagram that admits its own aspirations is, I suspect, a more useful thing than one that hides them, since it tells a newcomer both where the system is and where its keepers hoped it would go. Perhaps the kindest thing we can offer one another, and our successors, is a portrait honest enough to include the sitter's flaws alongside the sitter's best intentions.
