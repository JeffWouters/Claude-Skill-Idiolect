What is it, exactly, that we want when we say that a system ought to be rewritten from nothing? The official answer is technical and usually sincere: the codebase has grown tangled, the assumptions it was built on no longer hold and every change now costs considerably more than it should. Most of us have made that argument, and most of us have believed it while we made it. Yet the wish to start again seems to arrive long before the evidence for it, and it survives a great many attempts that end, after a long and tiring effort, in a new system with much the same difficulties as the old one. It may be that the rewrite is less a plan than a feeling, and that the feeling serves a purpose the plan does not admit. I suspect that what we are really asking for is not a better system but a clean conscience, a place in which none of the old compromises are yet our fault.

Consider the experience of opening an old module for the first time in a long while. The names are not quite the names you would choose; the structure reflects a set of priorities that nobody on the team can now remember agreeing to; the comments describe behaviour that the code no longer performs. Every oddity is, in some sense, a small reproach, and the reproaches accumulate until the whole thing seems less like a tool than like a record of other people's hurried decisions. Is it any wonder that the blank page begins to look like relief? A new system has no history — no awkward exceptions, no forgotten workarounds, no customers who depend on its peculiarities — and it is, for a little while, entirely comprehensible, which is a pleasure that working engineers are perhaps rarely allowed to feel about anything that is actually in use.

There is also the matter of what a rewrite does for the people who propose it. To maintain an old system is to be a custodian, and custodial work is quiet, largely invisible and difficult to describe in a promotion case; to rebuild one is to be an author, with a beginning, a launch and a name attached to the result. I do not wish to suggest that engineers argue for rewrites out of vanity, since the institutions they work in have, arguably, taught them precisely which kind of work gets noticed. The familiar warning against rewrites is correct as far as it goes, since old systems hold a great deal of knowledge that nobody ever wrote down and a fresh start quietly discards. Yet the warning and the wish share a curious blind spot: both treat the question as a technical one, when a considerable part of it concerns recognition, fatigue and the desire to feel that one's work belongs to oneself.

What might it mean to take the wish seriously without simply obeying it? It seems to me that a more honest conversation might begin by naming what the rewrite is meant to relieve — the embarrassment of a confusing design, the weariness of long maintenance, the suspicion that careful repair will go unnoticed. Some of those burdens could be eased without discarding anything; a team might, for instance, set aside time to rename, to delete and to explain, and might treat the patient improvement of an inherited system as work worth describing in the same terms as a launch. None of this requires a new methodology or a new set of tools, only a willingness to look at the old system as something we are responsible for rather than something that happened to us. It is slower and considerably less exciting, and it offers none of the clean satisfaction of a fresh beginning; I suspect that is precisely why it is so rarely proposed.

The rewrite, like so many ambitions of working life, is in some sense a hope about ourselves rather than about the system. We imagine that on the other side of it we will be more careful and more deliberate than the people who came before us, and we are usually wrong in the gentlest possible way, since the same deadlines and the same partial knowledge will be waiting for us too. There is something quietly humbling in recognising this — the old code was written by people much like us, under conditions much like ours, doing what they could. Perhaps the real temptation is not to start again but to believe that starting again would make us different people. Perhaps the braver thing, and the kinder one, is to stay with what we have inherited and leave it a little better than we found it.
