What is a demo really for? The official answer is that it lets the people who fund a piece of work see what their money has bought, so that they can decide, with some evidence in front of them, whether it deserves to continue. That answer is not wrong, and I do not wish to suggest that the funders of the work have no legitimate interest in seeing it; yet those of us who have stood in front of a room with half-formed software and a nervous smile know that the occasion is rarely a neutral inspection. It is closer to an audition, in which the thing being judged is as much the team as the work, and in which everyone present understands, without quite saying so, that what is shown has been arranged. I suspect the demo survives less because it informs a decision than because it gives an anxious institution a ritual through which to express confidence, or to withdraw it, without having to articulate either.

Consider the experience of preparing one. In the days beforehand the team quietly narrows its ambitions to what will survive being watched — a single path through the system, a set of data chosen because it behaves, a screen that loads quickly because nothing behind it is yet real. None of this is dishonest in any simple sense, since unfinished work cannot be shown in any other way; everyone in the room knows that a demonstration is a demonstration rather than a release. Yet the team spends a considerable part of its week rehearsing a performance rather than finishing the thing being performed, and the person at the keyboard carries a peculiar kind of exposure, as though each click might reveal not merely a defect but some failing of character. Is it any wonder that people learn to steer, almost without noticing, around the parts of the system they are least sure of? The demo, in some sense, teaches us to hide precisely the places where we most need help.

There is also the matter of what the audience is really judging. You may have noticed that the questions asked afterwards are rarely about the engineering at all. The funders of the work, however well-intentioned they may be, are usually too far from it to evaluate what lies underneath, and so they judge what they can see, as most of us would: the polish of the interface, the fluency of the presenter, the absence of awkward pauses. It seems to me that this is not a failing in them but a consequence of the format, which asks people with limited context to make large decisions in a short time, and which therefore rewards the appearance of progress over its substance. I do not wish to dismiss the standard remedy, which is to show real work early and often; there is genuine wisdom in it, since a team that demonstrates something every few weeks has less to hide and fewer surprises to explain. Yet frequency alone does not change what the occasion is for, and a team can hold a great many demos without ever showing anything that might cause alarm.

What might a more truthful demo look like? One possibility is that the team shows not only what works but what does not, and says plainly where it is uncertain — the part that is slower than it should be, the assumption nobody has yet tested, the question it cannot answer without help from the people in the room. Another is that the audience might ask, gently, what the team is worried about, and treat the answer as the most useful thing it will hear all afternoon. None of this requires a new process or a better template; it requires only that both sides agree, in effect, to treat the demo as a conversation between colleagues rather than a verdict delivered by one group upon another. It will be less comfortable, and if we are honest it will sometimes look worse, since an account of unfinished work that tells the truth contains more doubt than a rehearsed one. I suspect it would also make our decisions better.

The demo, like so many of the rituals of working life, is a way of managing trust between people who cannot see one another's work directly. There is something quietly moral in how we conduct it; it asks those who show to risk embarrassment, and those who watch to withhold judgement long enough to understand what they are seeing. When it goes well, it is less an inspection than an act of mutual faith, in which a team admits that the thing is not yet what it will be and the people who fund it choose to believe that it might become so. Perhaps that is what a demo is really for: not to prove that the work is finished, but to give everyone in the room a small and shared occasion for courage.
