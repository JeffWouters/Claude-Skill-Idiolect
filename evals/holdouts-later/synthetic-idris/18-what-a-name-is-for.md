Why do we find it so hard to name things? Most of us who have worked on software for any length of time will have sat through a conversation in which a group of capable people, faced with a new service, a new table or a new concept in the domain, spent considerably longer choosing what to call it than they had spent deciding what it should do. The conventional explanation is that naming is a technical matter of precision, and that a good name is simply one that describes its referent accurately enough for a stranger to guess what it holds. That explanation is correct as far as it goes; yet it does not quite account for the heat the question generates, or for the curious reluctance we feel about settling it. It may be that a name is less a description than a promise, a small public statement about what the thing is and, just as importantly, what it will never be allowed to become. I suspect that what we are really negotiating, when we argue about a name, is the future.

Consider what happens in the moment after a name has been agreed. For a little while it feels provisional, a placeholder that everyone privately assumes will, perhaps, be revisited once the shape of the work is clearer; then it appears in a ticket, on a dashboard and in a conversation with another team, and before long it has quietly hardened into something nobody quite remembers choosing. The name begins to do work of its own — it tells the newcomer where to look, it tells the reviewer what belongs and what does not, it tells the funders of the work what they are paying for. Is it any wonder that we hesitate? A team that calls something a scheduler has, in some sense, already decided that it will not also send messages, and a team that calls it a platform has invited every neighbouring group to bring it their problems. I suspect the hesitation, seen this way, is not indecision so much as an honest recognition that we are about to commit ourselves in public.

There is also the question of who gets to name things, and what that right quietly confers. The person who proposes the name that sticks acquires a small but durable authorship over the concept, since every later conversation will take place in the vocabulary they chose; the person whose suggestion was set aside must now speak, for as long as the thing exists, in a colleague's language. I do not wish to suggest that this is a contest most of us consciously enter, and yet the faint discomfort of these discussions arguably owes something to it. It is tempting to conclude that the remedy is a naming convention, a written guideline that removes judgement from the matter altogether, and such conventions do genuine good; they spare us a great many small arguments and make a codebase easier for strangers to read. The difficulty is that a convention can tell us how to spell a name but not what the thing is for, which is precisely the question the argument was trying, clumsily, to settle.

What might it look like to name things more honestly? It seems to me that the most useful change is to treat the argument over a name as a legitimate conversation about scope rather than as a distraction from the real work, and to let it run a little longer than feels comfortable. We might ask, when a name is proposed, what it would forbid as well as what it describes, and whether everyone in the room would recognise the same boundary. We could, perhaps, be more willing to rename things when they have genuinely changed, accepting the considerable cost of the change as the price of a vocabulary that still tells the truth; the old name, after all, is not a monument but a tool. None of this requires a new process or a new tool, only a shift in how we regard a discussion that we are accustomed to treating as faintly embarrassing. It will make some meetings slower, and it will occasionally force a team to admit that it does not yet agree about what it is building.

There is something quietly humbling in the recognition that the words we choose outlast our intentions for them. A name, like so much else in working life, is an inheritance we hand to people we will never meet — the engineer on call who searches for it late at night, the successor who must decide whether it still means what it says, the newcomer who learns our system first through its vocabulary. Arguably the kindest thing we can do for them is to treat naming as an act of care rather than of cleverness. Perhaps a good name is not the one that wins the argument but the one that keeps faith, long after we have gone, with the promise it was chosen to make.
