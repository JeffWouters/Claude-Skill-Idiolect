What is a handover really for? The official answer is simple enough: when someone leaves a team, the knowledge that lived in their head must be moved somewhere else before they go, and so we arrange a few meetings, write a document with a hopeful title and transfer whatever systems still bear their name. Yet anyone who has sat through one of these conversations, on either side of the table, knows that very little of what matters changes hands in them. The person leaving is tired, a little guilty and already half elsewhere; the person receiving does not yet know which questions to ask, since the questions only appear much later, at inconvenient hours, in front of a failing deployment. It may be that the handover survives less as a transfer of knowledge than as a ritual of departure, a way for a team to mark that someone is going and that the work will somehow continue. I suspect that is not a lesser purpose, and that it deserves more seriousness than we usually give it.

Consider the experience of the one who is leaving. They sit down with a blank page and try to write out everything they know, and discover almost at once that most of it cannot be written, since it is not really a set of facts but a feel for the system — which alerts can safely wait until morning, which colleague in another team will answer a message kindly, which part of the codebase is fragile in ways no test will reveal. What ends up on the page is the part that was easiest to articulate, which is rarely the part that was most valuable. Who among us has not opened a colleague's parting document, full of care and good intentions, and found that it answers every question except the one we have? There is no failure of effort here; the difficulty is that the knowledge was never held as knowledge at all, but as habit, attention and a long accumulation of small judgements.

There is also the question of to whom a handover is made, which is often decided less by thought than by availability. The work tends to pass to whoever has the most room in their week or the least ability to refuse, and that person inherits not only the systems but the half-finished promises and unspoken expectations of everyone who used to rely on someone else. I do not wish to suggest that the usual remedies are wrong; a written runbook, a list of owned services and a few recorded walkthroughs are genuinely better than nothing, and a team that insists on them is behaving responsibly. Yet they rest on an assumption that knowledge is a parcel, something that can be wrapped by one person and unwrapped by another, when in practice it behaves rather more like a relationship, which must be built slowly and in some sense cannot be delegated at all. I suspect most of us know this privately; we simply lack a format that admits it.

What might a more honest handover look like? It seems to me that it would begin by acknowledging what cannot be passed on, and by spending less effort on completeness and more on introductions — to the people who know the history, to the customers who will notice first, to the corners of the system where the departing engineer was always a little afraid. It might mean choosing the recipient deliberately, perhaps even choosing several, so that understanding is spread across the team rather than settled on a single new pair of shoulders. It might also mean that the person leaving remains reachable for a while, not as an obligation but as an ordinary courtesy between colleagues. None of this requires a new template or a new tool, only a willingness to treat the handover as the start of a slower process rather than an event that finishes on a particular afternoon. It is less tidy, however careful you may be; it asks the one leaving to admit how much they carried without ever saying so.

There is something quietly moral in the act of leaving well. A handover, like so many of the small institutions of working life, is arguably a way of expressing what we owe to people we will no longer see every day, and what they, in turn, owe to the work we built together. We cannot give our successors everything we knew, and a document, however long, will not do it for us. What we can give them is a sense that they are not alone with what they have inherited, and that the gaps they find were left not through carelessness but through the ordinary limits of what one person can say to another. Perhaps the best handover is less a transfer of ownership than a gesture of hospitality, offered by someone already standing in the doorway.
