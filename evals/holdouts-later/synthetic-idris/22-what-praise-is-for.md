What is praise at work really for? The official answer is that it rewards good work and encourages more of it, and most of the machinery we have built around it — the thank-you channel, the quarterly award, the round of applause at the end of a launch — assumes that recognition is a kind of payment, handed out in proportion to effort and received with gratitude. Yet anyone who has spent a few years in a team will know that praise rarely behaves like payment; it moves unevenly, it gathers around certain kinds of work and certain kinds of people, and it tells us at least as much about what an organisation can see as about what it values. It may be that praise survives in its familiar forms less because it motivates anyone than because it lets a group describe to itself what it believes good work looks like. I suspect that the most revealing thing about the way a team praises is not what it says but what it quietly leaves out.

Consider what happens at the end of a difficult piece of work, when someone stands up to thank the people who made it possible. The names that come most readily are those attached to the visible moments — the late fix that saved the release, the confident presentation to the funders of the work, the feature that customers noticed and wrote in to say they liked. These are genuine contributions, and nobody would wish to take anything away from them. Yet somewhere behind them sits a different kind of labour: the colleague who patiently answered the newcomer's questions for weeks, the reviewer whose careful comments prevented a problem that therefore never happened, the engineer who tidied the build pipeline so that everyone else could move a little faster. Is it any wonder that people learn, over time, which kinds of effort get noticed? Nobody decides that the quieter work matters less; it simply has no moment at which it can be seen, and so it slips out of the story the team tells about itself.

There is also the matter of what praise does to the person who receives it, which is considerably more complicated than we tend to admit. In my experience, public recognition carries a faint discomfort for many engineers, since it singles them out from colleagues who worked just as hard and places them, however briefly, in a position they did not ask for; you may have felt this yourself, standing at the front of a room while someone describes your work as rather more heroic than it felt. The standard remedy is to praise more often, and there is something right in this; a culture in which thanks are frequent is almost certainly kinder than one in which they are rare. The difficulty is that frequency alone can turn praise into a kind of weather, pleasant and oddly weightless, in which everyone is "amazing" and nobody feels particularly seen. Arguably, praise that costs the giver no attention is not quite praise; it is a gesture of goodwill, which has its own value but cannot do the work of genuine recognition.

What might a more truthful practice of praise look like? It seems to me that it begins less with generosity than with attention, with the slow and slightly unglamorous habit of noticing what our colleagues actually did and saying so in terms precise enough to show that we noticed. One possibility is simply to describe rather than to applaud, to tell someone that their patience with a confused stakeholder kept a difficult conversation from going wrong, rather than that they are wonderful to work with. You might think of this as testimony rather than applause. Another might be to make a point of recognising the work whose success consists in nothing happening, the outage that did not occur and the misunderstanding that was headed off before it began. None of this requires a new award or a new tool; it requires only that we look more carefully, which is harder than it sounds, since it means admitting how much of the work around us we had not been watching.

Praise, like so many of the small rituals of working life, is in some sense a mirror in which a team can see what it has learned to value. There is something quietly moral in the question of whose effort we notice, because every organisation depends on a great deal of labour that will never be mentioned in a launch announcement, and the people who do that labour know it perfectly well. They do not usually ask to be applauded; they ask, perhaps, only to be understood. Perhaps the truest form of recognition is not the loud thanks at the end of a project but the ordinary attentiveness of colleagues who can see what one another carry, and who take the trouble, now and then, to say that they have seen it.
