What does it mean to decline a request that is entirely reasonable, made by a colleague whose judgement we trust and whose work we admire? Most of the advice on the subject treats refusal as a problem of assertiveness, as though the difficulty lay chiefly in finding the nerve to say the word and the phrasing with which to soften it. That advice is not wrong, and a considerable number of us could stand to hear it; yet it seems to address the easier case, in which the request is unwelcome and the person making it is someone we would rather not oblige in any event. The harder case is the one in which we would genuinely like to help, in which the request is sensible and the colleague deserves a yes, and in which we decline anyway because the hours are already spoken for. I suspect that what makes this kind of refusal so uncomfortable is not the loss of the task but the small injury it seems to do to a relationship we value.

Consider the experience of being asked, late in the afternoon, whether you might look over a colleague's design before it goes to the wider group. The request is modest, the colleague is someone who has helped you more times than you could easily count, and the review would, in some sense, be a pleasure; yet you already know that the evening is promised to work you have been deferring for several days. There follows a curious interior negotiation — a rehearsal of reasons, a calculation of how much sleep might be spared, a search for some arrangement in which nothing has to be refused at all. Is it any wonder that so many of us simply say yes and then do both things badly? The yes costs nothing at the moment it is given; the cost arrives later, spread thinly across the review, the deferred work and the person who has to live with the consequences of both, who is very often ourselves.

There are also institutional reasons why refusal feels more dangerous than it is. Most organisations speak warmly of focus and prioritisation, and most of them reward, in practice, the person who is visibly available, whose name appears on every thread and who can be relied upon never to say that something is beyond them. Helpfulness becomes a kind of reputation, and a reputation, once earned, is something we are perhaps reluctant to spend. I do not wish to suggest that this culture of mutual obligation is a bad one; a team in which people readily help one another is precisely the kind of team most of us would choose to work in, and the instinct to say yes is, arguably, one of its healthiest features. The difficulty is that a culture which prizes availability has no graceful way of honouring its limits, and so each of us is left to discover those limits privately, usually by exceeding them.

What might it look like to say no in a way that honours the colleague rather than merely protecting ourselves? It seems to me that the most generous refusals share a few quiet features: they are given early rather than after a long silence, they explain the competing commitment rather than gesturing vaguely at being busy, and they offer something smaller in place of the whole — a later hour, a partial reading, the name of someone better placed to help. None of this requires a policy or a new way of tracking work; it asks only that we treat the refusal as information our colleague is entitled to, rather than as a verdict on them. It is, admittedly, harder than saying yes, since it requires us to admit in plain words that our time is finite and that we have chosen, however reluctantly, to spend it elsewhere. That admission can feel faintly exposing, and the colleague may well be disappointed; yet disappointment honestly delivered tends to heal far more quickly than a promise quietly broken.

There is something to be said, finally, for the trust that a well-made refusal can build. A colleague who has heard us say no, and who has seen that our reasons were real and our regret was genuine, learns that our yes means something; the word acquires a weight it never had when it was given to everyone and everything. In this sense saying no is less a withdrawal from a relationship than a contribution to it, since it tells the other person where we actually stand and lets them plan accordingly. Perhaps the deepest courtesy we can offer one another at work is not endless availability but an honest account of what we can carry, given kindly and in good time, so that the help we do give is help that can be relied upon.
