What is the one-to-one really for? Most organisations describe it in the language of process — a regular private conversation between a manager and a report, held at a fixed interval, in which progress is reviewed, obstacles are raised and development is discussed — and the templates that circulate for it tend to confirm that description, with their neat headings for goals, blockers and feedback. Yet anyone who has sat through a great many of these conversations, on either side of the table, knows that the agenda is rarely the point; the most useful ones often abandon it within the first few minutes. It may be that the one-to-one survives, in organisations that have cheerfully abolished so many other rituals, because it quietly serves a purpose that nobody quite wishes to write down. I suspect that purpose has less to do with the exchange of information than with the slow and uncertain business of learning whether we can trust one another.

Consider the experience of the report in the early weeks of such an arrangement. The meeting arrives on the calendar with a certain official weight, and the natural response is to prepare for it as one might prepare for an inspection, as you may remember doing yourself — a tidy account of what has been done, a modest list of difficulties, a question or two that shows engagement without revealing too much. The manager, for their part, is often doing something similar, I suspect, performing attentiveness and openness while privately wondering how much of what they hear is true. Is it any wonder that so many of these conversations settle into a comfortable exchange of status, since status is safe and both parties can recite it without exposure? The real conversation, if it ever happens at all, tends to arrive late and sideways; it begins with a hesitation near the end of the half hour, a remark offered almost as an afterthought, and the question of whether the listener will pick it up.

There is also the matter of power, which the format tends to acknowledge only obliquely. However kind the manager may be, and however informal the setting, the person across the table has some say over the report's pay, reputation and future, and both of them know it. The standard advice — that managers should listen more than they speak, that the time belongs to the report, that the agenda should be theirs to set — is correct as far as it goes, and I do not wish to dismiss it; it has made a great many of these meetings considerably more humane than they once were. The difficulty is that no rule about who speaks first can dissolve an asymmetry that exists precisely because of the relationship itself. Arguably the most honest one-to-ones are those in which the imbalance is named rather than concealed, in which the manager admits, in some sense, that they are asking for candour from someone who has good reason to withhold it.

What might it mean to approach the one-to-one differently? It seems to me that the most valuable thing a manager can bring to it is not a better template but a willingness to be surprised, to treat the conversation as a place where their own assumptions about the work, the team and the person in front of them might be quietly corrected. This might mean, perhaps, tolerating silences that feel faintly awkward, or returning to a remark made weeks earlier to show that it was genuinely heard, or admitting uncertainty about a decision rather than defending it. None of this requires new tools or an elaborate process; it requires patience, and a certain readiness to hear things that are uncomfortable to hear. It is also slower, since trust of this kind accumulates only through many small occasions on which something risky was said and nothing bad followed, and there is no way to hurry it.

The one-to-one, in the end, is one of the rare occasions in working life when an institution sets aside time for a single relationship and asks nothing of it except that it continue. Like so many of our rituals, it is easy to judge by its outputs — the actions recorded, the concerns escalated, the goals revised — and to conclude, on a busy week, that it can safely be cancelled. Yet what it produces is harder to see and slower to appear; it is the knowledge, perhaps, that there is somewhere to bring the thing you are not yet sure how to say. Perhaps the regular private conversation is less a management technique than a standing promise, renewed each time it is kept, that we are willing to listen to one another before we are certain what we will hear.
