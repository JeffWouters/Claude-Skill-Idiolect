What are we really hoping to learn when we gather after an incident to discuss what went wrong? The official answer is usually expressed in terms of causes and remediation — understanding the sequence of events, identifying contributing factors, agreeing on actions that will prevent a recurrence — and this answer is not wrong, though it is arguably incomplete. A postmortem is also a social occasion, perhaps one of the more emotionally charged occasions in the life of a technical team, since it involves revisiting a period of stress and confusion in front of colleagues, some of whom may have made decisions that contributed to the trouble. It may be that the quality of what is learned depends less on the rigour of the analysis than on the atmosphere in which it takes place; people who feel safe will describe what actually happened, while people who feel judged will describe what they wish had happened. I suspect most experienced teams understand this, even if their templates do not reflect it.

The movement towards so-called blameless postmortems represents a considerable advance in this respect, and I would not wish to diminish it. The recognition that incidents arise from systems rather than from individual failings, that people generally act reasonably given the information available to them, and that punishing mistakes merely teaches people to conceal them — these are hard-won insights, and teams that have absorbed them are markedly better at learning from failure. Yet the word "blameless" can itself become a kind of ritual, invoked at the start of the meeting and then quietly forgotten as the discussion proceeds. Is it possible to declare an absence of blame while the room is still full of it? You may have attended postmortems in which the formal language was impeccably neutral while the glances across the table told a rather different story.
