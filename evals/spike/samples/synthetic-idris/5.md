There is a moment, familiar to anyone who has joined a team partway through its history, when you open a codebase for the first time and realise that it was written by people who are no longer there to explain it. The experience is not unlike entering a house whose previous occupants left in a hurry: the furniture is arranged according to a logic you cannot quite reconstruct, certain rooms appear to have been abandoned, and there are fixtures whose purpose remains obscure however long you study them. We tend to call such systems "legacy", a word that carries a faint note of disapproval, as though the inheritance were a burden rather than a gift. It may be that the word itself shapes our attitude more than we realise; it invites us to see the previous authors as obstacles rather than as colleagues separated from us only by time. I suspect that a different vocabulary might produce a rather different kind of engineering.

Why is it so tempting to assume that the people who came before us were careless? Part of the answer, perhaps, is that we see only the results of their decisions and not the circumstances in which those decisions were made — the deadlines that pressed upon them, the constraints they were working around, the knowledge they did not yet have and could not have had. A piece of code that appears absurd in isolation often makes considerable sense once its history is understood; the strange workaround was a response to a defect in a library that has since been fixed, the duplicated logic reflects a period when two teams were not speaking to one another, the unusual structure preserves compatibility with a client that nobody now remembers. None of this is visible in the code itself, which records the decision while silently discarding the reasoning. The consequence is that we judge our predecessors against a standard of knowledge they did not possess, and we find them, predictably, wanting.
