Today, though, with cheap printing and poor-quality paper, and with a central library receiving the yearly flood of new publications, these small old libraries no longer serve any purpose except as storage. They belong to a time when books were scarce and costly, when few people could build a library of their own, when lecturers gathered knowledge that was not available to the wider world, when notes were painstakingly copied out and passed along, and when part of the pleasure of learning was knowing that you held secrets other people did not. A Dean of Christ Church in earlier times is said to have offered three reasons for studying Greek. First, it let you read the Saviour's words in the original language. Second, it gave you a proper contempt for people who didn't know it. Third, it led to well-paid positions. What a rich flavour this verdict has! The first reason is probably mistaken, the second is un-Christian, and the third is a crude motive that would apply just as well to any professional training at all.

Nowadays, knowing Greek no longer has the same obvious commercial value, except for schoolmasters and clergymen. Knowledge is more widely spread and easier to get hold of. It is no longer seen as a secret, precious, and somewhat awe-inspiring possession, and the person who has it is no longer respected and admired. On the contrary, a learned man is more likely to be thought tedious. Old folio volumes have become little more than props for the illustrators of sensational novels. Everyone knows the ridiculous old man with silky white hair, a velvet skullcap, and a dignified air, who sits reading a folio at an oak table and turns out to be the villain of the story, full of secret and unsuccessful wickedness. In real life, however, nobody reads a folio any more, because anything worth reprinting, along with plenty that isn't, has been reissued in a convenient format, if not in England then at least in Germany.
