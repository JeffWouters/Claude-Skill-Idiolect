"Well, I didn't think any more about it at the time, although I often walked in the field. It was a pleasant, quiet spot, full of thickets, as I mentioned, where the birds could nest undisturbed. There was usually a goldfinch's nest there.

"At one point a drain had to be laid across the field, so a large trench was dug. One day the workmen came to tell me they had found something and asked if I would come and look. I went out and saw that they had dug up a large Roman funeral urn holding some burnt bones. I informed the lord of the manor, a squire from the neighbouring parish, and from then on the two of us kept an eye on the work. We found another urn, and then another, both full of bones, and after that a large glass vessel that also held bones. The squire became interested and eventually had the whole area excavated. We uncovered a large enclosure that had once been surrounded by a stone wall, and you can still see what's left of it. In two of the corners there were huge deposits of wood ash in deep pits, as if great fires had burned there, and the walls in those corners were scorched and blackened with smoke. Altogether we found fifty or sixty urns, all filled with bones. In another corner there was a deep shaft cut into the chalk like a well, with handholds down the sides, and that was full of burnt bones too. We also turned up a few coins, and in one spot a lump of rust that might once have been a pile of tools or weapons. We brought in the antiquarians, and they concluded that it was what's called a Roman ustrinum, a public crematorium where people who couldn't afford a private funeral could bring a body to be burned. If they had nowhere to put the urn containing the bones, it seems they were allowed to bury it on the site until they chose to collect it. There was a large Roman settlement here, you know. There was a fort on that hill, and the remains of several big Roman villas have been found nearby. This place must have been fairly isolated, some way from the town, probably in the forest that covered the whole county back then. But isn't it curious," the vicar said, "that the tradition of its being an unlucky place should have survived all these centuries, long after anyone remembered what it was actually used for?"
