Yet this great thing is so close to us that we could take hold of it just by reaching out a hand; it is as near to us as the air and the sunlight. Even so, the well-meaning but misguided efforts of people who understood how precious it was, often the best and most high-minded people, have guarded it so closely, wrapped it up so thoroughly, and set it so far apart from ordinary life and thought that many who live by its light, and take it in as naturally as the air they breathe, never realise that they have come anywhere near it. A simple Methodist, once asked about a friend, replied, "Is he a good man? Yes, he is good, but not religious-good." He meant that his friend lived kindly, purely, and unselfishly, as a Christian should, but did not belong to any particular church. So he could not be credited with any religious motive for what he did, and was supposedly guided only by a worthless instinct, a mere preference for living unworldly.

If there was ever a divine attempt in this world to free religion from its wrappings, it was the preaching of Christ. As far as we can tell from records whose origins are obscure and mysterious, apparently written versions of oral tradition, Christ set out to put religion within reach of the humblest and simplest people. People may doubt whether these records are literally accurate about events, and it may be argued that the way incidents were reported was shaped by the popular belief of the time in miracles. Still, Christ's words and sayings stand out from the narrative. In places they seem to have been only partly understood, yet they contain and express ideas far beyond the reach of the minds that wrote them down. This gives them a real authenticity. In fact, the limited understanding of the people who compiled the records confirms that authenticity, in a way that would not have been possible if the compilers had clearly been sharp, sophisticated thinkers with broad philosophical insight.
