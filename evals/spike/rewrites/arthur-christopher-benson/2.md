The sense of sin, then, is to some extent artificial. It seems to be made up of two parts. One is a deep, divine instinct that holds the soul accountable for actions that may be trivial in themselves but seem to carry a sinful quality. The other is a conventional instinct that treats certain things as abominable, even though they are not necessarily sinful in themselves, simply because society is in the habit of regarding them that way.

For the philosopher, the whole question takes on a darker shade when he considers two further points. First, the evil impulses that it is sinful to give in to were deliberately placed in human beings by their Creator, or at least have not visibly been removed. Second, many people whose lives have been darkened, embittered, and ruined by sin have brought that misery on themselves by giving in to inherited tendencies that are, in practice, almost impossible to resist.

So in modern times, when reason and science together have cleared away the darkness of superstition, made miracles seem less and less likely, and laughed unscientific occultism off the field, what room is left for the priest?

There is no room for him if, somewhere deep in his mind, he still harbours any trace of the temptation to serve his own interests, or to raise up himself or his profession, by exploiting the fears of irrational and gullible people. The true priest must stand against that kind of priestcraft, alongside the scientist, the statesman, and the doctor. Everyone who loves freedom and loves God must unite against all personal and priestly domination. Those who practise it commit the sin of Simon Magus, of Hophni, and of Caiaphas: the sin of wanting people to remain in bondage so that they themselves can gain worship and honour. It is the most deadly and contemptible tyranny in the world.
