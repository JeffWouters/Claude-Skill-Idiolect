All you need is a small brass radiator key. It only costs a few euros, but it's the one tool I always manage to lose, so I now keep three of them: one on a hook in the boiler cupboard, one in the kitchen drawer, and one in the toolbox. That way I can usually find at least one.

To start, I switch the heating on and let it run for about 20 minutes until the radiators are hot. Then I check each one by running my palm flat from the top down to the bottom. If a radiator has air trapped in it, the bottom will be hot and the top will be cold, and you can feel the point where the warmth stops. On the radiator in the back bedroom, that line is about halfway down.

Next, I turn the heating off. People often skip this step, but if you bleed a radiator while the pump is running, you can draw more air into the system, or end up with a spray of hot, black water in your face. I've experienced both.

I wait ten minutes for things to settle and make a coffee.

Then I work my way up through the house, starting at the bottom: downstairs first, then the landing, and finally the bedrooms. Because air rises, starting at the top means air from the lower floors will just move up behind you.

I follow the same routine at each radiator.

I place an old cloth underneath the valve, which is a small square nut at one of the top corners. I fit the key, wrap a second cloth around it, and slowly turn it a quarter turn anticlockwise.

You'll hear a hiss as the air escapes, smelling of metal and stale water. It can last a while; on the back bedroom radiator, it went on for nearly a minute.
