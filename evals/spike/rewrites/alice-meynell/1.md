I won't ask the scholars whether Blake meant these two words to go together or to stand in opposition. In the art of literature, at least, they are certainly inseparable. For every poet, the songs of Innocence and Experience come from their own individual heart and life. To borrow the accumulated experience of other people and use it in place of one's own fresh thought, something we would hardly do even to manage the most routine parts of daily life, is to give up both Innocence and Experience at the same time. Experience, clearly, can only ever be personal and individual. And a rare, solitary kind of Innocence belongs to the person who does not reach into other people's histories and does not back up his own words with other people's summaries and conclusions. That is why I treat Innocence and Experience as one thing, a sign of the necessary and noble separateness of each person from every other, of each person's uniqueness.

Still, if I were willing to give up that personal separateness and use what belongs to others, I think I would rather take their future than their past. If I must borrow, let me take on their hopes and the colours of their confidence. I don't mean that I would weigh down my imagination with ambitions I hadn't earned, but even that would be easier to bear than filling my memory with a history I had no right to.

Yet some writers of a particular kind of love poetry see the matter very differently. These poets have no qualms about adopting the past of a crowd of people they have never even met. Their verse is packed with ready-made memories, varied, numerous, and cruel. No single life, assuming it is a full life concerned with more than sex, could supply that much experience, disillusionment, and betrayal. To achieve that tone completely, you would have to claim as your own the past of Alfred de Musset, say, and of the men who helped him not to live but to have lived. You would need to have lived far more than any one person does, and to pool your erotic memories with poets of every kind.
