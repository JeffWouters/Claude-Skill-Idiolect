So consider how promising the outlook actually is. Any individual actor may fail, and indeed someone must. Married couples have always been told that when two people ride one horse, one of them has to sit behind. Likewise, when two people are speaking slowly, one of them has to be the slower. Relative success always means relative failure for someone else. But whenever a particular actor or actress fails, the great cause of slowness clearly benefits, and the record moves forward. Nonsense: the word "forward" slipped out without thinking. It is hard to keep in mind what a foolish theatrical majesty one is critiquing here, and how every word ought to move backwards, without exception, out of respect for this mock throne.

Not long ago, the leading stage in London hosted the most significant event in the history of donkey racing up to that opening night. A famous tragedian and a well-known supporting actor performed a scene together in "The Dead Heart." Nobody who heard it could possibly have forgotten it yet. The two men echoed each other's delivery and then tried to outlast each other's pauses. The contest was so determined, so unrelenting, so deadly and so entrenched that you could have fallen asleep between exchanges, and you did. These were strong men who knew exactly what they wanted, and it was remarkable to watch two such fixed resolves collide. They held their purpose tight, their teeth were clenched, and their will was made of iron. They stood toe to toe.

The next morning, the newspapers reported that the supporting, though still renowned, actor had managed to share top honours in the play, having done exceptionally well even by his own standards. Only then did you realise, though you hadn't noticed at the time, that the tragedian must have lost that exchange. In a moment of weakness he had let himself be stirred, and for a moment, only a moment, he had picked up the pace.
