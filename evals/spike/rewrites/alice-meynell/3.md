The Italian woman lives very close to nature, and so does true drama.

Acting should not be judged the way some other arts are, or praised for its "noble convention." That word is fair praise for painting, which clearly depends on convention; in painting, the convention is the art. Acting is completely different. It has no separate material to represent things with, because the actor is his own material and nothing stands in between. For the actor, the style really is the man, in a more direct and obvious sense than that saying ever intended. So we can let a critic talk about the gap between art and nature in a landscape painting without calling him reactionary, but we cannot let him say the same about acting. Acting has technique, but it has no convention.

Put simply, then, to say that acting reaches nature and touches it to the quick is to say everything that needs saying. In other arts, imitation tends to be somewhat foolish and illusion somewhat vulgar. Acting, however, is imitation when it is less than good, illusion when it is at its best, and convention only at its worst, when it has stopped being an art.

In England, though, the idea that acting is conventional was bound to take hold. Here, acting is forced to undermine and destroy itself by adopting a complete, rigid, tiresome, and powerless convention: a whole system of demonstrative behaviour, with voices and mannerisms meant to be expressive and, above all, a feeble and lifeless set of stock gestures. Everyday English manners are so reserved and still that they offer no drama anyone can see or hear, and drama exists to be seen and heard. As a result, our acting (assuming we have any acting at all, which is a generous assumption) has to build its own small, separate world to supplement real life, and so creates a division between "art" and nature. In this one art, that division is fatal.
