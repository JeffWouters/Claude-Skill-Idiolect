Repetition plays the same role in ornament that symmetry plays in form. Both Greek and Gothic art are built on series, with repetition or alternation as their guiding principle. There is little need to spell out how this differs from the Japanese approach, whose guiding principles can be described as uniqueness and placement. Before Western designers studied Japanese decoration, these were not recognised as principles of ornament at all. Repetition and alternation do appear in Japanese ornament, of course, for example in the diaper patterns the Japanese invent with such remarkable skill, but even there uniqueness and placement are the main inspiration.

It is worth pointing out, and very relevant here, that the defining feature of Japanese diaper patterns is interruption. These patterns must repeat, but they avoid symmetry through interruptions that, at least to Western eyes, always feel fresh and unexpected. Where the lines break off, how those break points vary, and the refusal to let parts correspond are exactly what make this kind of Japanese design impossible to imitate. As a result, even a repeating pattern gives a strikingly successful impression of spontaneity. It feels as if the designer made a new decision at every corner. That constant self-awareness does not lead to greatness, however. Great design has a calm that is missing from the gentle abruptness and odd brevity of Japanese lines. In every other artistic tradition, a line is long or short depending on where it sits and what it is for. Only the Japanese designer arranges patterns so that the line is always short, and many repeating designs consist entirely of this varied and unpredictably placed shortness, this playful refusal to reach the goal.

The Japanese also avoid symmetry within the basic unit of a repeating pattern by another simple method: number. They make small differences in the number of curves and lines. A large difference would not produce the same sense of variety; it would read as a contrast instead. Three rods on one side and six on the other, for example, would be something other than a variation, and the feeling of variety would be lost. The Japanese decorator will set three in one place against two in another, and the viewer immediately senses that symmetry has been defeated. With more forceful means, symmetry would have been neither suggested nor overturned.
