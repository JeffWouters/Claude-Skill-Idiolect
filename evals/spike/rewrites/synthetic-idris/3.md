A house feels strangely quiet in the early hours of the morning when you're waiting for something to break. Anyone who has carried a pager, or whatever has replaced it, probably knows the feeling: a light, broken sleep in which part of your mind keeps listening for an alert that may never come, your phone sitting within reach on the nightstand, and a slight sense of guilt whenever you try to fully relax. We usually talk about on-call work in operational terms, such as rotations, escalation policies, and response times. Those descriptions are accurate, but they leave out what the work actually feels like, and that is arguably where most of its cost lies. The hardest part of being on call may not be the incident at all, but the long wait beforehand, along with the knowledge that the waiting will start again as soon as the incident is over.

So what happens when the alert finally goes off? In my experience, something odd happens. The vague anxiety of waiting turns into something sharper and, surprisingly, easier to cope with. Now there is a problem to understand, a system to investigate, and a series of hypotheses to test. A mind that had been spinning aimlessly in the dark suddenly has something to focus on. But this work has to be done in conditions that seem almost designed to undermine good judgment. You are alone and tired, using dashboards that someone else built for a different purpose, and unsure whether the anomaly on your screen is the actual cause or just a symptom. It is a real test of composure, and I doubt many of us have ever been properly trained for it. Organizations tend to assume that people who perform well during the day will perform just as well at night, and that assumption is only partly true at best.
