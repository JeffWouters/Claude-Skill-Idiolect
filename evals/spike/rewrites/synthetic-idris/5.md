Anyone who has joined a team partway through its life will recognize the moment when you open the codebase for the first time and realize that the people who wrote it are no longer around to explain it. It's a bit like moving into a house whose previous owners left in a rush. The furniture is arranged according to a logic you can't quite work out, some rooms seem to have been abandoned, and there are fixtures whose purpose stays unclear no matter how long you look at them. We often call these systems "legacy," a word with a slightly negative tone, as if what we inherited were a burden instead of a gift. The word may influence our attitude more than we think. It encourages us to see the original authors as obstacles rather than as colleagues who happen to be separated from us by time. A different vocabulary might lead to a different kind of engineering.

Why are we so quick to assume that our predecessors were careless? Part of the reason is that we see only the outcomes of their decisions, not the circumstances behind them: the deadlines they faced, the constraints they had to work around, and the knowledge they didn't have yet and couldn't have had. Code that looks ridiculous on its own often makes sense once you know its history. That odd workaround was a fix for a bug in a library that has since been patched. The duplicated logic dates from a time when two teams weren't talking to each other. The unusual structure keeps things compatible with a client nobody remembers anymore. None of this shows up in the code, which records the decision but quietly drops the reasoning behind it. As a result, we judge the people who came before us by knowledge they never had, and, unsurprisingly, we find them lacking.
