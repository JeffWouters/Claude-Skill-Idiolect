When a team meets after an incident to talk about what went wrong, what are they actually trying to learn? The standard answer focuses on causes and fixes: reconstructing the timeline, identifying the factors that contributed, and agreeing on actions to stop it from happening again. That answer is correct, but it leaves something out. A postmortem is also a social event, and often one of the most emotionally intense moments in a technical team's life. People have to relive a stressful and confusing period in front of their colleagues, and some of those colleagues may have made decisions that made things worse. As a result, the value of what the team learns may depend less on how rigorous the analysis is and more on the atmosphere in the room. When people feel safe, they describe what really happened. When they feel judged, they describe what they wish had happened. Most experienced teams know this, even if their postmortem templates don't show it.

The shift toward blameless postmortems has been a major step forward here, and I don't want to downplay it. The ideas behind it were hard to arrive at: incidents come from systems rather than individual failures, people usually act sensibly based on the information they have, and punishing mistakes only teaches people to hide them. Teams that have taken these ideas to heart are much better at learning from failure. Still, "blameless" can turn into a ritual word, said at the start of the meeting and then forgotten as the conversation goes on. Can a meeting really be free of blame just because someone announced it, when blame is still in the air? You may well have sat through a postmortem where the official language was perfectly neutral, but the looks exchanged across the table said something quite different.
