Why do we keep asking for estimates when so few of us trust them? This is a genuine question. Nearly every software organization still relies on estimation, despite a long and well-documented record of disappointing results, and when a practice keeps failing but never goes away, it is usually serving some purpose other than the one it claims. One way to see it is that an estimate isn't really a prediction. It is a negotiation that uses the language of prediction, a way to balance what the business wants against what the engineers are worried about, where both sides agree to act as though the final number describes the future. Most experienced engineers understand this on some level, even if they wouldn't phrase it that way. Problems start when everyone forgets it's a pretense: the negotiated number turns into a commitment, and that commitment becomes the yardstick people are judged by.

It helps to think about what actually happens when we estimate a piece of software work. We picture a series of tasks that haven't happened yet, in a system we only partly understand, with interruptions we can't anticipate, and then we squeeze all that uncertainty into a single number that has to fit neatly into a planning document. In a way, it's a heroic effort. It asks our imagination to do something it isn't well suited for, and it rewards confidence more than accuracy. So is it surprising that the results are unreliable? What may be more surprising is how seldom the planning process itself admits this, treating every new estimate as a fresh start rather than the latest in a long line of similar misjudgments.
