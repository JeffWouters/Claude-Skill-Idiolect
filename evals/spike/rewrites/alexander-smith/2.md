Doolan was eventually caught in Liverpool, and the three men stood trial at the Spring Assizes. The jury found them guilty but recommended mercy for Hickie, on the grounds that he might be mentally weak. The judge then passed sentence with all the usual formality. The men were condemned to die. At night, lying comfortably in bed, I would imagine my way into their bleak and hopeless minds and feel a misery that wasn't mine, because nothing stirs the imagination quite like contrast. As mentioned, Hickie had been recommended for mercy, and in the end the appropriate authorities acted on that recommendation.

Now it is the evening before the execution, and the reader should picture an early May sunset settling gently over the edge of the city. The houses facing onto an open square have small front gardens where dark red wallflowers and powdery auriculas grow. Along the side of the square where the City Road runs north stands a school for the blind, a brick building painted red with white trim in the neat English style. Next to it is a tall white cemetery wall, with the spire of the Gothic cathedral rising above it. Beyond that, across the ravine, a stone statue of John Knox stands among the crowded graves, looking down at the cathedral with a Bible gripped in his raised, threatening hand. The sunset falls over all of this, giving everything a warm pink glow. It lingers in the clumps of trees around the asylum and leaves the building half in light and half in soft shadow.

This open square is a perfect playground for us boys, and we play "Prisoner's Base" with the same seriousness that those of us still living now bring to the business of adult life. The girls play quieter games, which we looked down on with great contempt. They form two lines, arm in arm, and take turns dancing toward each other and back again, singing in clear, high voices a set of verses whose meaning and purpose time has long since erased.
