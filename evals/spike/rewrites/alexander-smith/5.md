This book contains what must be one of the most moving pieces ever written: a song by Martin Luther. The great Reformer's music can still be heard in churches today. One of the rough, powerful hymns he wrote has such a thundering stride that, according to Carlyle, the father of Frederick the Great used to call it "God Almighty's Grenadier March." The song I have in mind is quite different in mood; it is as gentle as tears. To appreciate it fully, you need to picture the man himself, sturdy, determined, humorous, and at the same time tender-hearted, and to remember what he achieved.

Like Franklin's kite, guided by the highest of hands, he rose into the dark papal storm cloud that hung over Europe. The angry lightning struck him but did not burn him; the threatening apparition collapsed amid harmless thunder, and the light of truth broke through the scattered black clouds and shone on the nations once more. This was the man who, when warned not to go to Worms, replied, "Although there be as many devils in Worms as there are tiles on the house-tops, I will go." This was the man who, cornered before that splendid assembly, declared, "It is neither safe nor prudent to do aught against conscience. Here stand I--I cannot do otherwise. God help me. Amen." A rock cannot be moved, even if lightning may split it.

Keep all of this in mind, and then read Luther's "Christmas Carol," with its tender dedication: "Luther--written for his little son Hans, 1546." Written by anyone else, the verses might not amount to much. Coming from him, they are as moving as the finest eloquence. The song settled deep in the hearts of ordinary people, and it is still sung from the dome of the Kreuzkirche in Dresden before dawn on Christmas morning.
