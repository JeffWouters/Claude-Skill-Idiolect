In literature and art, this expression of the individual self is what matters most. In fact, it is the only thing of real value. What makes an artist or writer great is not what they share with others in their field but what is distinctively their own. A great person is someone who does something for the first time. Discovering America was difficult; once it had been discovered, sailing there turned out to be easy enough. This distinctive quality in a poem or painting is the ultimate test of its worth, and a work that has it at least has what it needs to last. Beyond its other merits, such a work also has biographical value. It becomes a study of character, a window into another person's inner life.

There is a kind of cleverness in the world that seems to have no parents. It exists, but you cannot tell where it came from, just as you cannot pick up a fallen apple blossom in an orchard and work out from its colour and scent which branch it dropped from. Cleverness of this sort reveals nothing. It is like an anonymous letter: however long you study it, you cannot trace where it came from, and it ends up filed among the lost and unclaimed. By contrast, some people express themselves in a way that is characteristic every time, as if each idea came out of the same mould. In even their shortest remarks or most offhand comments, you can read something of their history, just as you can see the growth rings in the smallest slice of a tree. The first kind of cleverness dies because it has shallow roots and no life beyond itself. The second kind survives because it is connected to, and nourished by, something greater than itself.

Mrs. Glass's famous advice that to make hare soup you "must first catch your hare" applies far more widely than cookery. In art, literature, social life, and even morality, you must first catch your man. Once you have done that, everything else follows naturally. A person can learn a great deal, but for the most important thing of all there are no teachers and no schools.
