"Nae minstrels played to them but doubt, For gleemen there were halden out By day and eik by nicht: Except a minstrel that slew a man, So to his heritage he wan, And entered by brieve of richt."

The strange figures file past to the music of the one poet who has ended up in hell. The poem closes in pure farce, with the devil deciding to throw a great celebration:

"Then cried Mahoun for a Hielan Padyane, Syne ran a fiend to fetch Makfadyane, Far north-wast in a neuck; Be he the coronach had done shout, Ersche men so gatherit him about, In hell great room they took. Thae tarmigants, with tag and tatter, Full loud in Ersche begoud to clatter, And roup like raven and rook. The Devil sae deaved was with their yell, That in the deepest pot of hell He smorit them with smook."

One more of Dunbar's poems is worth quoting as a contrast to what we have seen so far. It stands out as the only poem in which he writes as a lover. Its way of thinking feels entirely modern; strip away the awkward old spelling and it could have been written today. It is crafted with considerable skill and elegance. His natural melancholy shows through, as it always does when he takes on a serious subject, and the poem has more tenderness and feeling than is usual for him.
