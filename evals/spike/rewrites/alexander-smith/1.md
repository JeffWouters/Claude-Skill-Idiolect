People who crave excitement might assume that my life here is dull and uneventful, but they would be wrong. It's true that the only concerts I hear are the ones thrushes give on spring mornings, and the only paintings I see are the sunrises and sunsets spread across the open sky. I don't go to parties or dances. The most indulgent thing I do is sit at the tea table, and the most scandalous news I hear is ordinary village gossip. Even so, I enjoy these concerts more than I would the grand ones in London. I like the paintings I see, and I think they are better done than anything hanging in the Royal Academy. And the village gossip suits me far better than the scandals that shake the London clubs.

What strikes me is how completely the wider world is mirrored in simple village life. The people around me are absorbed in their own concerns, and they could hardly feel more strongly about them if those concerns were matters of empire. Farmer Worthy worries about the next market, and the prospect of butter and egg prices falling keeps him awake at night. The village doctor (fortunately there is only one) rushes about in his carriage as if no one could be born or die without him. He spends his days at the edges of life, greeting new arrivals and saying goodbye to those who are leaving. Then there is the loud, hearty man who presides over the Jolly Swillers when they gather at the Blue Lion on Wednesday evenings. He is a great politician with powerful lungs, and he governs the village from the taproom much as Lord Palmerston governs the country from Parliament. His audience considers him wiser than the Prime Minister, and he tends to agree with them.

Everything other people find in the wider world, I find here. London is simply Dreamthorp on a larger scale.
