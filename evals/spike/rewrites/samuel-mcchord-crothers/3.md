For the man sitting by the roadside, the present moment was not complete in itself. Unlike the practical businessman, he did not see it as a final and satisfying reality. He was observant enough, and he noticed the people walking past. Yet each of them seemed to him, in that moment, like someone running away from the past and into the future. It was a pointless escape and an empty freedom, because the past would be waiting for them in the future. Whenever he watched someone act, it felt like watching the moving shadow of some old deed that had now become a person's fate.

Did I suggest that Hawthorne was hardly affected by his surroundings? It would be more accurate to say that he responded to a part of his surroundings that most people strangely overlook. He sensed what another Salem mystic described:

Around us ever lies the enchanted land In marvels rich to thine own sons displayed.

Despite objections from purists, the typical New Englander has always said "I guess" when he means "I think." To his shrewd and good-natured curiosity, all thinking comes down to a kind of guesswork, and everyone is equally entitled to his own guess.

The conversation at the village store may seem far removed from Emerson and Hawthorne, but for these New England writers, too, thinking was still a form of guessing. The observer looks at the outward appearance of things, which seems so final, and says, "I guess there's something behind all this. I guess it's worth looking into."
