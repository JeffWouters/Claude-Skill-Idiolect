As I listened to your reasoning just now, I kept thinking of those lines. Scholasticus, there is one point you seem to have missed. You speak about the laws of the mind as if they were fixed. When you were at college, setting out those laws looked straightforward. Nobody studied child psychology then, or noticed how quickly childhood gives way to adolescence, when everything changes. Nobody discussed the subconscious, where it becomes hard to say what counts as consciousness and what does not. In your day, the mind came in a single standard size."

"That's true," Scholasticus said. "At the Academy we studied Watts on the Mind. Watts handled the subject directly. He didn't bother with nervous reactions; he simply gave us the mind as it is. At college we moved on to Locke on the Understanding. When the time came to deal with conscience, we read Paley's 'Moral Science.' Together with his 'Evidences,' that gave us a solid preparation for life."

"It did," I replied, "and your education has served you well. Since then, however, psychologists have made several discoveries that require us to rethink the old approaches."

I noticed that he was actually listening to me for the first time. It occurred to me that I might be able to draw him away from the bitter and pointless criticism of current events that tempts so many men of his generation, and toward the more productive kind of criticism that comes from building something new.
