He criticizes parents who give their children mechanical toys, particularly well-made ones. In his view, even a doll shouldn't be too realistic. "It would be better to give a little girl a bundle of rags and tell her to imagine it is a baby. If she were left alone with nothing but her own imagination to rely on, she would learn to use all her latent powers of creativity and originality."

The Honest Miner has carried this kind of education into adulthood. He is full of imaginative notions. Even the plainest shack looks grand to him if it has a sign reading "Palace Hotel" or "Delmonico's." When he can't have the real thing, he is content with the name. More than anything, he wants variety.

The people of Gold Hill loved to tell stories about Sandy Bowers. After he found an astonishingly rich deposit in the mountain, Sandy built himself an enormous, costly mansion in Washoe Valley. He brought in trees from all over the world, and not one of them survived. He filled the house with pianos, and when someone mentioned that he would need sheet music, he sent a telegram to New York: "Send me some sheet music, one of every kind."

That same wish for one of every kind led our community, when it stopped being a "camp" and became a "city," to give brief prominence to an elderly Pennsylvania farmer. He had wandered into Nevada without changing any of his habits. Back in York County nobody would have noticed him, because there were so many others just like him. In the silver country, however, he stood out from the usual fortune-seekers, and people made a fuss over him. Some local Diogenes held up his lantern, looked him over, and declared him an honest man, honest in the slow and steady way of the Pennsylvania Dutch. "Honest John" became a well-known figure. Then someone remarked that we had "a grand old man in our midst." That was all it took to launch his political career. He was elected to an influential post in the new city government, because everyone was curious to see what our "grand old man" would do.
